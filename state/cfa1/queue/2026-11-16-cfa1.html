1 · Refresher: elasticity, and where a firm stops producing

Elasticity compares percentages, not units. It measures how strongly buyers respond: the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in what caused it, so it has no units. On a straight-line demand curve the slope is constant but elasticity is not: buyers are very responsive near the top, where price is high, and much less near the bottom (pp.7–8).

Spending follows elasticity. If demand is elastic, a price cut raises total spending; if inelastic, it lowers it (Summary, p.28). Signs classify goods: income elasticity is positive for normal goods and negative for inferior ones; cross-price elasticity is positive for substitutes and negative for complements (pp.11–13).

A firm stops where the last unit stops paying. Profit peaks where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and a price taker’s marginal revenue is the market price. Marginal cost eventually climbs because each added worker adds less output than the last, so an extra unit costs the wage divided by a shrinking marginal product (pp.17–24).

Memorize
  • E = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP = (ΔQ/ΔP)(P/Q)
  • |E| > 1: a price cut raises spending · |E| < 1: lowers it · |E| = 1: no change
  • Profit peaks at MR = MC; for a price taker MR = P
  • MC crosses average variable and average total cost at their minimums

2 · Today: how an economy keeps score

Module 3 asks how big an economy’s output is, why it swings around the level the economy can sustain, and what makes that level grow.

One flow, counted three ways. Whatever is produced is bought, and the money paid becomes someone’s wages, rent, interest or profit, so output, spending and income are one total (p.69). GDP is the market value of the final goods and services produced in a country in a period. “Final” prevents double counting: a loaf’s price already includes the flour. Summing each producer’s value added, its sales minus its purchases from other firms, gives the same total (pp.71–73). Transfers such as pensions, and resales of used goods, produce nothing new, so they are left out.

Nominal and real. Nominal GDP can rise on prices alone; real GDP values output at a base year’s prices. The GDP deflator is nominal over real, times 100, and its rise is inflation across all domestic output, not a consumer basket (pp.74–77).

Saving has to go somewhere. Income is spent, saved or taxed, and spending is consumption, investment, government purchases and net exports. Cancel consumption and private saving has three uses: investment, a government deficit, or lending abroad (p.80). This is accounting after the fact; it does not say which piece adjusts.

Demand and supply for the whole economy. Aggregate demand slopes down: at a lower price level money buys more, interest rates fall and home goods get cheaper abroad (pp.83–85). Short-run supply slopes up because wages adjust slowly, so higher prices widen margins for a while. Long-run supply is vertical at potential GDP, set by labour, capital and technology rather than the price level (pp.87–88). A demand shift moves output and prices the same way, opening a recessionary gap below potential or an inflationary gap above it. A supply shock such as dearer oil moves them opposite ways: stagflation (pp.102–109).

Everyday picture

A bakery’s ovens set its normal weekly output, its potential. A rush of orders allows overtime for a while, until overtime pay catches up. A flour shortage brings fewer loaves and higher prices at once, which more customers cannot fix.

Growth: only productivity lasts. Each extra machine per worker adds less than the last, so lasting growth per person comes from total factor productivity, the growth that labour and capital do not explain (pp.112–113). Sustainable growth is estimated as trend growth in hours worked plus trend growth in output per hour (p.121).

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

C, I, G
consumption; investment in new capital and inventories; government purchases, not transfers. Currency per year (p.77)
X − M
net exports
Deflator
nominal ÷ real GDP × 100; the base year is 100 (p.75)
WL, WC
labour’s and capital’s income shares, about 0.7 and 0.3, weighting input growth (p.113)
Assumptions and exceptions
  • A change in the price level moves along AD or SRAS; anything else shifts the curve.
  • Higher wages shift short-run supply left but leave long-run supply alone; only capacity moves it.
  • A stronger currency shifts AD left (dearer exports) but SRAS right (cheaper imported inputs).
Memorize
  • Output = income = expenditure
  • Real GDP = nominal ÷ (deflator ÷ 100)
  • S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)
  • %ΔY ≈ %ΔA + WL%ΔL + WC%ΔK
  • Demand shift: output and prices move together · supply shift: opposite

Once only: set up your BA II Plus

The calculator keeps its settings and stored values through power-off, so one wrong setting spoils every later answer. A reset leaves it on 2 decimals and chain mode, which works strictly left to right. Fix both, check the payment settings, then leave them alone. Pages are from TI’s BA II PLUS guidebook.

Setup, in order.

  1. Reset (pp.5–6, 20)

    BA II Plus 2ND RESET ENTER

    RST ? then RST 0.00. Erases all 10 memories, unfinished calculations and all worksheet data, and restores the defaults: 2 decimals, chain and END, with P/Y = C/Y = 1 in TI’s current guidebook (some older units come back at 12; step 4 catches that). To back out before ENTER, press 2ND QUIT.

  2. Four decimals (p.4)

    BA II Plus 2ND FORMAT 4 ENTER

    DEC = 4.0000. Display only: the calculator always keeps 13 digits.

  3. AOS instead of chain, still in FORMAT (pp.4–5)

    BA II Plus ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ 2ND SET 2ND QUIT

    The fourth arrow shows Chn; SET makes it AOS (× and ÷ before + and −).

  4. P/Y = C/Y = 1 (pp.20–21)

    BA II Plus 2ND P/Y (if not 1: 1 ENTER) ↓ 2ND QUIT

    P/Y = 1.0000, then C/Y = 1.0000 (C/Y copies P/Y). Now I/Y is the rate per period and N the number of periods.

  5. END (pp.20–21)

    BA II Plus 2ND BGN (if it shows BGN: 2ND SET) 2ND QUIT

    END. A small BGN mark on the screen means BGN is on.

CLR TVM
Press 2ND CLR TVM before every TVM problem. It zeroes N, I/Y, PV, PMT and FV, which otherwise survive power-off (pp.14, 20), and leaves P/Y and END/BGN alone.
CLR WORK
Clears the worksheet you are in: CF 2ND CLR WORK for cash flows (p.37); 2ND MEM 2ND CLR WORK for all 10 memories (p.11).
CE/C
Clears only the entry being typed, or an error. TVM values stay.
RESET
Clears everything, settings included; redo steps 2–5 afterwards.

Signs are cash flows. Money paid out is negative and money received is positive: type the number, then +|− (p.21). Solving for I/Y with PV, PMT and FV all one sign gives Error 5 (p.79), because no rate balances money that only flows one way. END/BGN does not affect the cash-flow worksheet (p.44).

Two checks. Now, and whenever the calculator has been reset.

  1. Order of operations

    BA II Plus 3 + 2 × 4 =

    11.0000. 20.0000 means chain mode is still on.

  2. $1,000 at 8% a year for 5 years

    BA II Plus 2ND CLR TVM 5 N 8 I/Y 1000 +|− PV 0 PMT CPT FV

    FV = 1,469.3281 (1,000 × 1.085). 1,033.7808 means P/Y is 12; −1,469.3281 means PV was left positive.

Why END matters: three end-of-year payments of 100 at 10% are worth 248.6852 today in END and 273.5537 in BGN, 10% more, because each payment arrives a period sooner.

Keep or change? Keep DEC 4, AOS, P/Y = C/Y = 1 and END. Switch to BGN only for payments at the start of each period (rent, leases, premiums), then switch back. For monthly problems, leave P/Y alone and convert yourself: N = years × 12, I/Y = annual rate ÷ 12.
