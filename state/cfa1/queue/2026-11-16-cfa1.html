1 · Recall first, closed book

Module 1 is logged as done. Module 2 was set for last night but isn’t logged; skip 3 and 4 if you didn’t get to it.

  1. Coffee’s price rises 10% and the quantity of tea demanded rises 4%. What is the cross-price elasticity, and what does its sign say?
  2. Price elasticity of demand is −0.5 and the price rises. Does total spending rise or fall?
  3. A price taker sells at 16 with MC = 4 + 2Q. What is its output, and by what rule?
  4. Why does economic profit vanish in the long run under perfect competition, but can last for a monopolist?
Answers open after you have tried
  1. +0.4 (4% ÷ 10%). Positive means substitutes. Printed pp.11–13.
  2. Rises. Inelastic (|−0.5| < 1): quantity falls proportionally less than price rises. Summary, p.28.
  3. Q = 6. Profit peaks where MR = MC, and a price taker’s MR = P: 16 = 4 + 2Q. Pp.48–49.
  4. Entry pushes price down to P = MC = minimum average cost (p.51). Barriers to entry protect a monopolist’s profit (p.60).

2 · Set up your BA II Plus (once)

The calculator keeps its settings and stored values through power-off, so one bad setting spoils every later answer. A reset leaves you on 2 decimals and chain mode, which works strictly left to right. Fix both, confirm the payment settings, then leave them alone.

Setup, in order. Pages are from TI’s BA II PLUS guidebook.

  1. Reset (pp.5–6, 20)

    BA II Plus 2ND RESET ENTER

    RST ? then RST 0.00. Erases all 10 memories, unfinished calculations and all worksheet data, and restores the defaults: 2 decimals, chain and END, with P/Y = C/Y = 1 in TI’s current guidebook (some older units come back at 12; step 4 catches that). To back out before ENTER, press 2ND QUIT.

  2. Four decimals (p.4)

    BA II Plus 2ND FORMAT 4 ENTER

    DEC = 4.0000. Display only: the calculator always keeps 13 digits.

  3. AOS instead of chain, still in FORMAT (pp.4–5)

    BA II Plus ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ 2ND SET 2ND QUIT

    The fourth arrow shows Chn; SET makes it AOS (× and ÷ before + and −).

  4. P/Y = C/Y = 1 (pp.20–21)

    BA II Plus 2ND P/Y (if not 1: 1 ENTER) ↓ 2ND QUIT

    P/Y = 1.0000, then C/Y = 1.0000 (C/Y copies P/Y). Now I/Y is the rate per period and N the number of periods.

  5. END (pp.20–21)

    BA II Plus 2ND BGN (if it shows BGN: 2ND SET) 2ND QUIT

    END. A small BGN mark on the screen means BGN is on.

CLR TVM
Press 2ND CLR TVM before every TVM problem. It zeroes N, I/Y, PV, PMT and FV, which otherwise survive power-off (pp.14, 20), and leaves P/Y and END/BGN alone.
CLR WORK
Clears the worksheet you are in: CF 2ND CLR WORK for cash flows (p.37); 2ND MEM 2ND CLR WORK for all 10 memories (p.11).
CE/C
Clears only the entry being typed, or an error. TVM values stay.
RESET
Clears everything, settings included; redo steps 2–5 afterwards.

Signs are cash flows. Money paid out is negative and money received is positive: type the number, then +|− (p.21). Solving for I/Y with PV, PMT and FV all one sign gives Error 5 (p.79), because no rate balances money that only flows one way. END/BGN does not affect the cash-flow worksheet (p.44).

Two checks. Now, and before each study session.

  1. Order of operations

    BA II Plus 3 + 2 × 4 =

    11.0000. 20.0000 means chain mode is still on.

  2. $1,000 at 8% a year for 5 years

    BA II Plus 2ND CLR TVM 5 N 8 I/Y 1000 +|− PV 0 PMT CPT FV

    FV = 1,469.3281 (1,000 × 1.085). 1,033.7808 means P/Y is 12; −1,469.3281 means PV was left positive.

Optional: three end-of-year payments of 100 at 10% are worth 248.6852 today in END and 273.5537 in BGN, 10% more because each payment arrives a period sooner. Switch back to END afterwards.

Keep or change? Keep DEC 4, AOS, P/Y = C/Y = 1 and END. Switch to BGN only for payments at the start of each period (rent, leases, premiums), then switch back. For monthly problems, leave P/Y alone and convert yourself: N = years × 12, I/Y = annual rate ÷ 12.

3 · Tonight’s principles: how an economy keeps score

Module 3 asks how big output is, why it swings around its potential, and what makes potential grow.

One flow, counted three ways. Every dollar spent on final output is someone’s income, so output = income = expenditure. GDP counts final goods and services produced in the period, at market value. Count either the value added at each stage or the final price, never both. Transfers and capital gains produce nothing, so they are left out. Real GDP removes price changes: nominal 1,100 with a deflator of 105 is real 1,100 ÷ 1.05 = 1,047.6.

Follow the saving. Income is consumed, saved or taxed (C + S + T), and spending is C + I + G + (X − M). Cancel C and private saving has exactly three uses: investment, funding a government deficit, or lending abroad (p.80).

Worked example (hypothetical, $ billions)
  1. S = 500, I = 420, G = 700, T = 600, so the government deficit G − T = 100.
  2. X − M = S − I − (G − T) = 500 − 420 − 100 = −20: a trade deficit, financed by borrowing 20 from abroad.
  3. Widen the deficit to 150 with S and I unchanged: X − M = −70. What domestic savers can’t fund, foreigners do.

AD–AS: who moves output and prices. AD slopes down because a lower price level raises real wealth, lowers interest rates and cheapens exports (pp.83–85). SRAS slopes up because wages are sticky, and LRAS is vertical at potential GDP because in the long run all prices adjust (pp.87–88). An AD shift moves output and prices the same way, opening a gap. SRAS shifting left, as in an oil shock, moves them opposite ways: stagflation. LRAS moves only when capacity changes: labour, capital, technology, natural resources (Exhibit 16, pp.99–100).

Growth: only productivity lasts. In Y = A·F(L, K), each extra machine per worker adds less than the last (diminishing marginal productivity), so capital deepening stalls. Lasting growth per person needs total factor productivity (A), measured as what is left after labour and capital (pp.112–113). With TFP growth of 1%, labour 1% and capital 3%, growth ≈ 1 + 0.7 × 1 + 0.3 × 3 = 2.6%.

S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)

GDP
C + I + G + (X − M), in currency per year: a flow (p.77)
Deflator
nominal ÷ real GDP × 100, an index with the base year at 100 (p.75)
%ΔULC
≈ %Δwages − %Δproductivity; rising unit labour cost shifts SRAS left (p.96)
%ΔY
≈ %ΔA + WL%ΔL + WC%ΔK, with shares WL ≈ 0.7 and WC ≈ 0.3 (p.113)
Potential growth
≈ growth in hours worked + growth in labour productivity (p.121)

The saving equation and GDP are identities: true by definition, and silent about cause.

MPC / MPS
share of an extra dollar consumed / saved; MPS = 1 − MPC (p.80)
Potential GDP
output with labour and capital fully employed; estimated, never observed (pp.111–112)
Recessionary / inflationary gap
short-run output below / above potential (pp.103, 106)
Stagflation
high inflation with weak growth (p.108)
Labour productivity
real GDP per hour worked (p.119)
National income
GDP minus depreciation (the capital consumption allowance) and a statistical discrepancy (p.124)
Memorize
  • GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
  • Real GDP = nominal ÷ (deflator ÷ 100)
  • S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)
  • MPS = 1 − MPC
  • %ΔY ≈ %ΔA + 0.7 %ΔL + 0.3 %ΔK
  • AD shift: Y and P same way · SRAS shift: opposite
Exam traps
  • Transfers are not in G. GDP “investment” means new capital goods and inventories, never buying securities.
  • A change in the price level moves you along AD or SRAS; anything else shifts the curve.
  • A currency depreciation shifts AD right (cheaper exports) and SRAS left (dearer imported inputs): both raise prices (Exhibits 14 and 16).
  • Stagflation comes from supply. A demand shock can’t raise prices while cutting output.
