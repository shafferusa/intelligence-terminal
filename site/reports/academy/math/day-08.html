In the 1730s a Calculation Said the Bees Were Off by Two Minutes of Arc. The Calculation Was Wrong — and a Miss That Small Would Have Cost Almost No Wax Anyway.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 16 min read

At the best point of a smooth trade-off the curve is flat, so a small miss costs almost nothing — which is why two minutes of arc could not matter to the bees, and why a speed limit saves fuel mainly from the drivers farthest from the best speed.

Mathematics

Day 8 of 293 · Mathematics 8 of 24 · Optimization: why the best point is flat

Two minutes of arc between the bees and a mathematician

In 1712 Giacomo Filippo Maraldi, an astronomer at the Paris Observatory, reported on honeybees to the Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris. A honeybee cell is a six-sided tube, and its closed end is not a flat floor but a small pointed cap made of three rhombus-shaped faces (a rhombus is a four-sided diamond with equal sides) meeting at a tip. Maraldi gave the diamonds’ angles as about 109°28′ and 70°32′. A minute of arc, written ′, is a sixtieth of a degree, and a second of arc, written ″, is a sixtieth of a minute. Whether he measured those angles to the minute or reasoned partly from the cap’s symmetry is unclear.

A generation later the naturalist René-Antoine Ferchault de Réaumur posed a question to a young mathematician, Samuel König: of all caps of this form that hold the same honey, which angles use the least wax? König attacked it with the calculus and, around 1739, answered 109°26′ and 70°34′. By his arithmetic the bees were wrong by two minutes of arc, one thirtieth of a degree.

In 1743 the Scottish mathematician Colin Maclaurin published the correction: the bees’ angles were the true optimum, and König’s calculation had erred. Later tradition blames a faulty table of logarithms; that is not established. Then, in 1964, the Hungarian mathematician László Fejes Tóth found a different cap, built from two hexagons and two rhombi, that with the walls idealized as paper-thin uses less wax than the bees’. By his own estimate it saves less than 0.35 percent of the area of the cell’s opening.

So the bees had the angle right, and a mathematician still found a cheaper cell on paper. Two questions follow, and you cannot yet answer either. If the bees had really been two minutes off, how much wax would they have wasted? And why would the answer depend so little on getting the angle exactly right?

The Big Idea

At the best point of a smooth trade-off the curve is flat, so a small miss costs almost nothing, because the loss grows only as the square of the miss.

Take any quantity you want as small as possible (wax, fuel, cost) as it depends on one choice you control (an angle, a speed, a radius). Often the cost falls as you move the choice one way and then rises, because two effects pull in opposite directions. At the very bottom of that valley the curve is level, tilting neither up nor down. Step away from the bottom by a small distance and the cost does not rise in proportion to your step. It rises in proportion to the square of your step: miss by half as much and you pay a quarter; miss by a tenth and you pay a hundredth.

Two consequences organize this lesson. Near a smooth optimum, precision is cheap, so a design or a policy does not have to hit the exact best point to capture nearly all the benefit. And the best point is the best only for a stated objective under stated constraints; change either and the best point moves, sometimes to an edge where the curve is not flat at all.

How It Works

Every optimization problem has the same three parts: a choice you control (call it x), an objective that depends on it (f(x), to be made small or large), and the constraints on which choices are allowed. The bee’s cell is a clean example. The cell must hold a fixed volume of honey, so volume is a constraint, and the wax in the walls and cap, their surface area, is the objective to minimize. This is Day 3’s surface-to-volume ratio again, but with the size held fixed: the volume is fixed, so minimizing the surface means choosing the shape, and here the cap’s angle is the unknown. Deepen the tip and, for the same volume of honey, the side walls can be shorter; but the three faces themselves grow larger. One effect saves wax, the other spends it, and the total has a lowest point somewhere between.

Finding that lowest point rests on one observation, known as Fermat’s theorem on stationary points. Suppose f is smooth (Day 4: it has a derivative everywhere) and x is a minimum that is not at an edge of the allowed range. If the slope there were positive, stepping slightly left would lower f, so x could not have been the minimum; if the slope were negative, stepping right would. Only a slope of exactly zero survives: f′(x) = 0. That is the first-order condition. Solving it yields the stationary points, the candidates. For a maximum the same argument runs with the inequalities flipped.

A zero slope is necessary but not sufficient: it also occurs at a maximum and at a flat inflection like x3 at x = 0. Day 4’s second derivative sorts them out, in the second-derivative test. If f″ > 0 at the stationary point, the curve bends upward like a bowl and the point is a minimum. If f″ < 0 it bends downward and the point is a maximum. If f″ = 0 the test is silent. You have already used this without the name: Day 7 found the logistic curve’s fastest growth by setting the derivative of the growth rate with respect to N to zero, which gave N = K/2, and that rate’s second derivative, −2r/K, is negative, so it was a maximum.

Why a zero slope makes errors cheap follows from the second-order Taylor approximation, which describes a smooth curve near a point by its slope and its curvature: f(x* + δ) ≈ f(x*) + f′(x*)δ + ½f″(x*)δ². Here x* is the optimum and δ is how far you miss. At the optimum f′(x*) = 0, so the term that would grow in direct proportion to the miss vanishes, leaving ½f″(x*)δ². The price of missing is the curvature times the square of the miss. Curvature sets how forgiving the optimum is: a gently curved bowl forgives large misses, a sharply curved one punishes small ones. Squaring is why small misses are cheap: a miss of 0.01 enters as 0.0001. The approximation holds only for small misses, and real curves are usually lopsided, costing more to miss on one side than the other.

Constraints and objectives can move the best point itself, in two ways. First, change the objective and the optimum moves. A driver’s cost per mile is not only fuel: time has a price too, and the time per mile falls as speed rises. Adding that falling term pulls the best speed upward, to the point where the extra fuel cost of one more mile per hour equals the value of the time it saves. That is marginal cost equal to marginal benefit, the first-order condition in an economist’s words. Second, a binding constraint can push the best allowed point onto an edge. If the law caps speed below the driver’s preferred speed, the best legal speed is the cap itself, and there the slope is not zero: the driver’s cost is still falling toward the speed they want, and its steepness says what a small relaxation of the limit would be worth. The flatness argument covers only interior optima. Fejes Tóth’s cap shows a third kind of movement: he did not adjust the angle but changed which shapes were allowed, and the floor itself dropped.

One caution about the word “optimal,” because it is easy to hear it as a verdict. The mathematics optimizes whatever function it is handed. “The bees minimize wax” is a model with a single objective, and real combs serve other purposes too: bearing weight, being built quickly, holding brood at a steady temperature. How bees arrive at hexagonal cells is itself unsettled: one 2013 study (Karihaloo and colleagues) argues that the hexagons form partly as warm wax flows into shape, while another (Bauer and Bienefeld) argues that the bees shape them actively. Debated So “bees optimize” is shorthand for “the cell is the wax-minimizing form,” a fact about the geometry and not a claim about what the bees intend.

Back to the story

The bees’ angle is the zero-slope point of the wax curve, so the curve is flat there. A two-minute miss in the faces’ angle works out to a three-minute miss in the tilt that the formula below uses, and the extra wax is under one part in a million of the cell’s total. König’s discrepancy mattered as mathematics, not as beekeeping. The exact optimum is 109°28′16″, so Maraldi’s figure was within sixteen seconds of arc and König’s was off by about two minutes sixteen. Fejes Tóth’s improvement is a different shape, not a better angle, and even it saves less than 0.35 percent of the opening’s area — as much as thousands of times the wax at stake in König’s two minutes, and still small. Near a smooth optimum, little is left to gain.

Key Concepts

Optimum (maximum or minimum)
The best value of an objective over the allowed choices: a minimum when you want it small (wax, fuel), a maximum when you want it large (profit, growth rate).
First-order condition
f′(x*) = 0. At a smooth optimum away from the edges the slope is zero. It is necessary, not sufficient.
Second-derivative test
At a stationary point, f″ > 0 means a minimum, f″ < 0 a maximum, and f″ = 0 settles nothing.
Quadratic loss (flatness)
Near an optimum the penalty for missing by δ is about ½f″δ², growing with the square of the miss; the curvature f″ says how forgiving the optimum is.
Constrained optimization
Finding the best choice among those allowed. When a constraint binds, the best allowed point sits on the boundary, where the slope is not zero; that slope measures what relaxing the constraint would be worth.

Example: The 55-mph limit and the shape of a fuel curve

The 1974 national speed limit sits at the regulation link of Logan’s car chain. On January 2, 1974, amid the oil crisis set off by the Arab oil embargo, President Nixon signed the Emergency Highway Energy Conservation Act, which required states to set a 55-mph maximum or lose federal highway funds. A car’s fuel use per mile is a smooth curve in speed, and its shape says where such a limit can and cannot save fuel. Department of Energy and Oak Ridge National Laboratory tests of 74 cars and light trucks (model years 2003–2012, so they give the shape, not 1974’s numbers) found fuel economy falling sharply above about 50 mph, by 36 percent on average from 50 to 80 mph; DOE’s summary puts the peak for most vehicles near 40 mph.

1. Model. Fuel per mile is F(v) = A/v + Bv²: engine burn per hour, spread over more miles as speed rises, plus air drag, whose energy per mile grows as v². Setting F′ = −A/v² + 2Bv = 0 gives v* = (A/2B)1/3; F″ = 2A/v³ + 2B > 0, so it is a minimum.

2. Calibrate. Put v* at 40 mph. With x = v/40, fuel per mile relative to the best is (2/x + x²)/3, which equals 1 at x = 1.

3. Test. The model’s fuel economy falls 11.7%, 14.8% and 15.9% going 50→60, 60→70 and 70→80 mph; ORNL’s averages are 12.4%, 14.0% and 15.4%.

4. Flat, then steep. Ten percent over the best speed (44 mph) costs 0.9% more fuel per mile; 55 mph costs 11.5% more, 70 mph 40% more, 80 mph 67% more.

5. The limit. A car cut from 70 to 55 mph saves 1 − 1.115/1.402 = 20.5% of its fuel per mile; one cut from 58 to 55 saves 1 − 1.115/1.161 = 3.9%.

The model is a caricature (it ignores tires, gearing and engine maps), but it shows where a cap’s savings come from: cars far out on the steep side, not cars near the bottom. The Transportation Research Board’s 1984 review, 55: A Decade of Experience, put the 1983 saving at about 167,000 barrels of petroleum a day, under 2 percent of highway fuel, while recognizing that such estimates are hard to make precisely. Debated The curve says where savings can come from; how many miles were driven where, and how well the limit was obeyed, decide the total.

Why It Matters

Once you see optima this way, you can ask three questions of any claim that something is “best.” What is being optimized, and who chose it? How curved is the optimum, which tells you what precision is worth? And is the optimum interior, where flatness applies, or pinned against a constraint, where the slope is not zero and loosening or tightening the constraint matters? In the honeycomb a miss of a few minutes of arc costs almost nothing; the fuel curve has cheap misses near its bottom and expensive ones far out on its steep side.

The mathematics cannot choose the objective, though. “Progress” in the sense of your book always means progress toward something, and that something enters the optimization as the choice of f. That is where values come in. Day 23 (Goodhart’s law) shows what happens when the quantity being optimized is only a proxy for what we care about, and Day 173 (gradient descent) searches for a low point of a loss function in a model with a great many settings, using the gradient, the vector of all the slopes.

Settled. The mathematics: a smooth interior optimum has zero slope and a quadratic penalty for missing. The bees’ cap angle is the wax minimum for a three-rhombus cap, and tested fuel use per mile climbs steeply above about 50 mph.

Debated. How bees come to build the form (two 2013 studies disagree), and what the 55-mph limit saved: the Transportation Research Board estimated roughly 2,000 to 4,000 lives a year while cautioning that an exact figure is not possible.

Value judgment. Whether those savings were worth the extra hours on the road weighs fuel, risk and time against one another, and the arithmetic cannot set those weights.

Day 3, The square-cube law: wax is surface and honey is volume, so the bee’s problem is Day 3’s surface-to-volume ratio with the size fixed and the shape left free, the cap’s angle trading wall area against face area.

Across. Biology, Medicine & Health: Cecil Murray’s 1926 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences treats a blood vessel’s radius as an optimum. By Poiseuille’s law, the power needed to push a given flow through a vessel of fixed length is proportional to 1/r⁴, where r is its radius, while the blood it holds, which the body must keep supplying, is proportional to r². Setting the derivative of the sum to zero gives flow proportional to radius cubed, so at a branch the parent’s radius cubed equals the sum of the daughters’. It is the same mathematics as the cell and the fuel curve, with a more sharply curved bottom: in this two-term model a radius 10 percent off costs about 3 to 5 percent more in total. Measured vessels follow the law roughly rather than exactly (T. F. Sherman, Journal of General Physiology, 1981).

  • Near the optimum a miss of δ costs about ½f″δ², so small misses are cheap and the curvature sets how cheap.
