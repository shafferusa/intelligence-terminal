Bertha Benz Bought Fuel at a Pharmacy and Drove About 106 Kilometers in 1888. Why Did the Car Win, and What Did Winning Cost?

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

The mathematics of this block explains a good deal of why the car won, but the chain those numbers sit in is a loop rather than a line: a rule came before the car, the invention arguably came before the need, and the consequences rewrote the rules.

Systems Case

Day 24 of 293 · Systems Case 24 of 24 · Systems Case 1: Car I, the car by the numbers (spiral visit)

Bertha Benz drives to her mother’s, August 1888

Early on 5 August 1888 — the date tradition gives — Bertha Benz, 39, left the family’s home in Mannheim with her teenage sons Eugen, 15, and Richard, 13. She had not told her husband. The machine was a three-wheeled Patent-Motorwagen, the design Karl Benz had patented two years earlier (German patent 37435, filed on 29 January 1886 and granted that November). Her destination was Pforzheim and her mother’s house, about 106 kilometers away.

Nothing along the way was set up for her. Fuel meant ligroin, a light petroleum fraction sold as a cleaning solvent, and she bought it at the town pharmacy in Wiesloch, the Stadt-Apotheke (the town now promotes it as the world’s first filling station, a label applied afterward). When the wooden brake blocks wore thin, a craftsman — a cobbler in most accounts — nailed leather onto them. Family tradition adds that she cleared a blocked fuel line with a hatpin and insulated a bare ignition wire with a garter. By one account she and her sons pushed the car up the steeper hills. She reached Pforzheim around dusk, sent Karl a telegram, and drove home several days later.

Forty-one years later, in 1929, the United States alone had about 23 million registered cars.

So here is the puzzle. How did a machine that needed a pharmacy for fuel and a craftsman for its brakes end up on 23 million American registrations within forty-one years? And how much of that can the mathematics you have just learned explain?

The Big Idea

Why did the car win, and what did winning cost? This lesson answers with the mathematics of this block. The car’s rise can be read in four kinds of numbers — an advantage in energy per kilogram, a price falling by a steady percentage each year, adoption tracing an S-shaped curve, and harms judged as rates rather than totals — but the chain they sit in is a loop, not a line.

That is the job of a Systems Case: take one real system and follow it through the chain you described when you set this path up — need, invention, physical constraint, engineering, financing and production, regulation, adoption, consequences, feedback. This is the first of four visits to the car. Only mathematics has been taught so far, so five links can be walked with numbers already in hand; the other four are mapped in a sentence or two, with the day that will teach them.

One assumption in the chain deserves a challenge. As written it reads like a pipeline, each link feeding the next, and the car did not travel it in order. Britain had capped steam road vehicles at walking pace about twenty years before Karl Benz filed his patent, so a rule came before the invention. The invention arguably came before any widespread need. And the car’s consequences, deaths among them, later rewrote its rules and its design.

The Chain

Each paragraph below is one link, in your order, with its field in bold. A link marked “preview” is only mapped here; the day that teaches it is named.

Need (Psychology, Sociology) — preview. Nothing in 1888 demanded the car: horses, trams and railways already moved people and goods, and accounts of Bertha’s drive commonly say she meant to show that Karl’s machine was practical, which suggests the want still had to be built. On that reading the invention came before the need, though that is an interpretation, not a measurement. Psychology (Day 159, Motivation and human needs) teaches what human needs are, and the third car visit (Day 216) takes up needs and status in the car’s social story.

Invention (History, Engineering) — preview. Karl Benz’s patent covered a vehicle driven by a gas engine; Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach were building engine-driven vehicles near Stuttgart in the same years. Day 224 (History II, The invention of invention) traces how invention itself became organized work, and Day 91 (Engineering, The internal combustion engine) explains how the engine works.

Physical constraint (Mathematics, Day 1) — deep. Energy density is how much energy a fuel or battery stores per kilogram. Gasoline holds about 12 kilowatt-hours (kWh, the unit on an electric bill) per kilogram (the EPA counts 33.7 kWh per gallon, and a gallon weighs about 2.8 kilograms); a mid-2020s lithium-ion battery pack holds roughly 0.15 to 0.20 kWh per kilogram. Day 1’s Fermi estimate put the raw gap at 60 to 80 times by weight and, once an electric drivetrain’s better efficiency is counted, about twenty times in useful energy. That ratio is a large part of why a gasoline car could carry a long range in a small tank.

Engineering (Engineering) — preview. Bertha’s repairs on the road — leather on the brake blocks and, by tradition, a hatpin and a garter — were field engineering, and she is credited with suggesting a lower gear for hills. The Engineering block (Days 87–103) teaches design trade-offs, the engine and making the machine safe, and the second car visit (Day 103) returns to the car with all of it in hand.

Financing and production (Mathematics, Day 2) — deep. The Model T touring car cost $850 at its 1908 launch; by 1925 a touring car listed at about $290 and a runabout at about $260, and about 15 million Model Ts had been built when production ended in 1927. A price that falls by a steady percentage each year is a straight line on Day 2’s logarithmic scale; the worked example below puts the Model T’s average fall at about 6 percent a year in nominal dollars, though the price did not fall every year. General prices were higher in 1925 than in 1908, so the fall in real terms was steeper. Why the price fell — interchangeable parts and learning curves — is Engineering’s job (Days 96 and 97). Financing, at your level, is a mechanism in one clause: a captive finance arm lends against the car as collateral and turns a purchase price into a monthly payment; General Motors founded such an arm, GMAC, in 1919. The second car visit (Day 103) returns to the Model T.

Regulation (Mathematics, Days 8, 21 and 23) — deep. This link came first. Britain’s Locomotive Act of 1865 capped steam road locomotives at 4 miles an hour in the country and 2 in towns, with a man walking ahead carrying a red flag, about twenty years before Benz’s patent. It was written for steam traction engines, not cars, but when petrol cars reached Britain in the 1890s they fell under it. The Act of 1896 raised the cap to 14 miles an hour, and the London-to-Brighton “Emancipation Run” that November celebrated the change.

Regulation, continued. A century later the evidence becomes quantitative. After the 1973 oil embargo, Congress set a national 55-mph limit (the Emergency Highway Energy Conservation Act, signed 2 January 1974). Day 8’s optimization lesson explains why the saving could be small: fuel use per mile is flat near its best speed and climbs steeply only well above it, so a cap helps mainly cars running far out on the steep side, and a Transportation Research Board review in 1984 judged the saving modest. The limit may also have saved lives, though that was estimated rather than measured. Its repeal in 1995 became Day 21’s natural experiment: Friedman, Hedeker and Richter (2009) compared states and attributed about 12,500 extra deaths over 1995–2005, a 3.2 percent rise, to the higher limits. Debated And regulation by fixed test cycle failed under Day 23’s Goodhart’s law (when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure): Volkswagen’s diesel software, which the US Environmental Protection Agency said in September 2015 could detect a test, was a flagrant case of engineering for the measure instead of the goal.

Adoption (Mathematics, Day 7) — deep. US automobile registrations rose from about 8,000 in 1900 to about 23 million in 1929 (Federal Highway Administration), an average of about 32 percent a year, the steep rise of an S-curve. Day 7’s warning applies: the ceiling is an assumption fed into the curve, not something early data reveal. The Depression stalled the count for several years, and a count has no natural ceiling, so registrations kept climbing for decades as the population grew and households added second and third vehicles. What saturates is the share of households that own a car.

Consequences (Mathematics, Days 14 and 18) — deep. The World Health Organization’s Global Status Report on Road Safety 2023 estimates about 1.19 million road deaths worldwide in 2021 — a modelled estimate, not a count — with more than half among pedestrians, cyclists and motorcyclists and over 90 percent in low- and middle-income countries. In the United States, deaths per 100 million vehicle-miles fell from about 24 in 1921 (a rough early estimate) to about 1.3 in the early 2020s, a fall of about 95 percent. That is a rate, and Day 18 says what to do with a rate: ask what else changed. Roads, vehicles, trauma care, seat-belt and drink-driving laws and drinking norms all changed together, so no single one gets the credit. Day 14’s game theory applies to the fleet itself: a heavier vehicle protects its own occupants and raises the risk to everyone it hits, so each household’s safest private choice pushes the fleet toward an outcome worse for all. Michelle White’s 2004 study estimated that for every fatal crash avoided by the occupants of an SUV or pickup, at least four more occur among others. Debated The analogy fails wherever buyers want a larger vehicle for reasons other than safety.

Feedback (Government, Law, Engineering) — preview. The consequences fed back into the rules and the machine. Ralph Nader’s Unsafe at Any Speed (1965) was followed by the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966. The 1973 oil shock produced the 55-mph limit and, in 1975, federal fuel-economy standards whose looser rules for light trucks are often blamed for the later SUV boom. Debated Government (Day 243, How regulation works), Law (Day 254, Tort and product liability) and Engineering (Day 101, Crashworthiness and the Haddon matrix) teach these loops.

Back to the story

So how did a machine that needed a pharmacy and a craftsman reach 23 million registrations? The numbers explain part of the answer. Hydrocarbon fuel packs 60 to 80 times a battery’s energy per kilogram, so a small tank carried real range. The Model T’s price fell an average of about 6 percent a year, which put it within reach of more buyers. Adoption climbed an S-curve at an average of about 32 percent a year for 29 years. And the harm, read as a rate, fell about 95 percent per mile even though the annual US total is still about 40,000 deaths. The numbers cannot say why people wanted cars, how the engine was made to work, or how credit and streets were reorganized around them; later blocks teach those. And they refuse a straight line: Britain had capped steam road vehicles at walking pace about twenty years before Karl Benz’s patent, the invention arguably preceded any pressing need, and the deaths rewrote the rules.

Key Concepts

Energy density
The energy a fuel or battery stores per kilogram; for a given weight it decides how much range a vehicle can carry.
Log-scale price decline
A price falling by a steady percentage each year, which plots as a straight line on a logarithmic axis (Day 2).
S-curve adoption
Ownership that starts slowly, speeds up, then levels off as the market saturates; the ceiling is an assumption, not a law (Day 7).
Rates versus totals
A rate divides harm by exposure, such as deaths per mile driven; the total is the rate times the exposure, so a rate can fall steeply while the total stays large.
Natural experiment
A change of rule or circumstance that sorts places or people into treated and untreated groups as if at random, as when states raised speed limits at different times (Day 21).
Feedback loop
A chain of causes that returns to its start, either reinforcing a change or balancing it; the car’s story is several loops running at once.

Example: Four Ratios, One Car

Here is the car’s numerical case with the arithmetic shown, using only tools from this block. The first two steps turn two endpoints into a compound annual growth rate (CAGR); Day 2’s caution applies to all of them: two endpoints can hide detours.

Worked example
  1. Price. The Model T touring car went from $850 (1908) to about $290 (1925), a factor of 850 ÷ 290 ≈ 2.9 over 17 years. The yearly factor is (290 ÷ 850)1/17 ≈ 0.94, so the price fell about 6 percent a year and halved roughly every ln 2 ÷ 0.063 ≈ 11 years.
  2. Adoption. US registrations went from about 8,000 (1900) to about 23 million (1929), a factor of about 2,900 over 29 years. The yearly factor is 2,9001/29 ≈ 1.32, so registrations grew about 32 percent a year on average and doubled roughly every ln 2 ÷ ln 1.32 ≈ 2.5 years.
  3. Harm as a rate. Deaths per 100 million vehicle-miles went from about 24 (1921) to about 1.3 (early 2020s): 1.3 ÷ 24 ≈ 0.054, a fall of about 95 percent.
  4. Rate times exposure. A Fermi check on exposure: roughly 250 million vehicles at roughly 12,000 miles each is about 3 trillion vehicle-miles a year. At 1.3 per 100 million miles that gives 1.3 × 30,000 ≈ 39,000 deaths, close to the actual total of about 40,000. The rate fell 95 percent and the total is still large because the miles driven grew so much.

The four ratios say how fast and how much. They are silent on why people wanted cars and who bore the harm, which is why the case also needs a loop diagram and an evidence check.

The Loops

A reinforcing loop (R) amplifies a change; a balancing loop (B) pushes back against it. The Systems Thinking block teaches the grammar of such diagrams (Day 26, Feedback loops: reinforcing and balancing; Day 27, Delays, overshoot and oscillation). Here the car’s main loops are described in words.

R1 adoption · R2 price · B1 saturation · B2 harm

Four loops. R1, adoption, is reinforcing. R2, price, is reinforcing. B1, saturation, is balancing. B2, harm, is balancing and delayed.

R1
Adoption, reinforcing. More cars lead to more filling stations, paved roads and dealers, which make a car more useful, which leads to more cars. Bertha’s pharmacy purchase is the loop at its first turn, a single supplier where a network was needed.
R2
Price, reinforcing. More output lowers the cost and price of each car, which brings more buyers and so more output. This is the Model T’s loop; Engineering (Days 96 and 97) teaches the mechanism.
B1
Saturation, balancing. As households acquire cars, fewer remain to buy a first one, so growth slows. This is the S-curve’s ceiling (Day 7).
B2
Harm, balancing and delayed. More driving brings more deaths, which bring safety law and safer engineering, which lower deaths per mile. The delay ran to decades: the federal safety act came in 1966.
Reversed order
Regulation before invention (Britain’s 1865 Locomotive Act) and, arguably, invention before need (Benz’s 1886 patent): the chain’s arrows can run backwards.
Side effects
A rule can gain little where the optimum is flat: the 55-mph limit’s fuel saving was modest (Day 8). A rule can be gamed: fixed test cycles invited Volkswagen’s defeat device (Day 23). And a rule’s effect can stay disputed for decades (Day 21).

Why It Matters

You can now read any technology’s rise in a handful of numbers and see where the reading stops. Fuel energy per kilogram, a steady-percentage price fall, an S-curve and a death rate per mile each answer “how much?” and “how fast?” with a figure you can check. None answers “did people want it?” or “who paid?”, and a chain read as a straight line hides that the rule, the invention and the need arrived in no tidy order. That bears on the question at the center of your book: “progress” needs a target and a beneficiary named, and the numbers supply neither. The loop diagram also tells you where to look next, because a balancing loop with a delay of decades, like the one running from deaths to rules, does harm for years before it corrects. The second car visit (Day 103) follows engineering and production in depth, the third (Day 216) the social links, and the fourth (Day 293) asks whether the car was progress.

Settled. The energy-density gap, the Model T’s price fall, US registration growth and the fall in US deaths per mile are measured; WHO’s 1.19 million is a modelled estimate.

Debated. How much of the falling death rate any one cause explains; how large the speed-limit effects were (the 2009 Friedman study is one estimate); and how far heavier vehicles raise net deaths.

Value judgment. Who gained (mobility, livelihoods), who paid (pedestrians, cyclists and poorer countries bear most deaths) and who consented (bystanders and children did not choose the risk). A utilitarian ledger sums lives and welfare; the natural-law and Christian traditions stress a duty of care to the vulnerable neighbor. The world without the car is an unknowable counterfactual.
