Fukushima Daiichi’s Design Tsunami Was 5.7 Meters. The Wave That Hit It Was More Than Twice as High.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

A distribution’s shape decides what “extreme” can mean: in a bell-curve world a wave more than twice the design value is all but impossible, but where the largest events dominate, the biggest one on record is a poor guide to the next.

Day 12 of 293 · Mathematics 12 of 24 · Distributions: the bell curve and fat tails

A design number of 5.7 meters, and a wave that was never told

Fukushima Daiichi stands on Japan’s Pacific coast. When its first reactors were designed in the 1960s, the tsunami they had to survive was set at 3.1 meters, a figure based on an assessment of the 1960 Chile tsunami. The main buildings of units 1–4 sat on a platform about 10 meters above sea level; the seawater pumps that carried heat away from the reactors stood near the shore, at about 4 meters. In 2002 the Japan Society of Civil Engineers published a new method for estimating the largest tsunami a coast should plan for. The plant’s operator, the Tokyo Electric Power Company (TEPCO), applied it and raised the design figure to 5.7 meters.

In 2008 TEPCO ran a trial calculation of a harsher case. A 2002 government assessment had held that an earthquake like the Meiji-Sanriku tsunami earthquake of 1896 could strike along the Japan Trench off Fukushima, not only farther north where it had been recorded. TEPCO placed such an earthquake there and computed the wave at its plant: up to about 15.7 meters at part of the site. The company treated the result as a trial figure needing further study, not a forecast, and by the spring of 2011 it had not acted on it.

At 2:46 p.m. on March 11, 2011, a magnitude 9.0 earthquake (the USGS now lists it as 9.1) struck off the Tōhoku coast. Within the hour the tsunami reached the plant. TEPCO’s surveys afterward put the water at roughly 13 to 15 meters on the plant’s own datum — more than twice the design figure, and several meters above the platform the main buildings stood on. It flooded the basements that held the emergency diesel generators and electrical switchgear. Without power for cooling, the cores of units 1, 2 and 3 melted.

The designers had planned for the largest tsunami they judged credible, and had raised that number as their methods improved. How could the real wave be more than twice as high — and was it a once-in-a-millennium freak, or a predictable kind of surprise?

The Big Idea

The shape of the full map of possible outcomes decides what “typical” and “extreme” mean. Where extremes are vanishingly rare, the average tells you almost everything and the biggest event on record is close to the biggest possible. Where the largest events dominate, the biggest one on record is a poor guide to the next.

That map is a distribution: every value a quantity can take, with how likely each is. Draw it for adult heights and you get the familiar bell — a hump at the average and tails that die away so fast that nobody is ten times the average. Draw it for earthquake energies or city populations and you get a spike with a long, slow-fading tail, where a few enormous members dwarf the rest. Two quantities can share the same average. What differs is how the far end behaves, and the far end is where the risk lives.

So before asking “how big could it get?”, ask which kind of world you are in. Where the tail is thin, planning for the largest event seen so far is close to enough. Where it is fat, it can fall far short.

How It Works

Day 11 gave probabilities as weights on a complete list of outcomes. A probability distribution is that list extended to every value a quantity can take, usually drawn as a curve: the higher the curve, the likelier the value, and the area under any stretch of it is the probability of landing in that stretch. Two numbers summarize a distribution before you know its shape: the mean (Day 11’s expected value, the curve’s balance point) and the standard deviation, written σ, the typical distance of a value from the mean. The shape does the rest.

The most famous shape is the normal distribution, the bell curve. It appears when a quantity is the sum of many small, independent nudges — the reason, the central limit theorem, is Day 16’s subject. Its signature is how fast the tails fall. About 68% of values land within one σ of the mean, 95% within two, 99.7% within three. Beyond that the odds collapse. The chance of landing more than 3σ above the mean is about 1 in 740; more than 4σ, about 1 in 31,600; more than 5σ, about 1 in 3.5 million; more than 6σ, about 1 in a billion. The curve’s height shrinks like e−k²/2, with the distance k squared in the exponent, so each extra σ makes the event rarer by more than the last one did. Double the threshold from 3σ to 6σ and the event becomes about 1.4 million times rarer.

Two things follow in a bell-curve world. Extremes are bounded in practice: the biggest of a million draws lands, on average, about 4.9σ out, and the biggest of eight billion — roughly the world’s population — about 6.4σ. Multiplying the data 8,000-fold moves the record out by only about 1.5 standard deviations. And averages rule: no single observation moves the mean of a large sample much, so the mean is a trustworthy summary of the whole.

A fat tail (also called a heavy tail) falls off far more slowly. The cleanest case is the power law: the chance of exceeding size x is proportional to x raised to a negative power, P(X > x) ∝ x−α. Day 3 met power laws as straight lines on log-log plots, and this is the same mathematics put to a different use. There the line related two measured quantities, such as metabolic rate and body mass; here it relates a size to how often events at least that large occur, and its slope is −α. Now double the threshold. The odds do not fall a million-fold; they fall by a factor of 2α, which is 2 when α = 1 and about 2.8 when α = 1.5. The tail is a gentle slope instead of a cliff.

Three consequences follow. The largest events dominate. The Gutenberg–Richter law, the 1944 finding of Beno Gutenberg and Charles Richter, says each step up of one magnitude brings roughly ten times fewer earthquakes. But each step releases about 32 times more energy (101.5, or 31.6), so the energy summed over each magnitude class grows about 3.2-fold per step: the rare giants dominate a catalog’s energy. Records are poor guides. With a power-law tail, the largest of n observations typically grows in proportion to n1/α, so when α = 1 a record ten times longer holds an event about ten times bigger, until a physical limit intervenes. In a bell-curve world the record barely moves. Averages are unreliable. Where the tail is fat enough, a handful of extreme values set the mean, so a sample’s average depends on whether it happened to catch one. For a pure power law with α at or below 1 the mean is not even finite; real systems are rescued only by physical limits, such as the finite length of a fault, and where that limit sits is exactly what a short record cannot show.

Reading a return period. Engineers and insurers express extremes as a return period: a “100-year flood” is one with a 1-in-100 chance, in any single year, of being equaled or beaten — its annual exceedance probability, 1%. It is a rate, not a schedule. Years do not take turns, so two 100-year floods can arrive in consecutive years with nothing wrong. The useful question is the chance of at least one over a span that matters. The chance of none in n years is (1 − 1/T)n, so the chance of at least one is 1 − (1 − 1/T)n; for T = 100 and a 30-year mortgage, that is 26%. The deeper catch is that instrument records rarely span more than a century, so a 1-in-1,000 level is extrapolated from a few decades of data by fitting a curve and extending its tail. A bell curve and a power law can agree about the middle of the same record and disagree wildly about the 1,000-year value. A return period is only as good as the assumed shape of the tail.

Every return-period calculation also assumes stationarity: that the distribution generating the data is itself fixed, so the past is a fair sample of the future. If rainfall patterns shift or paving replaces soil, the distribution moves and the old record understates the present. In 2008 Milly and six colleagues titled a paper in Science “Stationarity Is Dead: Whither Water Management?”, arguing that it should no longer be the default assumption in water-resource planning.

Back to the story

Fukushima’s wave looks like a freak only on a bell curve, where a wave more than twice the design value would be effectively impossible. But the tsunami was the child of a magnitude 9.0 earthquake, and earthquake sizes follow the Gutenberg–Richter power law: giants are rarer, but not nearly rare enough to rule out. Instrumental seismology had a little over a century of records, shorter than the spacing of several centuries to about a thousand years that geologists estimated between giant tsunamis on the Sendai plain to the north, where sand sheets left by the 869 Jōgan tsunami lay kilometers inland. Many seismologists had also judged a magnitude 9 unlikely on that stretch of the Japan Trench, partly on a 1980 analysis (Ruff and Kanamori) correlating the largest known quakes with plate age and convergence speed; newer data, and Tōhoku itself, undid that correlation. So the surprise was predictable in kind — a fat tail makes the biggest event on record a sample, not a limit — though not in date or size. Whether the engineers could have foreseen this particular wave is a separate and contested question, taken up in the Evidence check below.

Key Concepts

Probability distribution
The complete map of a quantity’s possible values and how likely each is: Day 11’s list of outcomes and probabilities, extended to every value on a continuous scale.
Normal (bell) curve and standard deviation
The symmetric bell shape that arises from many small independent influences. The standard deviation (σ) is the typical distance from the mean; about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values fall within 1, 2 and 3 σ.
Fat (heavy) tail and power law
A tail that fades far more slowly than a bell curve’s. In a power law the chance of exceeding x falls as x−α, so doubling the threshold only divides the odds by 2α.
Return period and annual exceedance probability
A T-year event has a 1/T chance of being equaled or exceeded in any given year: an average rate, not a schedule, and not a guarantee of a gap between events.
Stationarity
The assumption that the distribution is not changing, so past data fairly samples the future. Return periods rest on it.

Example: Houston’s three “500-year” floods

Houston shows return periods under strain, at the regulation and feedback links of your chain from need to consequence: floodplain maps decide what may be built and who must insure, and each flood feeds back into the maps. Local officials and news reports called the Memorial Day flood of May 2015, the Tax Day flood of April 2016 and Hurricane Harvey in August 2017 “500-year” floods in parts of the region — three in three years.

1. Annual odds. A true 500-year flood has a 1-in-500 chance each year: 1/500 = 0.2%.

2. Three in a row at one spot. If years are independent, three in three specific years is 0.002 × 0.002 × 0.002 = 8 × 10−9, about 1 in 125 million. As luck at a single location, that is not credible.

3. So what gives? Three suspects: the labels were applied to different places, rainfall durations and measures, so they were not one event counted thrice; the distribution moved, with paving and a warming climate (stationarity); or the tails were fatter than the estimates assumed.

4. The mortgage view. Over a 30-year mortgage, a “100-year” flood has a 1 − 0.9930 = 26% chance of occurring at least once; a “500-year” flood, 1 − 0.99830 = 5.8%.

5. The estimates moved too. NOAA’s 2018 rainfall update for Texas (Atlas 14, Volume 11, using data through 2017, Harvey included) raised the Houston-area “100-year” 24-hour rainfall by roughly a third.

How much of the run reflects climate change, urban development or loose labelling is disputed Debated. One attribution study found that global warming made Harvey’s rainfall about 15% heavier (range 8–19%) and roughly three times as likely (range 1.5–5), a finding about that storm, not the other two. In December 2017 Harris County extended its flood rules from the 100-year floodplain to the 500-year.

Equation / Model

P(X > μ + kσ) ≈ e−k²/2 / (k√2π)   versus   P(X > x) = (xm / x)α

For a normal distribution, the probability that X exceeds its mean plus k standard deviations is approximately e to the power of minus k squared over two, divided by k times the square root of two pi, for large k. For a power law, the probability that X exceeds x equals x-m over x, raised to the power alpha.

X
the random quantity: a wave height, an earthquake’s energy, a city’s population
μ, σ
the mean and standard deviation of the normal distribution
k
how many standard deviations above the mean the threshold sits
x, xm
the threshold size, and the smallest size from which the power law applies
α
the tail exponent: the smaller it is, the fatter the tail

In the first formula k is squared inside an exponential; in the second, x is raised to a fixed power. That is the whole difference between a cliff and a slope. The Gutenberg–Richter law turns out to be a power law in disguise, and the decisive step is to rewrite magnitude as energy:

log10 N(≥M) = a − bM,   E ∝ 101.5M  ⇒  log10 N(≥E) = a − (b/1.5) log10 E

The base-ten logarithm of the number of earthquakes of magnitude at least M equals a minus b times M, and energy E is proportional to ten to the power of one point five M. Substituting M equals log-ten of E over one point five, with the leftover constant absorbed into a, gives the logarithm of the number of earthquakes with energy at least E equal to a minus b over one point five times log-ten of E.

N(≥M)
the number of earthquakes per year, in a region, of magnitude M or larger
a
a constant setting how active the region is overall
b
the Gutenberg–Richter slope, about 1 in many regions: each magnitude step brings about ten times fewer quakes
M, E
magnitude, and the energy released
b/1.5
the power-law exponent α for energy, about 2/3 when b = 1 — below 1, so the largest quakes dominate the total

The last model is the return-period rule for the chance of at least one event across a span:

P(at least one in n years) = 1 − (1 − 1/T)n

The probability of at least one event in n years equals one minus the quantity one minus one over T, raised to the power n.

T
the return period in years; 1/T is the annual exceedance probability
n
the span of years you care about
(1 − 1/T)n
the chance of no event in n years, assuming each year is independent

Why It Matters

You now have a question to put to any claim about extremes — a design flood, a stress test, a “worst case” — before accepting it: is the tail thin or fat, and how do we know? If thin, the worst case on record is close to the worst case possible and the average is a safe guide. If fat, the record’s worst case is a sample, the average is hostage to rare events, and a margin of “twice the record” is a guess. It also shows where the numbers are soft: any return period longer than the record is an extrapolation resting on an assumed tail and on stationarity. Day 13, Networks, returns to heavy tails in the number of links per node.

And it complicates the tidy chain of your car example. “Physical constraints, then engineering” hides a step: choosing a design basis is choosing a probability you will live with, which no equation settles.

Settled. The mathematics of distributions and return periods, and earthquake sizes’ heavy tail.

Debated. Whether Fukushima’s tsunami was foreseeable. TEPCO’s 2008 calculation and the 869 deposits are cited on one side; TEPCO called that figure a trial result. Japanese courts split: a 2022 civil ruling held former executives liable (it was appealed), while the criminal courts acquitted them (2019, 2023). Also debated: how much of Houston’s run reflects climate, development or labelling.

Value judgment. How safe is safe enough, and who decides: cost-benefit analysts accept some residual risk; precautionary approaches weight irreversible catastrophe more; residents near the plant bore the risk without choosing the margin.

Across. Sociology, which studies cities, meets a fat tail of its own in city sizes. George Kingsley Zipf’s 1949 book popularized the rank-size rule, that the n-th largest city is about 1/n the size of the largest: a power law with α = 1. The 2020 Census roughly fits: New York City 8.80 million; Los Angeles 3.90 million (44% of New York’s); Chicago 2.75 million (31%); Houston 2.30 million (26%) — against 50%, 33% and 25%. Xavier Gabaix (1999) showed that if cities grow at the same average percentage rate, and with the same variability, whatever their size, with a floor on size, a power law with exponent 1 results. How far it holds is disputed Debated: Jan Eeckhout (2004) argued the whole size distribution is lognormal, and later tests found the largest cities depart from that.

Remember These
  • In a bell curve each extra standard deviation makes an event vastly rarer (6σ is about 1 in a billion); in a power law, doubling the size only divides the odds by 2α.
  • In a fat-tailed world the largest events dominate the totals, and the biggest on record is a sample, not a limit.
  • A “T-year” event is a 1-in-T annual chance, not a schedule: a 100-year flood has a 26% chance of occurring at least once in 30 years.
  • Return periods longer than the record are extrapolations that assume stationarity.
  • Fukushima’s wave, more than twice the design figure, was a surprise of a predictable kind.
