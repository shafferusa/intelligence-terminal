Dozens of Studies Said Hormone Therapy Protected Women’s Hearts. Then a Randomized Trial Found No Protection.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

Letting a lottery decide who gets a treatment makes the treated and untreated groups alike on average in everything, measured or not — which is why one trial in 2002 could overturn decades of observational evidence.

Mathematics

Day 20 of 293 · Mathematics 20 of 24 · How We Know VI: Randomized controlled trials

A trial stopped three years short of its plan

In September 1991 the New England Journal of Medicine published the ten-year follow-up of the Nurses’ Health Study, which had tracked 48,470 postmenopausal American nurses. After adjustment for age and other risk factors, the women currently taking estrogen had suffered major coronary disease at 0.56 times the rate of women who never had — a risk about 44 percent lower. That same year a review pooling more than thirty published studies put the figure at 0.56 as well. Over the following decade, millions of women were prescribed hormone therapy, in part on the strength of that evidence.

None of those studies had decided by chance who took the pills. The Women’s Health Initiative did. It enrolled 16,608 postmenopausal women who still had a uterus and gave each one a single daily tablet. By lottery, the tablet held either conjugated equine estrogens (0.625 milligrams) plus medroxyprogesterone acetate (2.5 milligrams) or an identical-looking placebo. The plan was to follow them for an average of 8.5 years.

On May 31, 2002, with the women an average of 5.2 years into the trial, its independent data and safety monitoring board recommended stopping. A boundary fixed in advance had been crossed — the excess of invasive breast cancer was past it — and across all the outcomes tracked, the harms outweighed the benefits. The National Institutes of Health announced the stop on July 9, 2002. On heart disease, the benefit the observational studies had promised, the women on hormones had more events, not fewer: 37 coronary events per 10,000 women per year against 30 on placebo, a hazard ratio of 1.29.

How could dozens of studies, following tens of thousands of real women for years, all point toward protection when the trial found none? And why should one trial outweigh them all?

The Big Idea

Assigning a treatment by chance makes the treated and untreated groups alike, on average, in every respect — age, income, habits, genes, and everything nobody thought to measure — so a difference in their outcomes estimates what the treatment itself does, give or take the play of chance. A study built this way is a randomized controlled trial.

Observation cannot make that promise. When people choose a treatment, or doctors choose it for them, the choosers differ from the non-choosers in ways that also affect their health, so comparing the two groups mixes the treatment’s effect with differences between the people. A lottery has no motives. It cannot favor the healthier, the richer or the more careful, so it can leave the groups different in those ways only by luck — and luck is something statistics can measure (Day 17).

The lottery is not the whole design. Three safeguards keep it from being undone afterward: a control group, usually given a placebo; blinding, so that nobody’s expectations change what is recorded; and a plan, fixed in advance, for which outcomes count and how they will be analyzed.

How It Works

The problem: you only ever see one of the two worlds. To say a treatment caused a recovery, you would want to compare each person with themselves in a parallel world where they went untreated. Nobody can visit that world. Statisticians call the missing outcome the counterfactual: what would have happened otherwise. Every method for finding causes is a way of approximating it with other people. The obvious approximation — compare people who took the treatment with people who did not — works only if the two groups would have fared the same without it. Day 18 showed how that fails: a third factor, a confounder, that affects both who gets the treatment and how they turn out, can create, hide or even reverse an association.

Why adjustment is not enough. The usual repair is statistical adjustment: compare women of the same age, weight and smoking history. The Nurses’ Health Study did this carefully. But adjustment balances only what was measured, and measured well. Surveys of who took hormones tended to find users better educated and better off than non-users, though not uniformly healthier on every measure. Everything that went into those choices — income, access to doctors, caution with one’s own health — can push heart disease down by itself. That tangle is the healthy-user effect, a case of selection bias: the treated group was selected, by its own choices and its doctors’, to be healthier than the untreated group before any pill was swallowed. No list of adjustment variables can be known to be complete.

Random assignment removes the choosing. A computer or a table of random numbers, not the patient or the doctor, decides who gets which arm, and the assignment is concealed until the person is enrolled so that nobody can steer a sicker patient toward one arm. Now ask what could differ between the groups. Not education or income or caution: the lottery paid no attention to them, so they are the same in both arms on average. The same holds for traits nobody measured or even knew existed. “On average” has a precise meaning. Run the lottery many times and the arms would differ by chance in one direction about as often as in the other, and the typical size of that chance imbalance shrinks like 1/√n as the arms grow — the square-root law of Day 16’s sampling error. Random sampling makes a sample resemble its population; random assignment makes two arms resemble each other. One small trial can still be unlucky. A large one rarely is.

The control group and the placebo. The comparison arm turns the question into a difference between arms rather than a before-and-after. Many things happen to patients whatever the treatment: people enrolled when they feel their worst tend to improve anyway (Day 19’s regression to the mean), and people who believe they are being treated often report feeling better. A control arm gets the same attention and the same expectations, usually with a placebo — an inert tablet that looks identical — so all of this happens equally in both arms and cancels in the comparison. What remains is attributable to what differed: the drug.

Blinding. If participants know which arm they are in, they may behave differently; if the clinicians who diagnose outcomes know, their judgment can tilt, especially on soft diagnoses. In a double-blind trial neither the participants nor the staff who assess them know who is on which tablet. Blinding does not remove confounding — randomization does that. It protects the measurement of the outcome and keeps the arms from being treated differently afterward.

Outcomes fixed in advance. Measure enough outcomes in enough subgroups and some will come out “significant” by luck alone (Day 17’s look-elsewhere effect). A trial therefore names its main outcome and its analysis plan before the data come in. The Women’s Health Initiative named coronary heart disease as the benefit it was testing and invasive breast cancer as the main risk to watch, and it set its stopping rule beforehand too. Day 22 returns to what happens when studies skip this step.

Intention-to-treat. After the lottery, things go wrong: some participants stop taking the pills, some drop out, some switch. The tempting move is to analyze only those who followed instructions. But people who stick with a regimen are not a random half of their arm — they tend to be the healthier, more conscientious ones — so analyzing only them lets selection bias back in through the side door. The remedy is intention-to-treat analysis: count everyone in the arm to which the lottery assigned them, whatever they then did. The Women’s Health Initiative analyzed its principal results this way, even though some women in both arms stopped taking their tablets before the trial ended. The price is that the result is a diluted estimate of what the pill does when taken as directed; the gain is that it measures what a policy of prescribing it does, with the bias protection intact.

Reading the result. A trial that waits for events such as heart attacks summarizes the contrast with a hazard ratio: the rate at which events happen in the treated arm divided by the rate in the control arm, among people still event-free at each moment. A ratio of 1 means no difference; 1.29 means the rate on hormones was about 29 percent higher. Read it beside the absolute figures: 37 versus 30 coronary events per 10,000 women per year is 7 extra events per 10,000 women each year, real but small for any one woman. Then chance is Day 17’s business. The reported 95 percent confidence interval for the heart-disease hazard ratio was 1.02 to 1.63; adjusted for the repeated interim looks at the data, which a stopping rule requires, it widened to 0.85 to 1.97. So this trial alone did not prove that the hormones harm the heart. What it showed is that protection of the size the observational studies suggested, 0.56, was far outside what its data would allow, since even the wider interval stops at 0.85.

Back to the story

The Nurses’ Health Study compared women who had chosen hormones, or been given them, with women who had not, and those groups already differed before the first pill. The trial removed the choosing. With a lottery in charge, the only systematic difference left between the arms was the tablet, and the tablet did not protect the heart. That is why a trial of 16,608 women could outweigh studies of tens of thousands more: not because it was bigger, but because it was built so that the explanation “the women were different” was no longer available. The healthy-user effect is the textbook account of the gap, and it is probably part of it Debated — reviews also point to differences in design, since many observational studies followed women already on therapy, and in the women themselves, who were generally younger and nearer menopause than the trial’s average participant, about 63. One more caveat: the 1991 paper was mostly about estrogen alone, while the trial tested estrogen with a progestin, so the match is not perfect; but observational studies of the combined pills had also suggested protection.

Key Concepts

Random assignment
Deciding by chance, with the next assignment hidden from whoever enrolls people, who gets which treatment, so the arms are alike on average in everything, measured or not.
Control group and placebo
The comparison arm, which does not receive the treatment under test. A placebo is an inert look-alike that gives that arm the same attention and expectations.
Blinding (double-blind)
Keeping participants, and in a double-blind trial also the staff who assess them, from knowing who received what, so that knowledge cannot color behavior or diagnosis.
Selection bias (healthy-user effect)
A difference between groups that comes from how people ended up in them. The healthy-user effect is the case where people who choose a treatment are healthier for other reasons.
Intention-to-treat
Analyzing everyone in the arm they were randomly assigned to, whatever they did next, which keeps the lottery’s balance intact.
Hazard ratio
The event rate in one arm divided by the rate in the other at any moment, among those still event-free. A value of 1 means no difference.

Example: The 1954 Salk Polio Vaccine Trial, Run Two Ways at Once

In 1954 about 1.8 million children took part in a field trial of Jonas Salk’s polio vaccine, evaluated by Thomas Francis Jr. of the University of Michigan. It used two designs side by side. In 11 states, children whose parents consented were randomly given vaccine or an identical placebo, double-blind. In 33 states, every consenting second-grader was vaccinated, and first- and third-graders, unvaccinated, served as observed controls.

1. The observed-control areas. Vaccinated second-graders had 25 cases per 100,000; the unvaccinated first- and third-graders had 54. That looks like a rate about 54 percent lower — but only consenting families’ children were vaccinated.

2. Two biases, pulling opposite ways. Consenting families tended to be better off, and in that era better-off children tended to be more vulnerable to polio, which makes the vaccine look worse than it was. And the doctors diagnosing polio knew who had been vaccinated, while mild cases resemble other illnesses, which makes it look better. Nobody could say what the net bias was.

3. The randomized areas. Parents consented first, then a lottery decided. About 200,000 children got vaccine and about 200,000 got placebo; the rates were 28 against 71 per 100,000. Since 28 ÷ 71 ≈ 0.39, the rate was about 61 percent lower.

4. Chance. That is roughly 200 cases in all, split between two nearly equal arms. A useless vaccine would put about 100 ± 7 in each; the arms held about 60 and about 140, more than five standard deviations from an even split — beyond Day 17’s five-sigma bar.

5. Why it was believed. Assignment after consent, an identical placebo and blinded diagnosis closed the selection and diagnosis routes, leaving only chance, which step 4 ruled out. The two designs landed in the same neighborhood here, but only the randomized one could show that the neighborhood was safe to trust. The announcement that the vaccine worked came on April 12, 1955.

Equation / Model

E[Y | D=1] − E[Y | D=0]  =  E[Y1 − Y0 | D=1]  +  ( E[Y0 | D=1] − E[Y0 | D=0] )

The average outcome among the treated minus the average outcome among the untreated equals the average effect of the treatment on the treated, plus the selection bias: the average untreated outcome of the treated group minus the average untreated outcome of the untreated group.

Y
the outcome actually observed, such as whether a woman developed heart disease
D
1 if a person received the treatment, 0 if not
Y1, Y0
the outcome the same person would have had with, and without, the treatment; only one is ever observed

the average over the people in the group with D equal to d
E[Y1 − Y0 | D=1]
the causal effect we want, averaged over those who were treated
E[Y0 | D=1] − E[Y0 | D=0]
selection bias: how different the two groups would have been even if nobody had been treated

The decisive step is what a lottery does to the second term:

D independent of (Y0, Y1)  ⇒  E[Y0 | D=1] = E[Y0 | D=0]  ⇒  selection bias = 0

If assignment D is independent of the potential outcomes Y-zero and Y-one, then the average untreated outcome is the same in the treated and untreated groups, so the selection bias is zero.

independent of
knowing who got the treatment tells you nothing about how anyone would have fared with or without it — exactly what a coin flip guarantees

What is left is the causal effect, plus the chance that this particular lottery drew slightly unbalanced arms. The equalities hold on average over repeated lotteries, not in any one trial, and that leftover chance is what Day 17’s tests and intervals measure.

Why It Matters

Randomization hands you a question to put to any claim that X causes or prevents Y: who chose X, and could their reasons also affect Y? If the answer is yes and nobody randomized, the association is a lead, not a verdict, however large it is and however many studies repeat it — thirty studies that share one bias add precision, not accuracy. It also gives you a checklist for reading a trial: how people were assigned and whether that was concealed, who knew what, whether the main outcome was named in advance, whether people were analyzed as assigned, and whether the headline is a relative or an absolute number. For a book about whether inventions and reforms made lives better, the same question governs every claim of progress: compared with whom, and who chose? Where a trial would be unethical or impractical, Day 21 turns to natural experiments, and Day 22 to how the published record of studies can mislead.

Settled. In the women enrolled (average age about 63), estrogen plus progestin did not prevent heart disease, raised breast cancer, stroke and clot risk, and lowered colorectal cancer and fractures; in 2022 the US Preventive Services Task Force again recommended against it for preventing chronic conditions.

Debated. Whether starting near menopause changes the heart risk (the trials’ own subgroup patterns were suggestive but not conclusive), and how much of the gap was healthy-user bias rather than design differences.

Value judgment. Whether it is right to assign people by lottery to something that may help or harm them, and who should carry that risk for knowledge others will use.

Connections

Back. Day 18, Correlation, confounding and Simpson’s paradox, showed that a hidden third factor can create, hide or reverse an association, and that stratifying fixes only what you measured. Randomization is the general answer: it balances every confounder at once, including those no one thought of. Once the arms are alike, only chance is left, and Day 17, Significance, p-values and systematic error, supplies the tools for it, such as the 1.02 to 1.63 interval above.

Across. In Computing, online A/B tests are randomized controlled trials at software scale: users are assigned at random to the current page or a variant and the outcomes compared. In 2012 an idea for lengthening the headlines of Bing’s search ads sat in a backlog for more than six months; when an engineer finally ran it as a randomized experiment, revenue rose by about 12 percent — over $100 million a year in the United States alone, the authors estimated — without hurting key user-experience metrics (Ron Kohavi and Stefan Thomke, Harvard Business Review, 2017). The shared mechanism is the lottery itself; the difference is cost, since millions of users can be assigned in software and results can arrive within hours.

Remember These
  • Choosers differ from non-choosers, so comparing them mixes a treatment’s effect with those differences; adjustment fixes only what was measured.
  • Random assignment makes the arms alike on average in everything, measured or not, so the remaining difference is the treatment’s effect plus chance.
  • A placebo, blinding, outcomes named in advance and intention-to-treat analysis each guard a different door through which bias could re-enter.
  • A hazard ratio is a relative rate (1.29: about 29 percent higher); read it beside the absolute difference, 7 extra coronary events per 10,000 women a year.
  • The Women’s Health Initiative ruled out heart protection near the 0.56 that the observational studies suggested, without by itself proving harm.
