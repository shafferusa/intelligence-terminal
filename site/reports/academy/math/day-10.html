Andrew Wiles Announced a Proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem in June 1993. Within Months, a Referee Had Found a Hole.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 16 min read

A proof is a chain of steps that settles a claim in every case at once — which is why computers checking exponent after exponent could not replace one, and why a single bad step could put seven years of work in doubt.

Mathematics

Day 10 of 293 · Mathematics 10 of 24 · Proof: how mathematics knows

‘I think I’ll stop here’

On Wednesday, 23 June 1993, at the Isaac Newton Institute in Cambridge, England, Andrew Wiles gave the last of three lectures under a title that gave nothing away: “Modular forms, elliptic curves and Galois representations.” In that final lecture he assembled an argument he had worked on in near-secrecy for seven years. Then he wrote Fermat’s Last Theorem on the board as a corollary of what he had shown — a consequence that follows almost at once — and said, “I think I’ll stop here.”

The claim was old. Traditionally dated to about 1637, it began as a note Pierre de Fermat scribbled in the margin of his copy of Diophantus’s Arithmetica: the equation xn + yn = zn has no solution in positive whole numbers when n is greater than 2, and he had “a truly marvelous proof” that the margin was too narrow to contain. No such proof was ever found. In the three and a half centuries since, the best mathematicians had picked at the problem. Euler handled n = 3. Sophie Germain, who as a young woman wrote to Gauss under a man’s name, proved a result that covered part of the problem for a whole family of exponents. By 1993 computers had ruled out solutions for every exponent up to roughly four million. Not one of these was a proof for all exponents.

Then came the trouble. While referees went through the long manuscript, one of them, Nick Katz of Princeton, raised a question about a single step, and it proved to be a real gap. In December 1993 Wiles acknowledged publicly that a problem remained. On 19 September 1994, after months of attempts to repair it, many of them with his former student Richard Taylor, he saw how to get around it, and the corrected proof appeared in the Annals of Mathematics in May 1995.

So here is the puzzle. If computers had already confirmed the theorem for millions of exponents, why did the world need Wiles’s proof — and how could one gap in one step put seven years of work in doubt?

The Big Idea

A mathematical proof derives a claim, step by step, from axioms and from results already proved, so that the claim holds in every case at once — with a certainty that no number of examples, and no experiment, can give. That certainty has a condition: it holds only if every single step is valid.

Evidence from cases works by accumulation. Each confirming example makes a claim more believable, and nothing ever makes it certain, because the next case might break it. Deduction works differently. It does not look at cases at all. It shows that if the starting assumptions are accepted, the conclusion cannot be otherwise — which is how you know the angles of any flat triangle add up to 180 degrees without measuring every triangle.

The price of that certainty is twofold. The claim is certain only relative to its starting points, and the argument is trustworthy only while no step in it is broken. The second condition is what made the autumn of 1993 so tense, and it is also why a repaired proof is as good as one that was never broken.

How It Works

Start with what a proof is not. A pile of confirming examples is evidence, sometimes very good evidence, but it is a different kind of thing. Take the polynomial n2 + n + 41, known since Euler to produce primes. Try n = 0, 1, 2, and so on up to 39 and you get a prime every time: forty cases in a row, no exceptions. Then n = 40 gives 402 + 40 + 41 = 1,681, which is 41 × 41. Forty successes, and then a counterexample: a single case that breaks a general claim. One counterexample is enough to refute a claim; no number of confirmations is enough to prove one. Even a conjecture of Euler’s fell this way. In 1769 he conjectured that it takes at least n whole numbers, each raised to the nth power, to add up to another nth power. It stood for nearly two centuries, until a computer search by Lander and Parkin in 1966 found 275 + 845 + 1105 + 1335 = 1445, both sides equal to 61,917,364,224: four fifth powers where the conjecture said at least five were needed.

A proof goes the other way round. It begins from axioms, statements accepted without proof as the agreed rules of the game: Euclid’s postulates for plane geometry, the Peano axioms for whole numbers, the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms of set theory for most of modern mathematics. It then moves by steps that logic forces: if A is established, and A implies B, then B is established. What comes out is a theorem. Its certainty is conditional but total: if the axioms hold, the theorem holds in every case at once, with nothing left to check. The condition matters. In the early nineteenth century Lobachevsky and Bolyai, working separately, replaced Euclid’s parallel postulate with a rival and built a geometry with different theorems, one in which the angles of a triangle add up to less than 180 degrees. Deduction guarantees what follows from the axioms; it does not by itself say which axioms describe the physical world.

One of the most powerful moves is proof by contradiction: assume the opposite of what you want, deduce something impossible, and conclude that the assumption was false. Euclid’s proof that there are infinitely many primes, in Book IX of the Elements, is usually told this way. Suppose the primes were finite, listed as p1, p2, …, pk. Form N = p1 × p2 × … × pk + 1. N has some prime factor q. If q were on the list, it would divide the product and divide N, so it would divide their difference, which is 1 — impossible. So q is a prime missing from the list, contradicting the assumption that the list was complete. The argument does not claim that N itself is prime: with the first six primes, 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 + 1 = 30,031 = 59 × 509. All it needs is a prime factor outside the list.

Large proofs are built from smaller ones. A lemma is a result proved on the way to something bigger; a theorem is a result worth stating in its own right; a corollary is a consequence that follows almost immediately from a theorem. The stack matters because it is how anyone can trust a proof too long for one mind: each piece has been checked, often by other people, and later work stands on it. Partial progress on Fermat shows the pattern. Sophie Germain proved that if p and 2p + 1 are both prime — as with p = 3, 5, 11, 23, 29, 41, 53, 83 and 89 below a hundred — then xp + yp = zp has no whole-number solution in which none of x, y, z is divisible by p. In the early 1800s she had written to Gauss as “Monsieur Le Blanc,” and in 1823 Adrien-Marie Legendre published her result with credit to her. It is a real theorem about a real family of exponents, yet it covers only part of the problem for each of them, far short of Fermat’s claim for every exponent.

Wiles’s proof has this same layered shape, and its outer layer is a proof by contradiction. Suppose Fermat’s equation did have a solution. In the mid-1980s Gerhard Frey noticed that a solution would let you write down an unusual curve, a particular kind of elliptic curve, and in 1986 Kenneth Ribet proved that such a curve could not be “modular,” a technical property of an elliptic curve’s arithmetic. Wiles, with Taylor, proved that curves of that type must be modular. Both cannot be true, so no solution exists. The Equation section below sets the chain out step by step. What matters here is its logical shape: at no point does the argument search for solutions.

Now the second half of the puzzle. A proof is a chain, and a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. If every step is valid, the conclusion is established; if one step is not, the conclusion has not been established, even when every other step is flawless. That is not the same as being false: a gap means the proof fails, not that the theorem does. Katz’s gap sat in one step of a very long chain, and the rest of the architecture — Frey’s curve, Ribet’s theorem, the plan to prove modularity — was untouched. Once Wiles and Taylor found a valid route around that one step, the whole chain was restored. Checking is part of the method, and it can fail in public: Alfred Kempe published a proof of the four-colour theorem in 1879, and Percy Heawood found a flaw in it eleven years later.

Back to the story

Now the puzzle resolves. The computer checks, however long their list, settled exponents one at a time, so infinitely many exponents stayed untouched, and nothing about the exponents up to roughly four million says what the next one holds — just as forty primes in a row from Euler’s polynomial said nothing about n = 40. Wiles’s proof settled every exponent at once, by deduction from earlier theorems, which is why the world needed it. And because it was a chain, a single invalid step was enough to leave the whole claim unestablished, while one valid repair, found with Taylor, was enough to restore it.

Key Concepts

Axiom
A statement accepted without proof as a starting point of a mathematical theory, such as Euclid’s postulates or the Peano axioms for whole numbers. A proof’s certainty is always relative to its axioms.
Deduction versus induction
Deduction (proof) reasons from premises so that the conclusion must follow. Induction, in the everyday sense used here, generalises from observed cases and can make a claim probable but never certain. Beware the name: “mathematical induction,” a technique for proving statements about all whole numbers, is deduction despite the word.
Lemma, theorem, corollary
A lemma is a result proved as a stepping-stone; a theorem is a result proved in its own right; a corollary is a consequence that follows almost immediately from a theorem.
Proof by contradiction
Assume the claim is false, deduce something impossible, and conclude the claim is true (reductio ad absurdum).
Counterexample
A single case that violates a general claim and so refutes it, like n = 40 for the prime-producing polynomial.
Formal verification
Using a theorem-proving program to check, step by step, a proof that a design or program meets its specification. It is only as strong as the specification and the assumptions behind it.

Example: The Pentium That Divided Wrongly

In October 1994 Thomas Nicely, a mathematician at Lynchburg College in Virginia, found that his Pentium computer sometimes divided wrongly: five entries were missing from a lookup table inside the chip’s division hardware. A division whose answer should have been 1.33382045 (4,195,835 ÷ 3,145,727) came out as 1.33373907 on a flawed chip, wrong from the fifth significant digit. Intel first called the error too rare to matter — roughly one division in nine billion on random inputs, by its estimate — then offered replacements, and in January 1995 took a $475 million charge. A flaw this rare is hard for testing to find, because testing samples, and a sample is evidence from cases.

1. Count the inputs. Fix the exponent and look only at the 52 stored fraction bits of an ordinary double-precision number: 252 possible fractions. A division takes two, so 252 × 252 = 2104 ≈ 2.0 × 1031 pairs. Signs and exponents only add more.

2. Time to try them all. At a generous 109 divisions per second: 2.0 × 1031 ÷ 109 = 2.0 × 1022 seconds.

3. Convert. 2.0 × 1022 ÷ 3.16 × 107 seconds per year ≈ 6.4 × 1014 years — more than forty thousand times the universe’s 13.8 billion.

4. Sampling does not rescue you. At one error per 9 billion random divisions, a billion random tests expect 109 ÷ (9 × 109) ≈ 0.11 hits — about a 10% chance of seeing the bug at all.

5. Proof reasons about structure. The divider’s correctness turns on a table of about a thousand non-empty entries. A proof checks each entry against the condition the algorithm needs and argues that the rest follows for every input: on the order of a thousand cases, not 1031.

Chipmakers drew the lesson that no test suite can try every input, so they invested heavily in proving designs correct. Formal verification states the specification as mathematics — for division, that the result is the correctly rounded quotient for every pair of inputs — and then has a theorem prover check a proof that the design meets it. The floating-point division program of AMD’s K5 was proved correct this way in the mid-1990s with the ACL2 prover, and Intel built formal-verification teams of its own. This is proof applied to hardware.

Equation / Model

xn + yn = zn has no solution in positive whole numbers x, y, z when n > 2

x to the n plus y to the n equals z to the n has no solution in positive whole numbers x, y and z whenever n is greater than 2.

x, y, z
the unknowns: positive whole numbers
n
the exponent: a whole number greater than 2

It is enough to prove this for n = 4 and for odd prime exponents. Any n above 2 is divisible by 4 or by an odd prime p, and a solution for n would give one for that divisor. Fermat himself proved n = 4 and Euler n = 3, so Wiles needed primes p of 5 or more. His proof is by contradiction, and its decisive step is a single displayed chain:

ap + bp = cp  ⇒  E: y2 = x(x − ap)(x + bp)  ⇒  E is not modular (Ribet) and E is modular (Wiles, Taylor)  ⇒  contradiction

Suppose a to the p plus b to the p equals c to the p. Then the curve E, y squared equals x times the quantity x minus a to the p times the quantity x plus b to the p, would exist. Ribet showed E cannot be modular. Wiles, with Taylor, showed E must be modular. That is a contradiction, so no such a, b and c exist.

p
an odd prime exponent, 5 or more
a, b, c
a hypothetical solution: positive whole numbers with no common factor, which can be assumed
E
Frey’s elliptic curve built from that hypothetical solution; its x and y are the curve’s own coordinates, not the x and y of Fermat’s equation
elliptic curve
a curve of the form y2 = a cubic polynomial in x
modular
an elliptic curve is modular when the counts of its points modulo each prime are matched by the coefficients of a modular form, a function with a very high degree of symmetry
⇒
“implies”: each arrow is a proved theorem or a valid step

The step Wiles and Taylor proved is that every semistable elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular; semistable is a technical condition on how a curve behaves modulo each prime, and Frey’s curve satisfies it. Every arrow is a result someone else can check, and the contradiction at the end is purely logical.

Why It Matters

Understanding proof lets you ask three questions of any confident claim. Is this a deduction or a pile of cases? From which starting points, and does the real thing actually satisfy them? And who or what checked each step? Outside mathematics proof in this sense is rare and evidence is the norm, so the useful habit is noticing which one you are being offered. It also separates two failures that look alike, a refuted claim and an unsupported one: Wiles’s gap left his theorem unsupported, not false, and that difference decides whether work is thrown away or repaired. For engineers it relocates the risk: a verified system is no longer exposed to the bugs its proof rules out, but to everything the proof assumed. And a challenge for the book: the word “proven” belongs to deduction. An empirical claim, however strong, is supported, and calling it proven borrows a certainty it has not earned. When proof is unavailable, Day 15 (Bayes’ theorem and base rates) begins the toolkit for weighing evidence.

Settled. A valid proof, correctly checked, settles its claim in every case, given its axioms; Wiles’s proof was refereed, published and accepted.

Debated. Mathematicians and philosophers disagree about what certainty means for proofs too long to survey or that lean on computers, such as the 1976 four-colour theorem or Thomas Hales’s proof of the Kepler conjecture, formally completed in 2014.

Value judgment. How much proof to demand before trusting a system, when verification costs years and an error may cost lives or liberty, is a judgment about acceptable risk and who bears it; mathematics can price the proof but not the risk.

Across. Law has its own standard of proof, and comparing it with mathematics shows where each one’s certainty comes from. In In re Winship (1970), a New York juvenile case, the US Supreme Court held that due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute a crime. That standard is a threshold of confidence reached from evidence — witnesses, documents, exhibits — with no axioms to deduce from. Justice Harlan’s concurrence rested it on “a fundamental value determination of our society that it is far worse to convict an innocent man than to let a guilty man go free.” Value judgment Both fields set a rule for when a claim counts as established, but the analogy breaks exactly where mathematics claims certainty: a verdict can be wrong though every juror was honest and every rule followed, while a valid proof cannot be wrong about what follows from its axioms.

Remember These
  • Examples are evidence and a proof is deduction: n2 + n + 41 gives primes for n = 0 through 39 and fails at n = 40.
  • A proof starts from axioms and takes only valid steps, so it settles every case at once, but only if every step is valid and only relative to its axioms.
  • Proof by contradiction assumes the opposite and derives an impossibility; Wiles’s proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem has that shape.
  • One bad step leaves a conclusion unsupported without making it false, and one valid repair restores the whole chain.
  • Formal verification proves a design meets its specification, so it is only as good as the specification and the assumptions behind it.
