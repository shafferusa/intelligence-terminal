John Snow Couldn’t Randomize London’s Water. Two Companies’ Tangled Pipes Nearly Did It for Him.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 14 min read

When an accident of history sorts people into groups as if by a coin toss, comparing how the groups change can expose a cause no one could randomize — but only if the groups would have moved together without it.

Mathematics

Day 21 of 293 · Mathematics 21 of 24 · How We Know VII: Natural experiments

Two water companies, one street

In the summer of 1854 cholera came back to London. John Snow, a London physician, had argued in print since 1849 that the disease spread through drinking water fouled by the waste of earlier victims, not through the foul air that many of his colleagues blamed. Arguing was not proving, and South London offered a chance to do more.

There, two private companies piped the Thames to customers’ taps. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company still drew its water from the tidal river at Battersea Fields, where London’s sewage reached it. The Lambeth Company had moved its intake in 1852 to Thames Ditton, upriver and beyond the reach of the tide. Their pipes ran down the same streets, and one house might take one company’s water while its neighbour took the other’s.

For every house where someone had died of cholera, Snow set out to learn which company supplied it. When residents did not know, a chemical test for salt, which the tidal water carried in greater quantity, could tell the two supplies apart. His tally for the seven weeks to 26 August 1854 ran like this. In the 40,046 houses on Southwark and Vauxhall water, 1,263 people died of cholera, or 315 per 10,000 houses. In the 26,107 houses on Lambeth water, 98 died, or 37 per 10,000. In the rest of London the rate was 59. The first rate was more than eight times the second.

Snow had not assigned anyone to drink sewage, and nobody had tossed a coin. The households he was comparing had not been chosen by him, and households differ, as a rule, in a hundred ways. How could that comparison show that water carried cholera?

The Big Idea

A natural experiment is a situation in which an accident of nature, history or policy sorts people into treated and untreated groups as if at random, so that comparing how the two groups change can reveal a cause that no one could ethically or practically randomize.

Day 20 taught the clean way to find a cause: let a coin decide who is treated, and the groups come out alike on average in everything, measured or not. But you cannot flip a coin to decide who drinks sewage, which states raise a speed limit, or whose mother lives through a famine. Sometimes the world does the sorting for you, with a blockade, a law passed in some places and not in others, or two companies’ pipes laid down the same streets. The only question is whether it sorted people arbitrarily enough to stand in for a coin.

The standard way to use such sorting is difference-in-differences: measure how much the treated group changed, measure how much the untreated group changed over the same period, and subtract. It carries one assumption that does all the work, parallel trends: without the treatment, the two groups would have moved together. Everything below is about why that assumption can sometimes be believed and when it cannot.

How It Works

Start with the two comparisons that come to hand and why each fails. Compare treated and untreated groups after the fact, and any difference may simply be a difference the groups already had: Day 18’s confounders. Compare the treated group before and after, and anything else that changed over the same stretch is credited to the treatment: a trend, a bad season, a recession, or Day 19’s regression to the mean if the group was picked because it looked extreme. Each fails for a different reason, and the method below combines them so that each covers the other’s weakness.

First, the sorting. What makes an event a natural experiment is that whatever decided who got treated must be unrelated to the outcome except through the treatment. This is called as-if random assignment, and it is a claim to defend, not a fact you are handed. Snow defended it the way it is still defended. He went to see how the sorting worked: the rival companies had laid mains down the same streets, and each house’s choice had been made long before, by an owner or occupier, when the two companies were competing for customers, for reasons with no evident connection to who would later catch cholera. He then looked at what he could see, writing that there was no difference either in the condition or occupation of the persons receiving the water of the different companies. It is not literally random, since people did choose, once. The argument is that the choice was unrelated to anything that would later matter. No fewer than 300,000 people, he wrote, had been divided into two groups “without their choice, and, in most cases, without their knowledge,” and the experiment, in his words, was “on the grandest scale.”

Second, the “before.” Snow also had the 1849 epidemic, when both companies drew polluted water from the tidal Thames (Lambeth’s intake was then near Hungerford Market, in central London). In outline, the South London districts that Lambeth would later supply fared no better in 1849 than those served by Southwark and Vauxhall. By 1854, after Lambeth moved its intake, its customers were dying at a fraction of the other company’s rate. This is a primitive version of what economists now call difference-in-differences. The groups did not have to start level. What matters is how each group changed.

Why subtract? Between any two periods, many things move a whole population at once: weather, the economy, how deaths are recorded. They show up in the untreated group’s change and in the treated group’s change alike, so they cancel when you subtract one from the other. What survives is whatever happened to one group and not the other. If the only such thing is the treatment, what survives is its effect. The untreated group is working as a stand-in for the counterfactual: what would have happened to the treated group had it not been treated. Nobody can observe that, so the stand-in is an assumption. That assumption is parallel trends.

Parallel trends cannot be tested in the period that matters, because the treated group’s untreated future never happens. What you can do is look at the periods before the treatment. If the two groups moved together across several earlier years, the assumption gains credibility. Those earlier movements are the pre-trend, and a parallel pre-trend is reassuring but not proof: groups that moved together in the past can part ways at the very moment of treatment.

So the work of reading a difference-in-differences study is the work of hunting for reasons the groups would not have moved together. Five come up again and again. The groups may have been on different paths already. A second event may have struck only the treated group at the same time. Treatment may have gone where the outcome was expected to change, so that choice, not chance, did the sorting. Places picked because they looked extreme may drift back toward ordinary by themselves. And the untreated group may be touched by the treatment through spillover, as when drivers cross a state line. Any one of these turns the comparison group into a poor stand-in, and the estimate then measures the stand-in’s failure as well as the treatment.

That is why a natural experiment is best read as an argument. It borrows the logic of a trial without a trial’s guarantee: the coin toss of Day 20 made the groups comparable by design, and here the case for comparability has to be built from how the world happened to sort people. Sometimes the case is strong, as in Snow’s South London. Often it is merely plausible. The skill is telling them apart.

Two limits are worth holding on to. First, parallel trends is a claim on a particular scale. If both groups’ death rates would have fallen by the same percentage rather than by the same number, subtracting raw numbers misstates the effect, so the choice of scale is part of the assumption. Second, the answer is an effect on the treated group, in that place and at that time. It says what happened to those who were treated; whether the same would happen elsewhere is a further question the design does not answer.

Back to the story

That is how a comparison of households Snow had not chosen could carry so much weight. The pipes, not the families, had decided who drank which water, and the decision had been made years earlier for reasons with no evident link to cholera. Snow reported that the two sets of houses did not differ in condition or occupation, and he found 315 deaths per 10,000 houses on one company’s supply against 37 on the other’s, more than eight times as many. The 1849 epidemic supplied the “before”: when both companies took polluted water, the districts Lambeth would later serve had fared no better than Southwark and Vauxhall’s, and after Lambeth moved upriver its customers did far better. Nobody flipped a coin, but the world had come close enough that Snow’s comparison is still a classic case of the design. It carried weight because the sorting was well understood and could be checked, not because of any elaborate statistical model.

Key Concepts

Natural experiment
A situation in which something outside the researcher’s control (nature, history, a law) sorts people into treated and untreated groups in a way that approximates random assignment.
As-if random assignment
The claim that who got treated was unrelated to anything else that affects the outcome. It cannot be proved, only argued from how the sorting worked and checked against what can be observed.
Counterfactual
What would have happened to the treated group had it not been treated. It is never observed, so every causal estimate substitutes something for it.
Difference-in-differences
The treated group’s change over time minus the untreated group’s change over the same time. Subtracting cancels whatever moved both groups alike.
Parallel-trends assumption
Without the treatment, the two groups would have changed by the same amount. Their levels may differ; only their changes must match.
Pre-trend
How the two groups moved in the periods before the treatment. A run of parallel movement makes the parallel-trends assumption more believable, though never certain.

Example: Repealing the 55-mph Limit, State by State

Day 8 told how the national 55-mph limit was imposed in 1974 to save fuel. The other half of that story is its end. Congress repealed the national maximum speed limit in the National Highway System Designation Act, signed on 28 November 1995, and handed the choice back to the states, which then raised their limits at different times and by different amounts. This sits at the regulation link of the car chain, and the staggered timing is what gives a researcher something to compare.

In 2009 Lee Friedman, Donald Hedeker and Elihu Richter published such a comparison in the American Journal of Public Health. They compared states and road types over 1995 to 2005, setting changes where and when limits rose against changes where they had not. They attributed 3.2% more road deaths to the higher limits, an estimated 12,545 deaths over the decade. Debated

Worked example: the arithmetic
  1. Invented numbers, not the study’s. Deaths per 100,000 people: states that raised their limit went from 15 to 16, and states that did not went from 18 to 16.
  2. Treated states, before to after: 16 − 15 = +1. Looking only here, the higher limit seems to cost one death per 100,000.
  3. After only, treated against untreated: 16 − 16 = 0. Looking only here, it seems to cost nothing.
  4. Difference-in-differences: (16 − 15) − (16 − 18) = 1 − (−2) = +3. Deaths fell by 2 in the comparison states. If the treated states would have fallen by the same 2, the higher limit left them 3 above where they would otherwise have been.
  5. That +3 is real only if the treated states would also have fallen by 2. Their different starting levels do not matter; their different trend would.

Whether the real study meets that standard depends on why states raised limits when they did. If timing followed legislative calendars, it looks close to arbitrary. If limits rose first where highways are long, straight and empty, the groups were already on different paths, and no arithmetic repairs that.

Equation / Model

effect ≈ (Tafter − Tbefore) − (Cafter − Cbefore)

The effect is approximately the treated group's outcome after minus its outcome before, minus the quantity of the comparison group's outcome after minus its outcome before.

effect
the estimated causal effect of the treatment on the treated group
Tafter, Tbefore
the treated group’s average outcome after and before the treatment
Cafter, Cbefore
the comparison (untreated) group’s average outcome after and before

The decisive step is why the subtraction isolates the treatment. Write each group’s change as a shared shock plus, for the treated group, the treatment’s effect:

(Tafter − Tbefore) = s + τ    (Cafter − Cbefore) = s  ⇒  (s + τ) − s = τ

The treated group's change equals s plus tau. The comparison group's change equals s. Subtracting, s plus tau minus s equals tau.

s
the shock that would have hit both groups alike, such as a national trend; the parallel-trends assumption is exactly that s is the same for both
τ
the treatment’s effect on the treated group, which is what remains after s cancels

Why It Matters

Most questions in law, regulation and policy cannot be settled by a randomized trial. Governments rarely randomize minimum wages, speed limits or school-funding formulas across cities. So much of the credible evidence about policy has to come from natural experiments, and difference-in-differences is among their workhorses. In 2021 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences gave the Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel to David Card, for his empirical contributions to labour economics, and to Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens, for their methodological contributions to the analysis of causal relationships.

What you gain is three questions to put to any claim that a policy caused an outcome. What sorted people into treatment, and could that sorting be related to the outcome? What is the comparison group standing in for? And what, besides a hope, says the two would have moved together? A narrow confidence interval cannot rescue a failed parallel-trends assumption: that is Day 17’s systematic error, which no amount of data shrinks. An estimate can be precise and still answer a question nobody asked.

Settled. Snow’s counts, and the logic that subtracting a comparison group’s change cancels whatever moved both groups alike.

Debated. Whether a given natural experiment is truly as-if random. Friedman and colleagues put the 1995 repeal at 12,545 extra deaths; Lave and Elias (1994) argued that the 1987 rise to 65 mph on rural interstates lowered statewide fatality rates by 3.4% to 5.1%, as drivers moved to safer roads and police to other work. The periods and roads differ.

Value judgment. Whether faster travel is worth an estimated loss of life, and who should bear that risk, is something no estimate decides.

Connections

Back. Day 20, Randomized controlled trials: a coin toss makes the groups alike on average in everything, measured or not, while a natural experiment must earn that property from an accident of history and argue for it. Day 18, Correlation, confounding and Simpson’s paradox: difference-in-differences cancels confounders that are fixed or shared by both groups, such as wealth, geography or a national trend, but not one whose trend differs between them, which is the form Day 18’s lurking variable takes here.

Across. In Biology, Medicine & Health, the Dutch Hunger Winter of 1944–45 is a natural experiment in prenatal development that no one could ethically run. A German blockade cut rations in the western Netherlands to starvation levels over the winter, with sharp start and end dates and unaffected regions nearby, so exposure was set by when and where a pregnancy fell, not by choice. Ravelli, Stein and Susser (New England Journal of Medicine, 1976) found higher obesity rates at age 19 among men exposed early in gestation, and Heijmans and colleagues (PNAS, 2008) found epigenetic differences about six decades later. The shared mechanism is sorting by an accident of history. It strains in one respect: the famine struck one urban region, and a famine can change who is conceived and who survives, so researchers must ask whether the exposed group is representative of anyone else.

Remember These
  • A natural experiment is an accident of nature, history or policy that sorts people into treated and untreated groups as if at random; the “as if” must be argued.
  • Difference-in-differences subtracts the untreated group’s change from the treated group’s, cancelling whatever moved both alike.
  • Everything rests on parallel trends: without the treatment the groups would have moved together, though their levels may differ.
  • Snow’s tangled pipes put 315 cholera deaths per 10,000 houses on one company’s water against 37 on the other’s, in houses he reported alike in condition and occupation.
  • Pre-existing trend differences, a second event, choice into treatment, regression to the mean or spillover break the comparison, and a narrow confidence interval cannot repair them.
