The Program That Could Never Beat a Single Opponent Won the Tournament. Twice.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

In Robert Axelrod’s two computer tournaments, reported in 1980, a strategy that could never outscore any partner won on total points, twice, because in a game where both sides can gain, beating the other player is the wrong target.

Mathematics

Day 14 of 293 · Mathematics 14 of 24 · Game theory: strategy when others choose too

A tournament won by one of the simplest programs

In 1980 Robert Axelrod, a political scientist at the University of Michigan, published the results of a contest among computer programs. He had invited game theorists from economics, psychology, sociology, political science and mathematics to write a program that would play the same simple game against every other entry, 200 moves per match. On each move a program chose to cooperate or to defect, and the points followed a fixed table. If both cooperated, each scored 3. If both defected, each scored 1. If one defected on a cooperator, the defector took 5 and the cooperator got nothing. Fourteen programs arrived, and Axelrod added a fifteenth that chose at random.

The winner was among the simplest of the fourteen. Called TIT FOR TAT, it was submitted by Anatol Rapoport of the University of Toronto, and it did only two things: cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever the other program did on the move before.

That simplicity hides something strange. Because it only ever copies, TIT FOR TAT can never score more than the program it is playing. Against a program that cooperates on every move it ties, 600 points each. Against one that defects on every move it loses, 199 to 204. At best it ties, and it never wins a match.

Axelrod then ran a second round and let every entrant study the first round’s results before writing a program. Sixty-two entries came in from six countries. Rapoport sent TIT FOR TAT again, unchanged. It won again.

A program that cannot outscore a single partner beat a field built by people who had read how it won. How can a program that never wins a match win the whole tournament?

The Big Idea

When your payoff depends on what others choose as well as on what you choose, the stable outcome is an equilibrium: a set of choices in which no one gains by changing course alone. In the prisoner’s dilemma that equilibrium leaves everyone worse off, unless the game repeats often enough for reciprocity to pay.

Game theory is the mathematics of that situation: two or more decision-makers, each with options, each caring about an outcome that all of them together determine. Ordinary optimization treats the world as fixed terrain. Here the terrain is other people’s choices, and they are reading yours.

The prisoner’s dilemma is the sharpest case. Each player does better by defecting whatever the other does, yet two players who both reason this way do worse than two who both cooperate. What rescues cooperation is not kindness but repetition. If you will meet again, today’s defection can be answered tomorrow, and the prospect of that answer can make cooperating the better bet.

How It Works

A game, in the mathematician’s sense, has three parts: the players, the choices open to each, and a payoff to each player for every combination of choices. With two players and two choices you can write all of it as a payoff matrix. This one is Axelrod’s, with the choices called cooperate and defect.

One round: your points, then the other player’s.
They cooperateThey defect
You cooperate3, 30, 5
You defect5, 01, 1

Now play one round against someone you will never meet again, and reason by cases. If the other player cooperates, you score 3 by cooperating and 5 by defecting, so defecting is better. If the other player defects, you score 0 by cooperating and 1 by defecting, so defecting is better again. There are no other cases, so defecting is better whatever the other player does. A choice that wins in every case is a dominant strategy. This is a small proof in the sense of Day 10: not a sample of cases but all of them, and there are only two. The other player faces the same table and the same argument. Both defect, both score 1, and the 3 each that mutual cooperation would have paid is left on the table. Each player’s reasoning is flawless, and the result is worse for both.

That outcome has a name. A Nash equilibrium is a set of choices in which no player can do better by changing only their own choice while the others stay put. Mutual defection is one: if you switch to cooperating while the other defects, you drop from 1 to 0. Mutual cooperation is not one: while the other cooperates, you gain by switching to defection, from 3 to 5. John Nash defined the idea in a short paper in 1950. The dilemma itself was devised the same year, as an experiment on his equilibrium, by Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation. Note what an equilibrium claims: stability, not goodness.

Everything changes if the same two players meet again. Suppose that after each round there is a probability w that another round follows. Axelrod’s phrase for how much tomorrow weighs against today is the shadow of the future. A move now is also a message about the next one: a player can punish a defection or reward a cooperation, and can let its choice depend on the whole history. What a later round is worth today is an expected value in Day 11’s sense, its payoff times the chance that it is played. Cooperating on every round is therefore worth 3 + 3w + 3w² + … = 3/(1 − w) points. With w = 0.9 the game lasts ten rounds on average, and that stream of cooperation is worth 30.

TIT FOR TAT is a strategy built for this setting: cooperate first, then copy the other player’s last move. Try to profit from it at w = 0.9. Defecting every round earns 5 once, then 1 per round: 14 in all, against 30. Defecting once and then cooperating again earns 29.3, still short of 30, because the 2 extra points you took in the first round cost you 3 in the next, and that 3 is worth 2.7 today. Defection stops paying once the future is heavy enough. The exact condition is in the Equation / Model section; for these payoffs TIT FOR TAT holds against every challenger only if w is at least 2/3, a game that lasts three rounds on average.

Axelrod credited its success to four traits. It is nice: it is never the first to defect. It is retaliatory: it answers a defection at once. It is forgiving: it returns to cooperation as soon as the other player does, because it remembers only one move. And it is clear: the other side can see what it is doing and learn that cooperating pays. The high scorers in the first round were mostly nice programs. A program that defects first and keeps defecting collects 5 once and then, against any retaliator, about 1 a move, while a pair that settles into cooperation earns 3 a move.

TIT FOR TAT is not the best strategy everywhere, and the reason lies in how it works. It answers every defection with a defection, so one mistake echoes. Suppose each move has some chance of going wrong, so that an intended cooperation comes out as a defection or the reverse. Two TIT FOR TATs fall out of step: one accidental defection is answered, the answer is answered back, and they trade defections until another error changes the pattern. Work through the four states a pair can be in and, however rare the errors, they spend a quarter of their time in each, so in the long run they average (3 + 0 + 5 + 1) ÷ 4 = 2.25 points a move instead of 3. A version that forgives a defection one time in three, called generous TIT FOR TAT, does far better: with errors on 5% of moves, two of them average about 2.86. Nowak and Sigmund’s simulations (Nature, 1993) found that another rule, win-stay, lose-shift, which repeats its move after a good payoff and switches after a bad one, beats TIT FOR TAT in a noisy population. Wu and Axelrod (1995), testing strategies in a noisy tournament, found that generosity and contrition both help TIT FOR TAT cope; a contrite player accepts the other side’s retaliation for its own mistaken defection instead of answering it.

Forgiveness has a price too. In the second round TIT FOR TWO TATS, which waits for two defections before retaliating, did worse than TIT FOR TAT because other programs exploited its patience. How much to forgive depends on who else is playing and how noisy the world is. Even the tournament result is less general than it sounds: a 2015 re-analysis by Amnon Rapoport, Darryl Seale and Andrew Colman argues that TIT FOR TAT’s success depends on the design of the tournament, the criterion for success and the payoff values chosen. Debated

Back to the story

Here is the answer to the opening puzzle. The tournament counted total points, not matches won, and the game is not zero-sum: one player’s gain is not the other’s loss, so both can score 3 a move. Because TIT FOR TAT copies, the gap between it and its partner is only ever 0 or 5 points. A partner that defects first leads by 5 until it next cooperates, when TIT FOR TAT’s copied defection hands the 5 back. So TIT FOR TAT never finishes a match ahead, but it earns the full 600 against a partner that cooperates throughout and trails by at most 5 points against any partner that does not. Programs built to win matches had to get ahead by defecting on a partner that cooperated, and against a retaliator that drags both players toward 1 a move. TIT FOR TAT did not beat its partners. It collected the cooperative payoff from every partner that would allow it, and that was enough to win.

Key Concepts

Payoff matrix
A table of what each player scores for every combination of choices; in a two-player game the rows are one player’s options and the columns the other’s.
Dominant strategy
A choice that pays more than any alternative whatever the other players do. In the one-round prisoner’s dilemma, defecting.
Nash equilibrium
A set of choices in which no player can gain by changing only their own. It is a test of stability, not of whether the outcome is good.
Prisoner’s dilemma
A game in which each player gains by defecting whatever the other does, yet both do better if both cooperate.
Repeated game and the shadow of the future
The same game played again, each time with probability w of another round. The larger w is, the more tomorrow weighs and the more cooperation can be sustained.
TIT FOR TAT (reciprocity)
Cooperate on the first move, then copy the partner’s last move: cooperation is answered with cooperation and defection with defection.

Example: The SUV Arms Race

The same structure shows up in the cars people buy. In “The ‘Arms Race’ on American Roads” (Journal of Law and Economics, 2004), Michelle J. White used US crash data and found that SUVs and pickups protect their own occupants but raise the risk to everyone they hit. For each fatal crash avoided by the occupants of an SUV or pickup, she estimated, at least 4.3 more fatal crashes involving other people occur. That is one study’s estimate, not a consensus number. In your car chain this is the consequences link, produced by choices made at the adoption link: millions of households each buying the vehicle that is safest for them.

Worked example: the vehicle choice as a payoff matrix
  1. Set the scale. Take White’s ratio, rounded down to 1 to 4, as a stylized scale, and assume effects simply add. Let Heavy stand for an SUV or pickup and Light for a car. Choosing Heavy instead of Light makes your household +1 safer and the other household 4 less safe.
  2. Both Light: 0 for each.
  3. You Heavy, they Light: you +1, they −4. Both Heavy: each gets +1 − 4 = −3.
  4. Test for dominance. If they go Light, Heavy gives you +1 against 0. If they go Heavy, Heavy gives you −3 against −4. Heavy wins in both cases.
  5. Read the result. Both choose Heavy and each ends at −3, while (0, 0) was available. The payoffs, in Axelrod’s order, are T = +1, R = 0, P = −3 and S = −4: it is a prisoner’s dilemma.

The analogy bends in two places. Buyers also want space, towing power or a high seat, so safety is not their only payoff. And the game is not repeated in the way that rescues cooperation: millions of strangers cannot answer one buyer’s choice with a choice of their own, so there is no shadow of the future. When there is none, the remedy is more likely to come from outside the game, as a rule or charge that changes the payoffs. In the chain, that is the regulation link.

Equation / Model

T > R > P > S  and  2R > T + S

T is greater than R, which is greater than P, which is greater than S; and twice R is greater than T plus S.

T
temptation: what you score for defecting on a cooperator (5)
R
reward: what each scores when both cooperate (3)
P
punishment: what each scores when both defect (1)
S
sucker’s payoff: what you score for cooperating with a defector (0)

The first chain makes defecting dominant in one round: T > R beats a cooperator, and P > S beats a defector. The second keeps mutual cooperation better than taking turns exploiting each other, since (T + S)/2 = 2.5 is less than R = 3. In a repeated game, let w be the chance of another round. The decisive step is to compare what each challenger earns against TIT FOR TAT with what TIT FOR TAT earns against itself:

R/(1 − w) ≥ T + wP/(1 − w)  ⇒  w ≥ (T − R)/(T − P)

R over 1 minus w is at least T plus w P over 1 minus w, which gives w at least T minus R over T minus P.

R/(1 − w) ≥ (T + wS)/(1 − w²)  ⇒  w ≥ (T − R)/(R − S)

R over 1 minus w is at least T plus w S over 1 minus w squared, which gives w at least T minus R over R minus S.

w
the probability that another round follows, so the game lasts 1/(1 − w) rounds on average
R/(1 − w)
what TIT FOR TAT earns against itself: R + wR + w²R + …, a geometric sum
T + wP/(1 − w)
what a player who defects on every round earns against TIT FOR TAT: T once, then P thereafter
(T + wS)/(1 − w²)
what a player who alternates defect, cooperate earns against it: T, then S, repeating every two rounds

The first line comes from multiplying through by (1 − w), which leaves R ≥ T(1 − w) + wP. The second comes from cancelling (1 − w), which leaves R(1 + w) ≥ T + wS. With T = 5, R = 3, P = 1 and S = 0 the thresholds are 2/4 = 0.5 and 2/3. TIT FOR TAT must clear both, so the larger one binds: w ≥ 2/3. No other challenger raises the bar, which is Axelrod’s result that no challenger beats TIT FOR TAT’s score against itself exactly when w is large enough. The alternating challenger binds because it gives up only half a point a round (2.5 against 3) in exchange for a head start of 2 points, so it takes a longer future for the loss to outweigh the gain.

Why It Matters

When a group is stuck in an outcome nobody likes, you can now ask whether it is a dilemma, a game where each member gains by defecting whatever the others do, and then ask what the mathematics says can change it. Three things can: lengthen the shadow of the future so the same parties expect to meet again, change the payoffs so that defecting costs more or cooperating pays more, or make behavior visible so that a defection can be answered. None of these asks anyone to become more generous; each changes what defecting costs or what cooperating returns. The popular summary that nice guys finish first overreaches. TIT FOR TAT finishes first only among enough reciprocators, with a long enough shadow, and it never finishes ahead of anyone it plays.

Settled. The one-round equilibrium and the conditions for cooperation in repeated play are mathematical results once the payoffs are fixed, and the tournament scores are published records.

Debated. How far the tournaments generalize: whether noise, payoff values and the mix of rivals favor TIT FOR TAT or a more forgiving rival, and how much real-world cooperation the model explains. The SUV ratio is one study’s estimate.

Value judgment. Whether the cooperation a model sustains is good depends on who sits outside the game. The mathematics explains why cooperation lasts, not whether it deserves to.

Across. History. In Tony Ashworth’s Trench Warfare 1914–1918: The Live and Let Live System (1980), drawn from soldiers’ diaries, letters and unit records, opposing infantry on quiet sectors of the Western Front held back their fire. Axelrod analyzed it as a repeated prisoner’s dilemma in chapter 4 of The Evolution of Cooperation (1984). The mechanism is this lesson’s: the same small units faced each other for long stretches, a long shadow of the future, and a breach could be answered. The system was fragile, and Axelrod follows Ashworth in tracing its end to raids ordered from above. It is a model fitted to a historical record, so it shows the mechanism at work rather than proving how the soldiers reasoned.

Remember These
  • In a one-round prisoner’s dilemma defecting is dominant, so mutual defection is the only equilibrium even though mutual cooperation pays both more.
  • An equilibrium means no one gains by changing course alone; it says nothing about whether the outcome is good.
  • With Axelrod’s payoffs, TIT FOR TAT resists every challenger only if the chance of another round is at least 2/3.
  • TIT FOR TAT never outscored a partner yet won both tournaments, because the game is not zero-sum and the score was total points.
  • It is not best everywhere: noise makes mistakes echo, and how much to forgive depends on who else is playing.
Apply It

Each member of a cartel like OPEC would earn more by quietly pumping above its quota while the other members hold back, so what could keep such a cartel, whose members all depend on a high price, from collapsing into everyone overproducing?
