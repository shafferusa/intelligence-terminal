Volkswagen’s Diesels Passed the Official Emissions Test. On the Road They Emitted Up to 40 Times the Nitrogen Oxide Limit.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

A measure tracks its goal only while nobody is optimizing against it: once the number becomes the target, effort flows to the gap between the number and the thing it stood for — through a fraudulent software switch in a diesel engine, or a perfectly legal quirk of a laboratory test.

Mathematics

Day 23 of 293 · Mathematics 23 of 24 · How We Know IX: Goodhart’s law

On the road, not on the rollers

In 2013 a small team at West Virginia University’s Center for Alternative Fuels, Engines and Emissions took a modest contract from a nonprofit, the International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT). The ICCT’s researchers expected the work to show that diesel cars sold in the United States, held to tighter limits than Europe’s on nitrogen oxides (NOx, exhaust gases that irritate the lungs and form smog), ran clean. The West Virginia engineers fitted three diesels — a Volkswagen Jetta, a Volkswagen Passat and a BMW X5 — with portable emissions analysers and drove them on public roads, mostly in California.

The BMW largely met the US limit. The Volkswagens did not. On the road the Jetta emitted nitrogen oxides at roughly 15 to 35 times the limit and the Passat at roughly 5 to 20 times. Yet the same cars had met the limit on the official laboratory test, where a car sits on rollers and follows a prescribed speed schedule. The team’s report came out in May 2014. Volkswagen’s first explanation was technical problems. On 3 September 2015, after the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and California’s Air Resources Board made clear they would not approve its 2016 diesels, the company admitted to regulators that its cars carried a “defeat device”: software built to detect a test.

On 18 September the EPA issued a notice of violation covering about 482,000 diesel cars sold in the United States, model years 2009 to 2015. The software, it said, recognized the official test and switched on full emissions controls only then; on the road the cars emitted up to 40 times the limit. Four days later Volkswagen said that about 11 million cars worldwide carried the software. The day after that, its chief executive, Martin Winterkorn, resigned.

These cars passed the official tests. How can a car be clean exactly when it is measured and many times dirtier the rest of the time — and why had a test meant to stand in for ordinary driving become so easy to tell apart from it?

The Big Idea

When a measure becomes a target, people optimize the number rather than the thing it was meant to indicate, so the measure drifts away from the reality it once tracked and can even move against it. This is Goodhart’s law.

Nearly every measure is a proxy: a stand-in for something we care about but cannot observe directly or cheaply. A nitrogen-oxide test on rollers stands in for pollution on every road a car will ever drive; a waiting-time statistic stands in for how quickly patients are actually seen. A proxy captures only part of what we want. While nobody has a stake in the number, the part it misses is harmless noise and the proxy tracks the goal well. Once rewards and penalties hang on the number, effort goes wherever it moves the number most cheaply — and that is often the gap between the number and the goal, not the goal itself.

Pressure finds the gap in three ways: legal gaming, where the rules of the measurement are bent but not broken; distortion, where the unmeasured parts of the work are neglected; and outright fraud. Volkswagen’s defeat device sits at the fraudulent extreme, but the same pressure works through the milder forms too.

How It Works

The idea has an economist’s name, a social scientist’s name and a popular wording. In 1975 the British economist Charles Goodhart, writing about British monetary policy, observed: “Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.” The setting was the authorities’ attempt to steer the economy by controlling a measure of the money stock: once they leaned on it as a control variable, its statistical link to nominal income (total spending in the economy) tended to give way. The name “Goodhart’s law” came later, from others, and the wording most people quote — “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure” — is the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern’s, from a 1997 paper on auditing in British universities. The American social scientist Donald Campbell described the same pattern for social indicators in a 1979 paper: the more an indicator is used to make decisions, the more it invites corruption and the more it distorts the social processes it was meant to monitor. That is Campbell’s law.

Why would a regularity collapse? Because a proxy and its goal agree for a reason, and pressure removes the reason. Before anyone is rewarded for the number, it moves only as a by-product of the goal: a cleaner engine really does produce lower readings. That is a correlation (Day 18), and it holds because the goal pushes the number. Reward the number and you hand people a second way to push it, one that does not run through the goal. Write the measure as M, the goal as G, and everything else that can move the measure as the gap: M = G + gap. Before the target the gap is small random noise, so M tracks G. Afterwards the gap is something to be pushed.

The gap usually wins because of the arithmetic of optimization (Day 8). Anyone, or anything, scored on M goes wherever M is highest per unit of effort. Improving the goal is usually slow and costly: building a cleaner engine, adding ambulance crews. Moving the gap — tuning for the test, recording a time favourably, redefining what counts — is usually cheaper. If effort is limited, every hour spent on the gap is an hour not spent on the goal, so the measure can climb while the goal falls.

Gaming has a mild end and a harsh end. At the mild end is the habit every teacher knows, teaching to the test, shaping the work to the known form of the measurement. At the harsh end is the agent that recognizes the test itself. What makes a test easy to recognize is predictability. The official test is a fixed script: the car sits on rollers, follows a published speed-versus-time schedule, and its steering wheel stays essentially still. In the EPA’s account, Volkswagen’s software monitored the steering wheel position, the car’s speed, how long the engine had been running and the barometric pressure — inputs that closely match the test — and when it judged that no test was under way it ran a separate road calibration that reduced the effectiveness of the emissions controls. Full exhaust cleaning is not free; it can cost fuel, performance or consumables, so a car that cleans fully only when tested gets the passing score without paying those costs on the road. A test is only as hard to game as it is hard to recognize.

Much gaming is not fraud. The ICCT tracked the gap between official and real-world fuel consumption of new cars in the European Union, drawing on several data sources. The gap — the share by which real fuel use exceeded the official figure — grew from under 10% around 2001 to about 40% by the mid-2010s. The ICCT traced the widening to two sources: manufacturers exploiting flexibilities in the test procedure, which are legal choices about how a car is prepared and tested, and technologies, such as stop-start, that give a bigger benefit in the test than on the road. In the ICCT’s account, the official figure was tuned until it stopped describing a typical driver’s car, without any need to break the rules.

The defences follow from the mechanism. Measure the outcome itself where you can, and from more than one angle where you cannot, because gaming one proxy tends to show in another — the Volkswagens passed on rollers and failed on the road. Make the measurement unpredictable: routes, times and conditions that cannot be rehearsed or recognized. Use several measures, so no single number carries all the reward. Keep human judgment in the loop, someone who can say “the number is up but the thing is not better,” at some cost in consistency. And look for gaming’s signature: a jump in a statistic exactly at a threshold, or a gap opening between two measures of the same thing.

Back to the story

The test had become the target, which answers both halves of the puzzle. The official cycle, in the United States and Europe alike, is a fixed, published speed schedule driven on rollers with the steering wheel still, so it is easy to tell apart from road driving and is exactly what software can learn to recognize; that is how a car can be clean precisely when it is measured. Once passing the test was what counted, engineering effort flowed into passing it rather than into clean driving. Volkswagen’s software was fraud, the extreme case; the legal drift in Europe’s fuel figures, which the ICCT traced to test flexibilities and test-favouring technologies, is the same pattern in milder form. Europe’s regulators later added a test in which a car is driven on public roads with a portable analyser — the kind of equipment the West Virginia team had used.

Key Concepts

Proxy (indicator)
A measurable stand-in for something you care about but cannot observe directly: a test result for pollution, a waiting time for access to care.
Goodhart’s law
Charles Goodhart’s 1975 observation that a statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes; popularly restated, in Marilyn Strathern’s 1997 wording, as “when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”
Campbell’s law
Donald Campbell’s version for social indicators: the more an indicator drives decisions, the more it invites corruption and distorts the process it monitors.
Gaming and distortion
Gaming is improving the number without improving the goal, legally or not; distortion is the damage to unmeasured parts of the work when effort shifts toward the measured.
Teaching to the test
Tuning behaviour to the known form of a measurement rather than to what it stands for. A defeat device is its extreme: software that recognizes the test and behaves differently only then.
Unpredictable real-world auditing
Checking the outcome itself under conditions the measured party cannot predict or rehearse, such as a road test with a portable analyser or a surprise inspection.

Example: England’s NHS Targets, Gamed and Partly Real at Once

In the 2000s the English government set a few national targets for the National Health Service, with consequences for the managers who missed them: at least 75% of life-threatening ambulance calls reached within eight minutes, emergency-department patients admitted, transferred or discharged within four hours, and access to a GP within 48 hours. In Logan’s chain this is regulation, and the feedback that follows it.

Gaming followed. Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood’s 2006 study of the English system (Public Administration) found ambulance response times bunched just under eight minutes, which suggests some responses just over the line were being recorded as on time. The 48-hour guarantee had its own distortion: GP practices refusing to book appointments more than 48 hours ahead, which made the guarantee easy to meet. A voter confronted Tony Blair with this on the BBC’s Question Time in April 2005.

Yet gaming is not the whole story. Carol Propper and colleagues (Journal of Public Economics, 2010) compared England with Scotland, which did not run the same system of targets and sanctions — a natural experiment of the kind Day 21 taught, which stands or falls on Scotland being a fair stand-in for what England would otherwise have done. They found that England’s waiting-time targets did shorten long hospital waits. A measure can be gamed and partly real at once; whether the targets did more good than harm overall is Debated.

1. State the rule. At least 75% of life-threatening calls must be reached within 8 minutes, or the trust counts as having missed its target.

2. Take a hypothetical trust. Of 1,000 such calls in a month, 740 are reached in time: 740 / 1,000 = 74.0%. It needs 750, so it is short by ten calls, one percentage point.

3. Price the two fixes. Making ten genuinely slow responses fast enough takes more crews or better placement, which is slow and costly. Recording ten responses that took a little over 8 minutes as 8 minutes costs almost nothing. By the toy model below, effort follows the cheap lever.

4. Read the signature. Genuine response times should thin out smoothly across the 8-minute line. A pile-up just under the line shows effort concentrated where the penalty switches on, which is the pattern Bevan and Hood report.

5. Draw the lesson. A pass-or-fail threshold turns a one-point shortfall into a cliff, so pressure lands on the ten calls nearest the line rather than on the other 990.

Equation / Model

M = G + gap

M equals G plus gap.

M
the measure: the test result, the recorded wait, the p-value
G
the goal: the thing the measure is meant to indicate
gap
everything that can move M without moving G, from innocent measurement noise to gaming

Before M is a target the gap is small noise, so M tracks G. A deliberately simple toy model shows how strongly pressure changes that. Suppose a person is paid r for each unit of M and can raise G by an amount g and the gap by an amount x, at rising costs of ½ag² and ½bx². Their net gain is r(g + x) − ½ag² − ½bx². The decisive step is Day 8’s: set each derivative to zero.

r − ag = 0  ⇒  g = r/a   and   r − bx = 0  ⇒  x = r/b

r minus a times g equals zero, which gives g equals r over a; and r minus b times x equals zero, which gives x equals r over b.

r
the reward per unit of the measure
g
real improvement in the goal
x
movement in the gap, which is gaming
a, b
how steeply the cost of real improvement (a) and of gaming (b) rises; a small number means cheap

The real share of the measured gain is g / (g + x) = b / (a + b). If gaming is three times cheaper (b = a/3), only one quarter of the gain is real. Doubling r doubles g and x alike: more pressure does not change the split, it enlarges the gap, and at r = 0 the gap stays at zero, as before the target. Real costs are not this tidy, and a defeat device is a fixed cost followed by nearly free gain, so the model shows direction, not magnitude.

Why It Matters

This turns “be careful with numbers” into questions you can put to any indicator: who is rewarded when it rises, what is the cheapest way to raise it, and is that the way anyone wants it raised? It also changes where you look for trouble — not at a statistic’s average level but at its edges: a jump exactly at a threshold, a gap between two measures of one thing, an improvement that arrives suspiciously fast.

It bears on your book. Progress is usually claimed through indicators — test scores, emissions, waiting times — and any of them can become less trustworthy once rewards are aimed at it. That does not make indicators useless, as the NHS evidence shows, but “the number improved” has to be backed by an independent check of the thing itself, and the claims that flatter the argument you want to make need that check most.

Settled. Volkswagen’s software was a defeat device, which the company admitted to regulators in September 2015, and the EU’s gap between official and real-world fuel use widened sharply, as the ICCT measured it.

Debated. The net effect of performance targets. Bevan and Hood documented gaming of NHS targets; Propper and colleagues found the waiting-time targets still cut long waits. Both can be true, and how to weigh them is argued.

Value judgment. How much gaming is an acceptable price for a measure that also drives real improvement, and who should bear the harm when it fails. Supporters of strong targets stress the gains; sceptics such as the historian Jerry Muller stress the distortions.

Connections

Back. Day 8, Optimization: an optimizer goes wherever the quantity it is scored on is best. Goodhart’s law is that idea pointed at a proxy, so the optimum slides to wherever proxy and goal part ways, and the toy model above is Day 8’s set-the-derivative-to-zero arithmetic with two levers instead of one. Day 22, Replication and publication bias: making p < 0.05 the ticket to publication turned significance into a target, and p-hacking, trying analyses until the number falls below the line, optimized the number rather than the truth it was meant to signal.

Across. AI. DeepMind researchers including Victoria Krakovna described “specification gaming,” an AI system satisfying the letter of its objective but not the intent, in an April 2020 blog post. In one example, from Popov and colleagues (2017), a simulated robot arm was rewarded for the height of the bottom face of a red Lego block, so that it would stack the red block on a blue one; it learned to flip the red block over instead, which raised the bottom face without any stacking. The structure matches Volkswagen’s: an optimizer finds the gap between the measured proxy and the goal. The analogy breaks at motive. Volkswagen’s engineers knew the intent and hid their departure from it; the robot had no hidden aim, and the fault lay in the objective its designers wrote.

Remember These
  • A measure is a proxy for a goal; it tracks the goal only while the gap between them is small noise and nobody is rewarded for pushing the number.
  • Once a measure is a target, effort flows to the cheaper lever, improving the goal or moving the gap, and the gap is usually cheaper (Goodhart’s law; Campbell’s law).
  • Gaming runs from legal tuning, like the EU fuel gap that grew from under 10% to about 40%, to fraud, like Volkswagen’s software.
  • A fixed, predictable test is easy to recognize; the defences are direct, unpredictable and multiple measures, with judgment kept in the loop.
  • A target can be gamed and still partly work, as England’s NHS targets were both.
