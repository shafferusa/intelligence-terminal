Berkeley’s Graduate Programs Admitted 44% of Men and 35% of Women in 1973. In Four of Its Six Biggest Departments, Women Were Admitted More Often.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

A pattern in combined data can be created, hidden or even reversed by a third variable, and the numbers alone cannot say whether to trust the combined table or the split one — that takes a story about what causes what.

Mathematics

Day 18 of 293 · Mathematics 18 of 24 · How We Know IV: Correlation, confounding and Simpson’s paradox

Autumn 1973: Berkeley counts who got in

In the fall of 1973 the University of California, Berkeley, tallied who had been admitted to its graduate programs, and one table was hard to look at. Of 8,442 men who applied, 3,738 were admitted: 44 percent. Of 4,321 women, 1,494: 35 percent. Treat each group as a random draw and apply the standard-error arithmetic of Day 16: a gap that size sits more than ten standard errors from zero, far too big to be luck.

Eugene Hammel, an anthropologist who was then associate dean of the Graduate Division, worried that the gap looked like discrimination. So with the statistician Peter Bickel and a colleague, J. W. O’Connell, he broke the figures down by department. That was the natural place to look: admission decisions were made by the departments themselves, so any discrimination would have to be happening inside particular departments.

The six largest departments took 2,691 male and 1,835 female applicants between them. Pooled, the gap there was even wider: 44.5 percent of the men admitted, 30.4 percent of the women. Then the team read the departments one at a time. In four of the six, women were admitted at the higher rate. Their paper, “Sex Bias in Graduate Admissions: Data from Berkeley,” appeared in Science in February 1975, and its conclusion was not what the first table suggested: if anything, a small bias in women’s favor.

Both readings came from the same admissions records. How can women be admitted less often overall and yet more often in four of the six biggest departments?

The Big Idea

A relationship in combined data can be manufactured, hidden or even turned upside down by a third variable that is tangled up with both things you are comparing. When the reversal is complete, so that a comparison points one way in the combined data and the other way inside the subgroups, it is called Simpson’s paradox.

Start with what correlation does and does not say. Two variables are correlated when they tend to move together. That describes a pattern; it does not explain it, and one common explanation is a third variable that pushes on both. Compare two groups that differ in that third variable and part of the gap you measure belongs to it rather than to what you meant to study. The remedy is to compare like with like: split the data by the third variable and compare within each slice. Sometimes the gap shrinks, sometimes it vanishes, sometimes it flips sign.

The flip is not a trick or a mistake. A combined rate is an average of the subgroup rates, and an average depends on how much weight each subgroup gets. The harder lesson is that the numbers cannot tell you whether to trust the combined table or the split one. That takes a causal story, a claim about what influences what — and sometimes the combined table is the honest one.

How It Works

Begin with the plainest case. Taller people tend to weigh more: the two measurements are correlated, meaning they tend to rise and fall together. The correlation coefficient, written r, puts a number on this, from +1 (a perfect straight-line rise together) through 0 (no straight-line tendency) to −1 (a perfect opposite movement). It measures how tight a pattern is. It says nothing about where the pattern comes from.

When X and Y are correlated, there are four basic explanations: X influences Y; Y influences X; chance, the subject of Day 17; or a third variable Z that influences both. The fourth is confounding, and Z is a confounder (many introductory texts also say lurking variable). Unlike chance, it does not fade as data accumulate. Like the sampling bias of Day 16, confounding does not shrink as the sample grows; a bigger sample just measures the wrong thing more precisely. The reason is that groups defined by X differ in more than X. If the people given treatment A are not the same sort of people as those given treatment B, the gap in outcomes mixes the treatment’s effect with the difference between the people.

The standard remedy is stratification: split the data into strata that are alike on Z, and compare within each. To report one summary number you then combine the strata using the same weights for both groups, a method called direct standardization; it is how an “age-adjusted” death rate is made. Both are ways of comparing like with like.

Now the paradox. A group’s overall rate is a weighted average of its rates in each subgroup, where each weight is the share of the group’s cases that fall in that subgroup. That is Day 11’s expected value: the probability of landing in a subgroup times the rate there, added up. If two groups have different weights, their overall rates can differ even when their subgroup rates are equal, and if the weights differ enough the order can reverse. Read the shares first. Departments A and B admitted about 64 percent of everyone who applied, and they received 51 percent of the men’s applications but only 7 percent of the women’s. Departments C to F admitted about 26 percent, and received 93 percent of the women’s applications against 49 percent of the men’s. Women did not meet a harder committee inside each department; more of them applied to departments where most applicants of either sex were turned away. To see the weights at work, re-weight the women’s department rates by the men’s shares of applications, so that the only thing that changes is where the applications go: 0.307×82.4 + 0.208×68.0 + 0.121×34.1 + 0.155×34.9 + 0.071×23.9 + 0.139×7.0 ≈ 25.3 + 14.1 + 4.1 + 5.4 + 1.7 + 1.0 = 51.6 percent. Had women applied the way men did, and each department’s rates held, their overall rate would have been 51.6 percent, above the men’s 44.5.

So which table do you believe? Neither is wrong; they answer different questions, and which question you are asking depends on a causal story. Draw the Berkeley story as a causal diagram, a picture with an arrow from each cause to each thing it influences: sex → department applied to → admission, and possibly sex → admission directly, which is what the university feared. Department sits on the path between sex and the outcome, so in the sharper vocabulary of causal diagrams it is a mediator rather than a confounder (a confounder is a common cause of the group and the outcome). The everyday statistical shorthand calls it a confounder or lurking variable because sex and department are tangled together in the data. If your question is “did departments treat comparable applicants differently?”, hold department fixed and read the split table. If it is “do women end up admitted less often?”, the combined number is simply true, and the explanation lies in the arrow from sex to department.

Compare a case where the split table is the one to trust: the kidney-stone comparison in Connections below, where stone size comes before the choice of operation and influences both that choice and the result. And compare Judea Pearl’s standard illustration of the opposite case: a drug that works by lowering blood pressure. Split patients by blood pressure measured after treatment and the drug can look useless, or even harmful, inside each group, because you have conditioned away the very path by which it works. The table of numbers can be identical to one where the splitting variable is a true confounder, and the right answer is the opposite. In 1981 Lindley and Novick argued that the table alone cannot tell you which case you are in. Pearl’s answer, in a 2014 article in The American Statistician, is that a causal diagram does. Debated Not every statistician accepts that this settles the matter: Timothy Armistead published a critique of Pearl’s analysis in the same volume of that journal.

Back to Berkeley

Women were not admitted less often inside most departments. They applied more heavily to departments that turned away most applicants of either sex: C to F took 93 percent of the women’s applications and admitted about 26 percent, while A and B, which admitted about 64 percent, took 51 percent of the men’s. The combined gap of about 14 points in the six departments was a difference in weights, not in treatment. The authors drew the conclusion the split table supports — if anything, a small bias in women’s favor — and then pushed the question upstream. Their data could show where the gap arose, not why women were applying where they did. They argued that the answer lay earlier, in what schooling and socialization had steered women toward: fields that were more crowded and less well funded. Removing one explanation had opened another.

Key Concepts

Correlation
A measure of how closely two variables move together (r runs from −1 to +1); silent on why.
Confounder
A third variable that influences both the supposed cause and the outcome, so that comparing groups mixes its effect with the cause’s. Also called a lurking variable.
Stratification
Splitting data into groups alike on a confounder and comparing within each; combining the groups with common weights is direct standardization.
Simpson’s paradox
A comparison that points one way in combined data and the other way within the subgroups. Udny Yule described the effect in 1903 and Edward Simpson in 1951; Colin Blyth gave it Simpson’s name in 1972.
Mediator
A variable on the causal path from cause to outcome; holding it fixed removes the part of the effect that flows through it.
Causal diagram
A picture of arrows running from causes to effects, used to tell confounders from mediators and so to decide which table answers your question.

Example: Driver Death Rates by Car Model

The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS), a research group funded by auto insurers, publishes driver deaths per million registered vehicle years for each make and model (one registered vehicle year is one vehicle registered for one year). In its report of July 30, 2026, covering 2023 models and earlier models of the same design, with deaths counted from 2021 through 2024, the Kia Rio had the highest rate, 170, followed by the Nissan Versa at 164. The all-vehicle average was 37, and eighteen of the twenty lowest-rate models were SUVs. In your car chain this is the consequences link: what the technology does once it is on the road.

Does the table measure the cars? Partly: a small, light car fares badly against a heavy pickup, and that is physics. But IIHS says differences in driver behavior most likely explain why rates are lower for ordinary cars than for sports and muscle cars, and who drives which model is not random. Driver age and sex are textbook confounders here: each plausibly influences both the model someone drives and how risky their driving is. IIHS says it adjusts its published rates for both. Adjustment can hold fixed only what is measured, though, and how far, how fast and where people drive is not fully captured, so some residual confounding remains: the rate belongs to a bundle of car, driver and road.

A toy version — invented round numbers

1. Two equally safe cars. In either car, drivers under 25 die at 100 per million registered vehicle years and drivers 25 and over at 20.

2. Different drivers. A muscle coupe’s registered vehicle years are 60% under-25 drivers and 40% older; a minivan’s are 10% and 90%.

3. Raw rates. Coupe: 0.6×100 + 0.4×20 = 68. Minivan: 0.1×100 + 0.9×20 = 28. The coupe looks 2.4 times as deadly (68/28), though the cars are identical by construction.

4. Standardize. Give both the same mix, 25% young and 75% older: 0.25×100 + 0.75×20 = 40 for each. The gap vanishes. In real data the within-group rates differ, and whatever difference survives is the car plus whatever the adjustment did not hold fixed.

Equation / Model

An overall rate is a weighted average of subgroup rates:

p = Σ wi pi

p equals the sum, over every subgroup i, of w sub i times p sub i.

p
a group’s overall rate, such as the share of its applicants admitted
i
labels the subgroups, here the departments A to F
wi
subgroup i’s share of the group’s cases: a weight between 0 and 1, the weights summing to 1
pi
the rate within subgroup i

A full Simpson reversal needs only that pW,i ≥ pM,i in every subgroup while pW < pM overall, which is possible whenever the weights differ enough. (Berkeley is a milder case: women ahead in four of six.) To see how much of a gap is weights and how much is rates, start from pW − pM = Σ wW,ipW,i − Σ wM,ipM,i, add and subtract Σ wW,ipM,i, and regroup:

pW − pM = Σ wW,i(pW,i − pM,i) + Σ (wW,i − wM,i) pM,i

The women's overall rate minus the men's overall rate equals the sum, over departments, of the women's weight times the difference between the women's and men's rates in that department, plus the sum, over departments, of the difference between the women's and men's weights times the men's rate in that department.

pW, pM
the women’s and men’s overall rates; pW,i and pM,i are their rates in department i
wW,i, wM,i
the share of women’s and of men’s applications that went to department i
first sum
the rate effect: differences in rates inside departments, weighted by the women’s mix
second sum
the mix effect: how differently the two groups’ applications are spread across departments, valued at the men’s rates

For the six departments, from the unrounded rates: −14.2 points = +0.1 (rate effect) − 14.3 (mix effect). Nearly all the gap is mix. The split depends on the mix you hold fixed: with the men’s mix instead, the within-department difference is +7.1 points (51.6% against 44.5%). The causal diagrams behind the two readings:

X ← Z → Y   versus   X → M → Y

X with an arrow into it from Z, and an arrow from Z into Y, versus X with an arrow into M, and an arrow from M into Y.

X
the group or treatment being compared (sex, the operation, the car model)
Y
the outcome (admission, success, a driver’s death)
Z
a confounder, a common cause of X and Y: split by it (stone size; driver age)
M
a mediator, on the path from X to Y: splitting by it removes the part of X’s effect that runs through it (department, in the question “do women end up admitted less often?”)

Why It Matters

You can now put a standing question to any comparison of rates between groups, in a news story, a study or your own book: are these groups alike in everything else that bears on the outcome, and if not, what is the mix? A hospital with a worse survival rate may simply admit sicker patients; a car model with a high driver death rate may attract riskier drivers; a country’s overall fatality rate can mislead when the ages of its cases differ. Hold the opposite trap just as firmly: “control for everything” is not a safe default. Adjusting for a mediator, or for something that is itself an effect of what you study, can hide a real effect or fake one, so every adjustment is a causal claim. A remedy that works even for confounders you never measured is Day 20’s randomized trial: assigning treatment by chance makes the groups alike on average in every respect, measured or not.

Settled. The arithmetic of Simpson’s paradox, and the Berkeley counts.

Debated. How completely causal diagrams settle which table to trust, and whether the departmental pattern at Berkeley reflected earlier steering of women, a question the 1975 authors raised and their data could not answer.

Value judgment. Whether fairness is judged at the admissions decision or along the whole path that leads to it is a moral question, which no table can settle.

Across. Biology, Medicine & Health: in a 1986 BMJ study Charig and colleagues compared treatments for kidney stones. Two groups, 350 patients each, had open surgery or percutaneous nephrolithotomy (removal through a small cut in the back). Open surgery succeeded in 273 (78%), the other operation in 289 (83%). Julious and Mullee used the data in a 1994 BMJ article, “Confounding and Simpson’s paradox”: split by stone size, open surgery did better for stones under 2 cm (93%, 81 of 87, against 87%, 234 of 270) and for stones of 2 cm or more (73%, 192 of 263, against 69%, 55 of 80). Same weighted average: three in four open-surgery patients had large stones, against fewer than one in four for the other operation (80 of 350). Unlike Berkeley, stone size comes before the choice of operation and affects the result, a true confounder, so the split table is the better guide.

Remember These
  • A correlation says two variables move together, not why; a third variable that affects both can create, hide or reverse it.
  • A combined rate is a weighted average of subgroup rates, so unequal weights can make the combined comparison point against the subgroups.
  • Berkeley’s graduate programs admitted 44% of men and 35% of women, yet women led in four of the six biggest departments, because more applied where most applicants were turned away.
  • Comparing like with like removes a confounder, but the data cannot say whether to pool or split; that takes a causal story.
  • Adjusting for what you can measure leaves what you cannot: even IIHS’s adjusted death rates still mix car, driver and road.

Early in the COVID-19 pandemic Italy’s overall case-fatality rate (deaths divided by confirmed cases) was higher than China’s even though, as von Kügelgen, Gresele and Schölkopf (2021) reported, it was lower within age groups, so what would have to differ between the two countries’ confirmed cases to produce that reversal?
