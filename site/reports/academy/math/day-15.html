An Expert Told the Jury the Odds Were 1 in 73 Million. He Had Answered the Wrong Question.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 14 min read

Bayes’ theorem says evidence moves a belief by the ratio of how likely it is under each rival explanation, applied to how plausible each was beforehand — the step a famous 1-in-73-million figure skipped.

Mathematics

Day 15 of 293 · Mathematics 15 of 24 · How We Know I: Bayes’ theorem and base rates

1 in 73 million at Chester Crown Court

On 9 November 1999, a jury at Chester Crown Court convicted Sally Clark, a solicitor from Wilmslow in Cheshire, of murdering her two baby sons, by a majority of ten to two. Christopher had died in 1996, aged eleven weeks; Harry in 1998, aged eight weeks. The prosecution called Professor Sir Roy Meadow, a leading paediatrician, as an expert witness.

Meadow told the court that in an affluent, non-smoking family like the Clarks’ the chance of one sudden infant death — a cot death — was about 1 in 8,543, a figure he took from a large study of infant deaths. Squaring it, he said, put the chance of two such deaths in one family at about 1 in 73 million. He made the number vivid: it was like backing an 80-to-1 outsider in the Grand National four years running and winning each time.

Clark was sentenced to life imprisonment. A first appeal failed in October 2000. In October 2001 the Royal Statistical Society said publicly that the 1-in-73-million figure had no statistical basis. In January 2003 the Court of Appeal quashed both convictions, chiefly because a pathologist had not disclosed microbiology results suggesting that Harry had a bacterial infection. By then she had spent more than three years in prison. She died in 2007.

Set the appeal aside and look only at the number the jury heard. If the innocent explanation had a chance of just 1 in 73 million, doesn’t that make guilt all but certain? What exactly is wrong with that reasoning?

The Big Idea

Bayes’ theorem says how much a piece of evidence should change what you believe: start from how plausible each explanation was before the evidence, then multiply by how much more probable the evidence is under one explanation than under its rival.

Two quantities do the work, and neither can be dropped. The first is the starting plausibility of each explanation, which statisticians call the prior, or the base rate when it comes from counting. The second is the likelihood ratio: how many times likelier the evidence is if one explanation is true than if its rival is. A positive medical test, a DNA match, a witness’s identification, an expert’s “one in a million”: each is strong evidence only to the extent that it would be far more likely if the suspicion were true than if it were false, and only against how common the suspicion was to begin with.

The story turns on one consequence. How rare an event is, taken alone, tells you almost nothing about what caused it. Two babies dying in one family is extraordinarily rare, whatever the cause, so both explanations on offer were extraordinarily rare. A rare event is strong evidence for an explanation only if it is much rarer under the rival.

How It Works

Start with a distinction that sounds trivial and is not. A conditional probability is the chance of one thing given that another is true, written P(A | B) and read “the probability of A given B.” Nearly every player in the NBA is taller than six feet, so the probability of being tall, given that you play in the NBA, is close to 1. Almost no six-footer plays in the NBA, so the probability of playing in the NBA, given that you are tall, is close to 0. The two conditional probabilities use the same two facts, run in opposite directions, and can differ enormously in size. Confusing one for the other is a classic error in reasoning about evidence.

Bayes’ theorem, named for Thomas Bayes, an eighteenth-century English minister whose essay on the problem was published after his death, is the rule for turning one direction into the other. Its most useful form compares two rival explanations, H1 and H2, in the light of some evidence E, and it works in odds, which are probabilities written as a ratio. A probability of 1% is odds of 1 to 99; odds of 1 to 9 are a probability of 1 in 10. The rule reads: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. The prior odds say how plausible H1 was against H2 before E arrived. The likelihood ratio says how many times more probable E is if H1 is true than if H2 is. The posterior odds are where you stand afterwards.

Three things follow at once. If the likelihood ratio is 1, the evidence is equally probable either way and your odds do not move. If it is 10, your odds are multiplied by ten — but ten times a very small prior is still small, which is why a positive test for a rare condition is mostly a false alarm (the Example below). And it is a ratio: the numerator alone, the chance of the evidence if one explanation is true, means nothing until you know the chance under the rival.

Now see what the 1-in-73-million figure was and was not. Taken at face value, it is a likelihood: how probable two cot deaths are if the family is innocent. The rival explanation makes the evidence almost certain — if a mother kills both babies, both babies die — so the likelihood ratio looks enormous, about 73 million to one. Stop there and guilt seems settled. But the rule has a second factor, the prior odds, and here the prior is also minuscule: before any baby has died, how likely is it that a given mother will murder two? A likelihood ratio of millions multiplied by prior odds of one in millions can land almost anywhere. The size of the two numbers relative to each other is the whole answer, and the court heard only one of them.

Counting whole families, a method known as natural frequencies, makes this concrete. Suppose, purely for illustration — invented round numbers, not data — that among ten million families with two babies, ten lose both to cot death and one loses both to murder. A family arrives in court having lost both babies. Eleven families in ten million fit that fact, and ten of the eleven are innocent. Each group is a rarity; what matters is the ratio between them, here ten to one in favour of innocence before any other evidence is weighed. The mathematician Ray Hill estimated in 2004 that, for a family with two unexplained baby deaths, double cot death was several times likelier than double murder Debated — an estimate of this kind depends on assumptions about how common each event is. Nothing here says Clark was probably innocent. It says the number the jury heard could not, by itself, say anything either way. Other evidence — the pathology, the withheld microbiology — would each be a further likelihood ratio, multiplying the odds again.

The figure also had a plainer flaw. Meadow reached 1 in 73 million by multiplying 1 in 8,543 by itself, and chances multiply like that only for independent events — the rule Day 11’s problem of points used for successive rounds of a game. Cot deaths are not independent within a family: genetic and household factors make a second death more likely after a first, so the squared figure was far too small. The Royal Statistical Society’s verdict was that it had no statistical basis, and the Court of Appeal criticised the statistical evidence as well as the withheld microbiology.

The error has a name. Mistaking P(evidence | innocent) — how likely the evidence is if the defendant is innocent — for P(innocent | evidence) — how likely the defendant is to be innocent given the evidence — is the prosecutor’s fallacy. Its mirror image, sometimes called the defence attorney’s fallacy, argues that because many people in a large population would also match a piece of evidence, the match proves nothing; it overlooks that the match still multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio.

One caution, aimed at the habit of treating a calculation as a verdict. Bayes’ theorem is a theorem: given the definition of conditional probability, its arithmetic is as certain as any proof (Day 10). What it cannot certify is its inputs. A prior is a judgment about how common something is, and a prior that already assumes guilt will hand guilt back. Enough strong evidence pulls people who began with different priors toward the same conclusion, but it takes more the further apart they began, and a prior of exactly zero can never be moved by anything. Much of the real argument in real cases lives in the priors.

Back to the story

So here is the answer to the puzzle. A 1-in-73-million chance of two cot deaths was never the chance that Sally Clark was innocent. At best it was one half of the calculation — the likelihood — and a flawed half, because squaring ignored that cot deaths cluster in families. The other half, the prior, was missing: mothers who murder two babies are extraordinarily rare too. The jury’s real task was to compare two very rare explanations, a double cot death and a double murder, and Hill’s estimate put the first several times likelier than the second. The number answered “how rare is this if she is innocent?” when the question was “which rare thing happened?”

Key Concepts

Prior probability (base rate)
How probable an explanation is, or how common a condition is, before the new evidence is considered. It is called the base rate when it comes from counting a population.
Likelihood ratio
How many times more probable the evidence is under one explanation than under its rival. A ratio near 1 means the evidence barely tells the two apart.
Posterior probability
The probability of an explanation after the evidence has been taken into account; in odds form, the prior odds multiplied by the likelihood ratio.
Conditional probability
The chance of one thing given another, P(A | B). In general P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers.
Prosecutor’s fallacy
Mistaking the probability of the evidence given innocence for the probability of innocence given the evidence.
Natural frequencies
Probabilities restated as counts of whole cases in a concrete group (“of 1,000 women, 10 have cancer”), which keeps the base rate in view and turns the arithmetic into counting.

Example: A Positive Mammogram, Counted in Whole Women

The same logic runs through medicine, where the rival explanations are “has the disease” and “does not,” and the evidence is a test result. Gerd Gigerenzer and colleagues (“Helping Doctors and Patients Make Sense of Health Statistics,” Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2007) use breast-cancer screening as a standard case. Gigerenzer reports a training session in which 160 gynecologists were given the facts as percentages — how common the disease is, how often the test is positive in women who have it, how often in women who do not — and asked what a positive result means. The most common answer was 90%; the right answer is close to one in ten, and only about one in five chose it. After a short training in natural frequencies, which means thinking in counts of women, most answered correctly.

Worked example: 1,000 women screened (teaching values)
  1. Start with 1,000 women. If 1% have breast cancer, 10 do and 990 do not.
  2. The test is positive for 90% of women who have the disease: 9 of the 10.
  3. It is also positive for 9% of women who do not: 9% of 990 is about 89.
  4. Count the positives: 9 + 89 = 98. Only 9 of those 98 have cancer — about 9%, close to one in ten.
  5. In odds form: prior odds 1 to 99; likelihood ratio 0.90 ÷ 0.09 = 10; posterior odds 10 to 99, a probability of 10/109, about 9%. Same answer by multiplication.

The test is good — it catches 9 cancers in 10 — and a positive result still probably means no cancer, because the 990 healthy women so outnumber the 10 sick ones. A tenfold likelihood ratio acting on a 1-in-100 prior reaches about 9%, not near-certainty. The doctors who answered 90% had most plausibly confused the two directions — “the test is positive in 90% of cancers” is not “90% of positives are cancers” — the prosecutor’s fallacy in a white coat. Real screening performance varies with age, test and programme; these are round values for teaching.

Equation / Model

P(H1 | E) / P(H2 | E)  =  [ P(E | H1) / P(E | H2) ] × [ P(H1) / P(H2) ]

The probability of H one given E, divided by the probability of H two given E, equals the quantity probability of E given H one divided by probability of E given H two, times the quantity probability of H one divided by probability of H two.

The decisive step is one division. Writing the probability of “H and E together” two ways gives P(H | E) P(E) = P(E | H) P(H), so P(H | E) = P(E | H) P(H) / P(E). Write that for H1 and for H2 and divide:

[ P(E | H1) P(H1) / P(E) ] ÷ [ P(E | H2) P(H2) / P(E) ]  =  P(E | H1) P(H1) / [ P(E | H2) P(H2) ]

Probability of E given H one times probability of H one, over probability of E, divided by probability of E given H two times probability of H two, over probability of E, equals probability of E given H one times probability of H one, over probability of E given H two times probability of H two.

Why It Matters

Once you see evidence as a ratio applied to a base rate, you can ask two questions of almost any striking claim: “compared with what?” and “how common was this before the evidence arrived?” A DNA match, a screening result, an algorithm’s alert, an expert’s “one in a million”: each is a likelihood that means little until it is set against a rival explanation and a prior. It also shows why independent pieces of evidence strengthen one another, since their likelihood ratios multiply, while a second look at the same evidence adds nothing. Day 17 (Significance, p-values and systematic error) takes up the p-value, which, like Meadow’s number, is a probability of the data given a hypothesis, not of the hypothesis given the data.

Whether juries should be walked through this arithmetic is Debated: in R v Adams (1996, with a second appeal in 1997), a rape case in which DNA evidence was set against other evidence, the Court of Appeal criticised the way Bayes’ theorem had been put to the jury.

Settled. Bayes’ theorem is a theorem, and mistaking the probability of the evidence given innocence for the probability of innocence is a recognised error (the Royal Statistical Society, 2001).

Debated. How probabilistic reasoning should be put to juries; which priors are defensible; and whether probability measures long-run frequencies or degrees of belief.

Value judgment. How much risk of convicting the innocent a society accepts in order to avoid freeing the guilty is a moral choice, and no formula makes it for you.

Remember These
  • Posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds; the base rate and the likelihood ratio are both indispensable.
  • A rare event is strong evidence for an explanation only if it is much rarer under the rival explanation.
  • P(evidence | innocent) is not P(innocent | evidence): confusing them is the prosecutor’s fallacy, and squaring the chances of two deaths assumes they are independent.
  • Counting whole cases makes base rates visible: in the mammography example, a positive result means cancer in about 9 of 98 cases.
  • The theorem is certain; the priors and likelihoods you feed it are judgments.
