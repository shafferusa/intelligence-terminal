The Literary Digest Counted 2.4 Million Ballots and Called the Wrong President. Gallup Asked Far Fewer People and Called the Winner.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 15 min read

A sample’s size controls only its random error, which shrinks with the square root of the number of people asked; who was asked, and who chose to answer, controls bias, and no number of respondents shrinks that.

Mathematics

Day 16 of 293 · Mathematics 16 of 24 · How We Know II: Sampling and the margin of error

The poll that counted 2.4 million ballots

By the autumn of 1936 the Literary Digest, a weekly magazine, had a record to protect. Its mail-in straw polls had called the winner of the presidential races of 1920, 1924, 1928 and 1932. That year it mailed about ten million mock ballots, drawing the addresses largely from telephone directories, automobile-registration lists and its own subscriber rolls. About 2.4 million came back.

In its issue of October 31 the Digest published the result. Alf Landon, the Republican governor of Kansas, would defeat President Franklin Roosevelt, 57 percent to 43 percent of the vote for the two major candidates. The number came with a long reputation behind it.

Three days later, on Tuesday, November 3, Americans voted. Roosevelt won about 61 percent of all votes cast — roughly 62.5 percent of the two-candidate vote the Digest had been trying to measure — and carried 46 of the 48 states. Landon took Maine and Vermont. The magazine had missed Roosevelt’s share by about twenty points, and in 1938 it was absorbed by Time.

George Gallup, a young pollster who ran the American Institute of Public Opinion from Princeton, New Jersey, had told his newspaper clients that Roosevelt would win. He had interviewed only a small fraction as many people. He had even said, months earlier, roughly what the Digest’s own poll would show.

The usual rule of thumb is that more data means less error, and 2.4 million is a great deal of data. So how can 2.4 million answers lose to a sample a tiny fraction of the size?

The Big Idea

A sample tells you about a population only if it was drawn at random. Then its error is predictable and shrinks with the square root of the sample size, while bias from who gets asked, or who answers, does not shrink at all.

The population is the whole group you want to know about — every voter, every American worker. The sample is the part you actually ask. If everyone had a known chance of being picked and everyone picked answered, the sample would miss the truth by a small amount, in either direction, and a pollster could say honestly how far off it might be. That statement is the margin of error.

But the calculation is silent about a sample that leans one way, and a bigger sample leans the same way, only more precisely. So the question to put to any survey is not “how many people?” but “who was left out, and who stayed silent?” The first question is about precision. The second is about truth.

How It Works

Start with a jar holding a million beans, exactly half of them red. You cannot count the jar, so you scoop out a thousand beans without looking and count the red ones. You will almost never get exactly 500, and a second scoop gives a different count. Each scoop is a simple random sample: every bean had the same chance of being picked, and in a jar this large picking one hardly changed the odds on another. A poll is the same experiment with people for beans. Its general name is random (probability) sampling: every member of the sampling frame — the list you actually draw from — has a known, non-zero chance of being picked, set by a chance device rather than by anyone’s choice.

Now imagine repeating the scoop ten thousand times and recording the share of red each time. Those results pile up in a pattern called the sampling distribution. Its centre is the true share, 50 percent, because random scooping errs high as often as low. Its width — the typical miss — is the standard error (SE), and it depends on only two things: how much variety the jar holds, and how many beans you scoop.

Why a square root? Treat each bean as a yes-or-no draw whose variance — the average squared distance from the mean, the square of Day 12’s standard deviation — is p(1 − p), where p is the true share of red. When you add independent quantities, their variances add. A sum of n draws therefore has variance n·p(1 − p), and its standard deviation grows like √n. But a poll reports the average, which is the sum divided by n, and dividing by n divides the spread by n too, leaving √n / n = 1/√n. The result is SE = √[p(1 − p)/n]. For p = 0.5 and n = 1,000, SE = √(0.25/1,000) ≈ 0.0158, about 1.6 percentage points.

How often is a miss of a given size? Day 11’s law of large numbers says the sample share settles toward the truth as n grows, but not how the misses are distributed. The central limit theorem does. Whatever the shape of the individual draws — here only yes or no — the average of many independent draws, provided their spread is finite, has a distribution that approaches Day 12’s bell curve, centred on the truth with width SE. That brings in the bell curve’s rule, Day 12’s about 95 percent within two standard deviations, made precise: 95 percent of its area lies within 1.96 standard deviations of its centre. So 95 percent of the time the sample falls within 1.96 × SE of the truth, and 1.96 × SE is the margin of error. For a thousand people split near 50–50 it is 1.96 × 1.58 ≈ 3.1 points, the “plus or minus 3” printed under thousands of polls.

Read the margin as a property of the procedure: run this method over and over, and about 95 times in 100 the truth lies within that distance of the result. It applies to each candidate’s share separately. A lead is the gap between two shares that move in opposite directions, so in a close two-way race the margin on the lead is about double. And it assumes the bell-curve approximation is good. For yes-or-no answers it is. For fat-tailed quantities like household wealth or insurance losses, Day 12’s warning applies: a few giants can dominate the average, the approximation converges slowly, and a printed margin can badly flatter the estimate.

Notice what n does. The margin is proportional to n−1/2: a power law like Day 3’s, a straight line of slope −½ on log–log axes. Quadrupling the sample only halves the margin: 1,000 people give ±3.1 points, 4,000 give ±1.55, 16,000 give about ±0.8. The Digest’s 2.4 million ballots were 2,400 times as many as 1,000 — more than three orders of magnitude, in Day 1’s language — and bought a margin of about ±0.06 points, 49 times smaller.

All of that rests on two assumptions the formula never checks. First, the draws are random from the whole population, so no one is likelier to be picked because of what they would say. Second, everyone picked answers, or at least those who do not answer resemble those who do. Break the first and you have selection bias: the frame misses part of the population or over-represents another, as when telephone subscribers stand in for all voters. Break the second and you have non-response bias: the people who answer differ from the people who do not. Both push the whole sample’s average the same way every time, so there is no random scatter for more data to average out. A bigger sample gets closer to a wrong number, while the margin of error, which measures only the scatter, stays small and misleading.

Real surveys rarely use a simple random sample. Stratified sampling divides the population into groups and samples each; cluster sampling picks geographic areas first and households within them, which is cheaper but less precise than the same number of independent draws, because neighbours resemble one another. Both remain probability designs, so the mathematics applies with adjustments. Quota sampling, which Gallup used in the 1930s, is different: interviewers are told to find a set number of people of each type — by age, sex, income — and choose whom to approach themselves. That matches the sample to known totals but leaves selection to human judgement, so the formulas no longer strictly hold. The method underestimated Roosevelt in 1936 by several points and failed outright in 1948, when Gallup and the other major pollsters picked Dewey over Truman.

Back to the story

Now the puzzle resolves. Size controls only the random scatter, and the Digest had driven that to about ±0.06 points. Its error was bias, and enormous. Telephone, car and subscriber lists in 1936 over-represented better-off households, in a year when votes split sharply by income. And only about one ballot in four came back, 2.4 million of roughly ten million. Which of the two did more damage is disputed Debated: the textbook account blames the lists, while Peverill Squire’s 1988 analysis of 1937 survey data argued that Landon’s supporters were also likelier to mail their ballots back, and that this mattered at least as much. Either way it was bias, and bias does not shrink with size. Gallup’s far smaller sample was imperfect too — quotas, not chance — but its bias was smaller, small enough not to change the winner.

Key Concepts

Population, sample, sampling frame
The population is everyone you want to know about, the sample is the part you ask, and the frame is the list you draw from, which may differ from the population: a telephone directory is not the electorate.
Random (probability) sample
A sample in which every member of the frame has a known, non-zero chance of selection, set by a chance device rather than by an interviewer’s or a respondent’s choice.
Central limit theorem
The average of many independent draws, whatever their individual shape (given finite spread), is approximately bell-shaped around the true value, which is why bell-curve rules govern sampling error.
Standard error and margin of error
The standard error is the typical size of the random miss; for a share it is √[p(1 − p)/n]. The margin of error is 1.96 of them either side of the result for 95 percent confidence, and it covers random error only.
Selection bias
Systematic error because the frame, or the way people are picked, over- or under-represents some part of the population.
Non-response bias
Systematic error because the people who answer differ from the people who were picked and did not.

Example: How 60,000 households become the unemployment rate

Nobody counts the unemployed. The Bureau of Labor Statistics estimates the rate from the Current Population Survey, which the Census Bureau runs for it every month. The survey takes about 60,000 eligible households through a multistage probability design: geographic areas chosen at random within strata, then clusters of neighbouring housing units chosen at random within those areas. Each household is in the sample four months, out for eight, then in for four more, so 75 percent of one month’s sample is also in the next, which sharpens the estimate of change. The survey speaks for a labour force of about 170 million people. In Logan’s chain it sits at the feedback link, the measurement that tells a society whether its earlier choices are working.

The main threat today is bias, not size. BLS’s published household response rate was 86.4 percent in February 2016 and 65.9 percent in February 2026: about one selected household in three does not answer. Like most surveys, the CPS reweights the answers it gets to match known population totals, which works only if non-respondents resemble respondents of the same type, and how well it works is disputed Debated.

1. Read the two numbers. The seasonally adjusted unemployment rate was 4.1 percent in August 2026 and 4.2 percent in September, as published to the nearest tenth: a change of 0.1 percentage point.

2. Take BLS’s own interval. At 90 percent confidence BLS puts the margin on a one-month change in the rate at about ±0.3 point, a figure it states for a rate near 6 percent. Near 4 percent the rate is somewhat less variable, so the margin is somewhat narrower, but still well above 0.1.

3. Convert the margin to standard errors. A 90 percent margin is 1.645 SE (BLS rounds to 1.6), so one SE on the monthly change is about 0.3 ÷ 1.645 ≈ 0.18 point.

4. Compare. 0.1 ÷ 0.18 ≈ 0.55 SE. Sampling noise alone produces a swing that large about half the time or more, so “unchanged” is a defensible reading of the data and “rose” is something the survey cannot establish.

5. Price the precision. Halving the margin to ±0.15 would take four times the sample, about 240,000 households — and the 34 percent who do not answer would still be missing.

Equation / Model

SE = √[ p(1 − p) / n ]  ⇒  margin of error (95%) ≈ 1.96 × SE

The standard error equals the square root of p times one minus p, divided by n. This implies the 95 percent margin of error is approximately 1.96 times the standard error.

p
the true share of the population with the trait being counted, as a fraction between 0 and 1; in practice the sample’s own share is plugged in
n
the number of people in a simple random sample, all of whom answer
SE
the standard error: the typical size of the random miss in the sample share
1.96
the number of standard deviations that encloses the middle 95 percent of a bell curve

For n = 1,000 and p = 0.5 this gives ±3.1 points; for n = 2.4 million, ±0.06. Now the half of the story the formula leaves out: total error is not just SE. Writing est for the sample’s estimate and E[ ] for the average over many repeated samples, the decisive step splits the miss into scatter plus offset and squares it:

E[(est − p)²] = E[(est − E[est])²] + 2(E[est] − p)·E[est − E[est]] + (E[est] − p)² = SE² + 0 + b²

The average squared miss, E of est minus p squared, equals the average squared scatter, E of est minus E of est squared, plus twice the offset times the average of est minus E of est, plus the offset squared. The middle term is zero, so the average squared miss equals SE squared plus b squared.

est
the estimate from one sample, such as the sample share
E[ ]
the average over many repeated samples drawn the same way
b
the bias, E[est] − p: how far the average estimate sits from the truth
0
the middle term vanishes because E[est − E[est]] = 0: scatter averages to nothing around its own centre

Take the square root and the typical miss is √(SE² + b²). For the Digest, SE² ≈ 0.0000001 while b² ≈ 0.2² = 0.04, so the total miss is about 0.20, or twenty points. Raising n shrinks the first term toward zero and leaves the second exactly where it was.

Why It Matters

You can now split any survey’s uncertainty into the part you can compute and the part you must judge. The margin of error is a floor, not a ceiling: it says how wrong a perfectly random, fully answered survey of that size would typically be, and the real uncertainty is at least that. So for any statistic resting on a survey — an unemployment rate, an approval rating, a study of what Americans believe — ask two questions in order. How big and how random? Who is missing or silent?

This also challenges a common instinct, worth checking in your own thinking: that more data is more trustworthy. The Digest is the counterexample, and the same failure recurs whenever huge convenience samples, such as online reader polls or scraped posts, are treated as measurements of a population. A large biased sample is not a weaker version of a good one; it is a more confident version of a wrong one. Day 17 carries this split, random against systematic error, from polls into laboratories.

Settled. Sampling theory: random error is computable and shrinks as 1/√n, and bias does not shrink with n at all.

Debated. Why the Digest failed — its lists or non-response, per Squire — and how well reweighting can repair today’s falling response rates.

Value judgment. Whether a sample-based estimate may allocate House seats and money, when a literal headcount is more transparent but misses people, trades fairness to the undercounted against the legitimacy of counting; statistics cannot settle that.

Across. Law. In Department of Commerce v. U.S. House of Representatives (525 U.S. 316, decided January 25, 1999) the Supreme Court held, five to four, that the Census Act forbids using statistical sampling to produce the counts that apportion House seats. The Census Bureau had planned two uses for the 2000 census to fix chronic undercounting of some groups, including certain minorities, children and renters: visit a random sample of the households that did not mail back a form and treat it as representative of the non-responders in the same census tract, then use a second, much larger survey of housing units to estimate who the count had missed. These are remedies for non-response bias and for people missed altogether, put to a court. The Court ruled on the statute and did not reach the Constitution’s “actual Enumeration” clause; whether accuracy for the undercounted should outrank a transparent headcount is a question of values Value judgment.

Remember These
  • A sample speaks for a population only if drawn at random; then its random error is predictable.
  • For a share, SE = √[p(1 − p)/n] and the 95 percent margin is about 1.96 SE: ±3.1 points for 1,000 people split near 50–50.
  • The margin shrinks as 1/√n: four times the sample halves it, and 2.4 million ballots bought about ±0.06 points.
  • Bias from who is in the frame and who answers does not shrink with n: the Digest missed by about twenty points with 2.4 million ballots.
  • A margin of error covers random error only, so ask who was left out and who stayed silent; the CPS response rate fell from 86.4 to 65.9 percent in ten years.
