Two Airliners Had the Whole Sky Over the Grand Canyon. They Flew Into Each Other Anyway.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 14 min read

Subtract one mover’s velocity from another’s and you learn whether they will meet — and if they will, the other craft hangs motionless in your view while it slowly grows.

Mathematics

Day 9 of 293 · Mathematics 9 of 24 · Vectors and relative velocity

A crowded sky that looked empty

On the morning of Saturday, June 30, 1956, two airliners left Los Angeles about three minutes apart. TWA Flight 2, a Lockheed Super Constellation, was bound for Kansas City; United Flight 718, a Douglas DC-7, for Chicago. Their routes converged over northern Arizona, and both were cruising at about 21,000 feet, in daylight, among towering clouds, in airspace where no controller was keeping them apart. The rule there was see and avoid: each crew was expected to look out of the window and keep clear.

At about half past ten that morning, above the canyon, they met. The DC-7’s left wing struck the Constellation’s tail section, and both airliners fell into the Grand Canyon. All 128 people aboard died, then the deadliest commercial air disaster in United States history.

The Civil Aeronautics Board, the federal body that investigated airline accidents at the time, found that the pilots “did not see each other in time to avoid the collision.” It could not say why. Its list of possible reasons ran from the practical to the human: clouds between the two aircraft, the limits of what could be seen from the cockpit, crews occupied with their ordinary cockpit duties, perhaps a wish to give passengers a look at the canyon, and the physiological limits of human vision.

Now notice how ordinary the setting was. It was mid-morning, in daylight, and two professional crews were expected to be looking out for exactly this. How does an airliner on a course to hit you go unseen?

The Big Idea

Quantities that have both a size and a direction, such as a velocity, are vectors, and the relative velocity of two movers — one’s velocity minus the other’s — decides whether and when they will meet.

A speed is just a number: 12 knots. A velocity is 12 knots toward the north, and the direction is not decoration. Two ships both making 12 knots can be steaming apart or steaming toward each other. Anything described by a size and a direction — a velocity, a force, a wind — is a vector. You draw it as an arrow whose length is the size and whose pointing is the direction, you add vectors by laying the arrows tip to tail, and you subtract them just as simply.

The payoff comes from asking what the world looks like from where you sit. Your own motion is the background; what matters is how the other craft moves compared with you. That is a single arrow, and its direction answers the question of whether you will meet. If it points straight back along the line from the other craft to you, you will collide if neither of you changes course or speed — and, strangely, the other craft will not appear to slide sideways across your view at all.

How It Works

Start with what a vector is. A scalar is a quantity with only a size: a temperature, a mass, a speed. A vector has a size, called its magnitude, and a direction. Velocity is the vector version of speed: its magnitude is the speed and its direction is the way you are going. Day 4 introduced the derivative as a rate of change and showed a car’s speed as the derivative of its position, the rate at which where you are changes. Once position can change in two dimensions, that rate of change has a direction too, so velocity is a vector for the same reason position is: it takes two numbers, not one, to say it.

Two numbers are how you compute with vectors. Pick two perpendicular axes — east and north on a chart — and describe any velocity by how much of it points along each. Those are its components. Navigators measure a heading as an angle clockwise from north, so a craft moving at speed |v| on heading θ has an east component |v| sin θ and a north component |v| cos θ. Sine and cosine are the standard names for two ratios of a right triangle’s sides; here the triangle’s long side is the velocity arrow and its other two sides are the components. A ship making 12 knots on heading 037° (a knot is one nautical mile per hour) has components of about 7.2 knots east and 9.6 north, the sides of a 3-4-5 triangle scaled by 2.4. Going back from components to the arrow uses the Pythagorean theorem for the size: components of 3 east and 12 north make a speed of √(3² + 12²), about 12.4.

Adding vectors is adding components. A boat steering north at 12 knots through water that is itself flowing east at 3 knots moves over the seabed at 3 east and 12 north — the two arrows laid tip to tail — a ground speed of about 12.4 knots on a track about 14° east of north. The velocity the ground sees is the velocity through the medium plus the velocity of the medium.

Subtraction is the move this lesson turns on. To subtract a vector, add its reverse: the same size, the opposite direction. And subtraction answers a natural question: how does something else move as seen from here? Drive at 60 mph past a car doing 50 and it drifts backward at 10 mph: 50 − 60 = −10. Meet a car doing 50 coming the other way, counting your own forward direction as positive, and it closes at −50 − 60 = −110 mph. In two dimensions the same operation works on components. The relative velocity of B as seen from A is vB − vA: east component minus east component, north minus north. Subtracting your velocity removes your own motion from the picture, and what is left is how the other craft moves compared with you.

Now ask when two craft meet. Suppose both hold a steady course and speed. Call the arrow from you to the other craft its position relative to you, r. It changes at a steady rate, the relative velocity, so r(t) = r0 + vrelt, where r0 is the arrow now. That is a straight line traced at a constant speed. It reaches you only if the line passes through you, which happens only when vrel points exactly opposite to r0, straight back along the line of sight. Then the other craft slides along that one line, closing at a steady rate, until the range (the distance between you) is zero at time T — the range divided by the closing speed. If vrel points anywhere else, the line misses you, the bearing (the compass direction from you to the other craft) drifts, and the craft passes at its closest point of approach, the smallest distance that straight-line path ever reaches. While your own heading holds steady, a bearing that draws aft means the other craft will pass behind you, and one that draws forward means it will cross ahead.

That gives the constant-bearing rule: if the bearing of another craft stays fixed while the range shrinks, and you both keep your courses and speeds, you are on a collision course. Sailors abbreviate it CBDR, constant bearing, decreasing range. Fixed bearing with growing range means the craft is moving away, which is why the range matters as well.

Notice what this means for whoever is on watch. A craft on a collision course does not move across your view. Everything else drifts past; this one holds its place at a fixed angle in the window, and the only thing that changes is its size. It grows slowly. Apparent width goes as one over the range, and the range is R0(1 − t/T), so the craft has doubled in apparent width when half the warning time is gone, and has grown tenfold only when nine-tenths of it has gone. Human vision is built to catch movement, especially at the edges of the visual field; a small spot that neither moves nor yet grows is easy to overlook. The Board’s list of reasons the 1956 crews might not have seen each other included the physiological limits of human vision. A watchkeeper’s habit follows from the geometry: do not wait for movement. Take a bearing, take another a little later, and if it has not changed, act.

The rule has limits that the mathematics states for itself. It assumes steady velocities, so a turn or a change of speed by either craft breaks it. And it is exact only for points; a very large vessel, or any craft at close range, can hit you while its bearing is visibly changing, because it is wide.

Back to the Grand Canyon

Treat the two airliners the way we treated the ships. Accounts of the collision describe a shallow-angle encounter, the DC-7’s left wing striking the Constellation’s tail section. To see the shape of the problem, use round numbers rather than the reported ones: both airliners at 300 mph, on courses 25° apart. Put one on heading 000° and the other on 025°. Their velocities are (0, 300) and (127, 272) mph, so the relative velocity is (127, −28), a speed of about 130 mph. For two airliners cruising near 300 mph, that is a gentle approach, less than half of either one’s speed.

If both held course and speed until the last moments, the geometry forces a steady bearing: each crew would have seen the other airliner as a small speck holding its place in the window and swelling slowly. From 5 miles away, a closing speed of 130 mph leaves a little over two minutes. The speck doubles in apparent width only after about 70 seconds have passed, and has grown tenfold only with about 14 seconds left. Human vision is tuned to motion, and a target that neither moves nor grows much is easy to miss, especially if it sits behind a window post or a cloud. That is a reconstruction, not a finding. The Board could not say which of its listed factors operated, and a steady bearing is a general mechanism consistent with its list, not part of it.

Key Concepts

Vector
A quantity with a size (magnitude) and a direction, drawn as an arrow. Velocity is a vector; speed, its magnitude, is a scalar.
Components
A vector’s parts along perpendicular axes. For speed |v| on heading θ from north: |v| sin θ east and |v| cos θ north. Vectors add and subtract component by component.
Relative velocity
The velocity of B as seen from A, vB − vA; subtracting a vector means adding its reverse.
Bearing
The compass direction from you to another craft, measured clockwise from north.
Constant bearing, decreasing range (CBDR)
The signature of a collision course when both craft hold course and speed: the other craft holds one place in your view while it gets closer.
Closest point of approach (CPA)
The smallest distance two steadily moving craft reach; zero means a collision.

Example: A Rule Written Into Maritime Law

Ships treat this geometry as law. The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (the COLREGs, in their 1972 form) tell every watchkeeper what to do about it. Rule 7 says that risk of collision shall be deemed to exist if “the compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change.” It adds a caution that the reverse does not always hold: risk may sometimes exist even when the bearing is changing appreciably, especially with a very large vessel, a tow, or at close range. Here is the rule at work, each ship treated as a point.

Setup. Your ship heads north at 12 knots. Another ship is 6 nautical miles east and 8 north of you, heading due west at 9 knots.

1. Position. r0 = (6, 8) nautical miles: range √(36 + 64) = 10, bearing arctan(6/8) ≈ 037°.

2. Velocities. vA = (0, 12); vB = (−9, 0), since west is negative east.

3. Subtract. vrel = vB − vA = (−9, −12), a closing speed of √(81 + 144) = 15 knots.

4. Compare directions. r0 = 2 × (3, 4) and vrel = −3 × (3, 4): exactly opposite, straight back along the line of sight. The bearing stays at 037°.

5. Time. 10 nautical miles at 15 knots is 40 minutes. Check by components: you cover 8 miles north at 12 knots and they cover 6 miles west at 9 knots, both in 40 minutes, arriving at the same spot together.

Slow the other ship to 6 knots and everything changes: the bearing drifts from 037° to about 056° in half an hour, and the ship passes astern of you, coming no closer than about 1.8 miles.

Equation / Model

v = (|v| sin θ, |v| cos θ)    vrel = vB − vA

The velocity v has an east component equal to the speed times the sine of theta and a north component equal to the speed times the cosine of theta. The relative velocity of B seen from A equals the velocity of B minus the velocity of A.

The decisive step shows why a collision course is a fixed line:

r(t) = r0 + vrelt  ⇒  if vrel = −r0/T, then r(t) = r0(1 − t/T)

The position of B relative to A at time t equals the starting position plus the relative velocity times t. If the relative velocity equals minus the starting position divided by T, then the position at time t equals the starting position times the quantity 1 minus t over T.

r(t)
the arrow from A to B at time t
r0
that arrow at the start; its length R0 is the starting range
t
time since the start
T
the time of collision, R0 divided by the closing speed |vrel|

The right-hand side never leaves the line through r0, so the bearing never changes, and its length R0(1 − t/T) falls to zero at t = T. The model assumes steady velocities and a flat, straight-line world.

Why It Matters

You can now look at two arrows and tell a miss from a hit before either craft has moved far, and you know what a hit looks like from the inside: nothing moving. That is counterintuitive and useful, because the instinct is to watch for the thing rushing across your view, and the thing that will hit you is the one that is not.

It also changes what counts as a fix. The Board could not say why the Grand Canyon crews failed to see each other, and the remedy that followed was institutional rather than better eyes. The Federal Aviation Act, signed on August 23, 1958, created the Federal Aviation Agency with authority over how civil and military aircraft use the nation’s airspace. The crash was not the only pressure: an April 1958 collision near Las Vegas between a United DC-7 and an Air Force jet fighter weighed on Congress as well. Note the order, too. Your chain, from need through invention and engineering to regulation and adoption, reads like a straight line, but here the regulator arrived after airline travel was established and after the deaths: regulation as feedback, not foresight.

Connections

Back. Day 4, The derivative: velocity is the derivative of position, and the arrow appears once position has two coordinates. Day 8, Optimization: closest approach is an optimization problem. The squared distance between two craft, as a function of time, has a flat bottom where its derivative is zero. For the ships above, d² = (6 − 9t)² + (8 − 12t)² with t in hours; setting its derivative −18(6 − 9t) − 24(8 − 12t) to zero gives t = 2/3 of an hour, the 40 minutes found above, with d = 0 there.

Across. Space & Astronomy. NASA histories recount that on Gemini 4, in June 1965, astronaut James McDivitt tried to fly his capsule up to the spent upper stage of the Titan rocket that had launched him, and could not close on it. Relative velocity is still what matters for whether two spacecraft meet, but gravity bends both paths, so today’s straight-line arithmetic stops working. A burn that speeds you up raises your orbit, and a higher orbit takes longer to go round, so the gap can open instead of closing. Rendezvous planning therefore uses relative-motion equations that include gravity.
