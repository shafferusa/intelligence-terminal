Learning Brief

Milgram Mailed Folders to Strangers, Mostly in Nebraska. The 64 That Reached a Boston Stockbroker Took About Six Hand-Offs. The Other 232 Never Arrived.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 16 min read

A few long-range shortcuts turn a clustered world into a small one, which is why Milgram’s finished chains were short; most of his folders still never arrived, because a short path that exists is not one anyone can find; and the links that carry things fast also carry faults, as a fault in Ohio showed in 2003 by cutting power to 50 million people.

Mathematics

Day 13 of 293 · Mathematics 13 of 24 · Networks: nodes, links, hubs and small worlds

Folders from Nebraska for a Boston stockbroker

In the 1960s the Harvard social psychologist Stanley Milgram mailed folders to 296 people, 196 in Nebraska and 100 in the Boston area. Each folder named a stranger: a stockbroker who worked in Boston and lived in Sharon, Massachusetts. The task was to get the folder to him, under one rule. You could hand it only to someone you knew on a first-name basis, and that person had to do the same; nobody could post it straight to him. The experiment asked a simple question: how many hand-offs does it take?

Milgram and his co-author Jeffrey Travers reported the results in the journal Sociometry in 1969. Sixty-four of the 296 folders reached the stockbroker. The finished chains averaged 5.2 intermediaries, which is about six hand-offs from starter to target. That average is the seed of the phrase “six degrees of separation”, a phrase that came later and owes its fame to John Guare’s 1990 play of that name. The chains were also funnelled: nearly half of the finished ones, 48 percent, ran through the same three people just before the stockbroker.

Two things make this odd. Social worlds are clustered: your friends mostly know one another, because they share a street, a workplace or a family, so each person’s circle largely overlaps with the circle next door. How can a Nebraskan more than a thousand miles from Boston reach a stranger in about six hand-offs through a world like that? And if the world is that small, why did 232 of the 296 folders, nearly four in five, never arrive?

The Big Idea

A network’s structure decides how fast things spread across it and how failures cascade through it. Four features do most of the work: how many links each node has, whether a few hubs hold most of the links, how clustered each neighbourhood is, and whether a few shortcuts bridge distant clusters.

A network is nothing more than things and the connections between them: people and friendships, airports and flights, substations and power lines, neurons and synapses. The same structural features appear whichever kind of thing the nodes are, which is why a result about mailed folders can help explain a blackout.

Two results carry the lesson. A clustered world with only local ties is enormous, but a few long-range shortcuts make it small, which is how strangers end up about six hand-offs apart. And when some nodes have far more links than the rest, those hubs make spreading faster and failure more concentrated. The catch runs through both: whatever carries a rumour, a virus or an idea quickly also carries a fault quickly, and a short path that exists is not necessarily one anyone can find.

How It Works

A network has two ingredients: nodes (the things) and links, also called edges (the connections between them). The simplest fact about a node is its degree, the number of links it has; your degree in a friendship network is your number of friends. The degree distribution says how many nodes have each degree and, as in Day 12, its shape matters more than its average. Everything below is about what the pattern of links, not the nature of the nodes, does to the traffic.

Start with the friendliest case: no structure, each person knows about k others chosen at random. You have k acquaintances one step away. Each of them knows k others, so about k² people lie two steps away, k³ at three, and reach multiplies by k at every step. With k = 100 the counts run 100; 10,000; a million; 100 million; and at the fifth step 10 billion, more than the roughly 8 billion people alive. Five steps are enough. This is Day 2’s exponential growth read backwards: because reach grows exponentially, the number of steps needed grows only as the logarithm of the population, and at k = 100 each hundredfold increase in population adds just one step. On this arithmetic alone “six degrees” is unremarkable.

But acquaintances are not random. The clustering coefficient measures how far they are: take a node, look at every pair of its neighbours, and ask what fraction of those pairs are linked to each other. If you have four friends there are six possible pairs, and if three of those pairs are friends with each other, your clustering is 3/6 = 0.5. In a clustered world most of your friend’s friends are already your friends, so the “k² people at two steps” count is a large overcount and reach grows far more slowly. Push this to the extreme: put 1,000 people on a ring, each linked only to the 10 nearest. Clustering is high (0.67, exactly two thirds), but to cross the ring a message must be passed along a few seats at a time, and the average path is about 50 steps. In general it grows in proportion to N/(2k), the population divided by twice the number of neighbours; for 8 billion people with 100 neighbours each, that is about 40 million steps. Clustering without shortcuts gives a vast world.

In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz, both then at Cornell, asked what lies between the two extremes. Take the ring and, for each link, with some small probability p detach one end and reattach it to a node chosen at random anywhere on the ring. A rewired link is a shortcut: it joins two people who may sit on opposite sides. In simulations of the ring of 1,000 with 10 links each, the setup Watts and Strogatz studied, rewiring nothing leaves the average path at about 50 steps and clustering at 0.67. Rewiring just 1 percent of the links, about 50 of the 5,000, drops the average path to about 9 steps while clustering barely moves, to about 0.65. Rewire everything and you reach the random network: paths of about 3.3 steps, but clustering of 0.01. A shortcut is that powerful because it joins not two individuals but two whole neighbourhoods; everyone near one end becomes close to everyone near the other. Path length is a global property that a few bridges transform, while clustering is a local one that a few bridges leave nearly intact. Watts and Strogatz called a network with high clustering and short paths a small-world network and found the pattern in three real ones, among them a collaboration graph of about 225,000 film actors. Sociologists already had a name for such ties: in 1973 Mark Granovetter called them “weak ties”, the acquaintances who often belong to circles other than yours.

So far every node has about the same number of links. Many real networks do not. Most airports have a handful of routes while a few have hundreds; most web pages are linked from almost nowhere while a few are linked from millions. This is the fat-tailed distribution of Day 12, and the few nodes far out in its tail are hubs. In 1999 Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert showed that a simple growth rule produces them. If each newcomer links preferentially to nodes that already have many links (preferential attachment, the rich-get-richer rule), the degree distribution becomes a power law, in their model with the chance of a node having k links falling as 1/k³: the straight line on a log-log plot that Day 3 introduced.

Hubs do three things. They shorten paths: route through a hub and a trip from a small town to the far side of the world takes a few legs. They speed spreading: in 2001 Romualdo Pastor-Satorras and Alessandro Vespignani showed that in an idealized, endlessly large network with such hubs, an infection needs no minimum transmissibility to persist (the epidemic threshold vanishes), because infected hubs seed so many others. And they concentrate risk: in 2000 Réka Albert, Hawoong Jeong and Barabási simulated deleting nodes and found that a hub-dominated network barely notices random failures, since most nodes are minor, but breaks apart when the hubs are removed on purpose. How common such networks are is itself disputed Debated: in 2019 Anna Broido and Aaron Clauset tested nearly 1,000 real networks and concluded that strict power laws are rare, a finding that Barabási and other defenders of the “scale-free” picture (the name for power-law networks) dispute. What survives either way is the qualitative point: where degrees are heavy-tailed, hubs are both highways and single points of failure.

Last, failure. Most real networks have capacity: a power line carries only so much current and a runway handles only so many flights. When a node fails, its load shifts to its neighbours, and if they cannot absorb it they fail too and pass it on. That is a cascading failure. Whether it stops at one node or sweeps the system depends on the structure, meaning how many alternative routes the neighbours offer, and on how close to capacity everything was running. The links that carry a rumour fast carry a failure fast. The Example works one through.

Back to the story

Two puzzles, two answers. Why so short? The logarithm does the same work for a country as for the world: roughly 200 million Americans, each with a few dozen usable acquaintances, gives about five to six steps, close to Milgram’s six hand-offs. A clustered world can reach that only through shortcuts, such as the old roommate in another state or the business contact in the next city. The three people who funnelled nearly half the finished chains were hubs in miniature, standing right beside the target.

Why did most folders never arrive? A path that exists is not a path that gets found. Nobody in Nebraska could see the map. Each person saw only their own links, had to guess which acquaintance stood closest to a stranger in Sharon, and had to bother to pass the folder on. Chances multiply: if each person forwards with probability three in four (an illustration, not Milgram’s measured rate), a chain of six hand-offs survives with probability 0.756 ≈ 0.18, and a chain of ten with only 0.7510 ≈ 0.06. Long chains die first, so the 5.2 describes the survivors, which are biased toward the short ones. In 2000 Jon Kleinberg proved that, in his model of a lattice with shortcuts, a searcher who sees only local links can find short routes only if the long-range ties are spread in a particular way. Short paths exist; whether people can find them is a separate question.

Key Concepts

Node and link (edge)
A node is one thing in the network (a person, airport, substation or neuron); a link is a connection between two nodes. Everything else is built from these two.
Degree and degree distribution
A node’s degree is its number of links. The degree distribution counts how many nodes have each degree; whether it is bell-shaped or fat-tailed decides whether hubs exist.
Hub
A node whose degree is far above the typical node’s, found in the fat tail of the degree distribution. Hubs shorten paths and speed spreading, and their loss does disproportionate damage.
Clustering coefficient
For one node, the fraction of pairs among its neighbours that are themselves linked. High clustering means “my friends know each other”.
Path length and shortcuts (small world)
Path length is the number of links on the shortest route between two nodes, averaged over all pairs for the network. A shortcut is a long-range link joining distant clusters; a small-world network keeps high clustering and still has short paths.
Cascading failure
A chain reaction in which one node’s failure shifts load onto neighbours that then fail in turn.

Example: The 2003 Northeast Blackout

The power grid, a recurring system in this course, sits at the physical-constraint and engineering links of the chain: electricity must be moved the instant it is made, over lines that carry only so much. The US–Canada Power System Outage Task Force’s final report (April 2004) reconstructs 14 August 2003. At about 2:15 p.m. the alarm software in FirstEnergy’s Ohio control room failed, leaving operators unaware of their system’s state. Between about 3 and 4 p.m. several 345-kilovolt lines in northern Ohio sagged into overgrown trees and tripped, each trip pushing power onto neighbouring lines. At about 4:06 p.m. another overloaded line tripped and the cascade ran, cutting power within minutes to about 50 million people in eight US states and Ontario.

The 1998 Watts–Strogatz paper had analysed a grid too: the western US network of 4,941 generators, transformers and substations, with about 2.7 links each. Its clustering was about 0.08, sixteen times the 0.005 of a random network of the same size and density, and its average path was about 19 steps against about 12: a small world. But its degrees have an exponential tail with no dominant hubs, and the 2003 outage struck the eastern grid, a different network, so the blackout illustrates the cascade half of the lesson, not the hub half. Why does a fault sometimes stop and sometimes sweep? Toy numbers show the logic (not 2003 data; real flows divide by each line’s impedance, not equally).

1. Set up. A corridor of three parallel lines, each rated for 100 MW (megawatts) and each carrying 70 MW, delivers 210 MW.

2. One trips. Its 70 MW splits between the two survivors: +35 MW each, so 105 MW on lines rated for 100.

3. It spreads. Both survivors are now overloaded. If they trip, all 210 MW is pushed onto the next corridor: a fault in one line has become a fault in the corridor.

4. Add one more path. Share the same 210 MW over four lines: 52.5 MW each. If one trips, its 52.5 MW splits three ways: +17.5 MW each, so 70 MW, well under the rating. The system absorbs the fault.

5. Or add margin. Keep three lines but run each at 60 MW: the survivors take +30 MW, so 90 MW, and also hold. Structure and loading both decide how far a fault travels.

Real lines trip with a delay and operators can shed load, but the mechanism holds. The Task Force traced the initiating failures mainly to FirstEnergy: its alarm software, its tree-trimming and its grasp of its own system. Network structure did not start the cascade; with the lines’ loading, it helped set how far the cascade ran.

Equation / Model

The reach argument fits in one line, and the decisive step is taking the logarithm of both sides.

kL ≈ N  ⇒  L · ln k ≈ ln N  ⇒  L ≈ ln N ⁄ ln k

k to the power L is approximately N, so L times the natural logarithm of k is approximately the natural logarithm of N, so L is approximately the natural logarithm of N divided by the natural logarithm of k.

L
the typical number of steps between two nodes (the path length)
N
the number of nodes in the network
k
the typical number of links per node, assuming they lead to different people
ln
the natural logarithm, the inverse of the exponential function (Day 2)

For N = 8 × 109 and k = 100: ln N = ln 8 + 9 ln 10 ≈ 2.08 + 20.72 = 22.80, and ln 100 ≈ 4.61, so L ≈ 22.80 ÷ 4.61 ≈ 4.95, about five steps. The answer barely depends on k: k = 50 gives 5.8 and k = 1,000 gives 3.3. This is the benchmark for a network with no clustering. Clustering lengthens real paths, and the Watts–Strogatz result is that a few shortcuts bring them most of the way back down.

Ci = 2ei ⁄ [ki(ki − 1)]

The clustering coefficient of node i equals twice e sub i, divided by k sub i times k sub i minus one.

Ci
the clustering coefficient of node i, between 0 (no two neighbours linked) and 1 (all linked)
ki
the degree of node i, so ki(ki − 1)/2 is the number of possible pairs of its neighbours
ei
the number of links that actually exist among node i’s neighbours

The four-friend example gives 2 × 3 ÷ (4 × 3) = 0.5. For the ring of 10 neighbours, the clustering works out to exactly two thirds (the 0.67 above).

Why It Matters

You can now put four questions to any system where something spreads or breaks. How many links does a typical node have, and is the distribution fat-tailed, so that hubs exist? How clustered is it? Are there shortcuts between the clusters? How close to capacity is everything running? Rumours, ideas, infections and faults travel by the same structure, so one answer predicts both the benefit and the risk: the shortcut that carries a job lead across a city carries an infection with it, and the hub that makes an airline network efficient is the node whose loss strands travellers everywhere.

It also challenges a tempting picture. “Everything is connected within a few steps” sounds profound, but short paths are what you get almost automatically once a few long-range links exist, so the claim is cheap evidence. For a book about how fields connect, the test is not whether a short path exists between two fields (it nearly always does) but whether the link carries something, a shared mathematics, a prerequisite or a causal path, the way a power line carries current.

Settled. The mathematics of reach, clustering and shortcuts, and that a local fault can cascade through a grid.

Debated. How small the world really is. Milgram’s starters were not a random sample and most chains failed (Judith Kleinfeld, 2002), though an email replication (Dodds, Muhamad and Watts, 2003) and Facebook’s whole-network count (Backstrom and colleagues, 2012: an average of 4.74 steps among about 721 million users, which shows paths exist, not that anyone could find them) also found short paths. Also debated: how common power-law networks are.

Value judgment. How much slack to buy against rare cascades, and who pays: ratepayers and shareholders bear its cost, while the public bears the cost of its absence.

Connections

In a thin-tailed network nearly every node is typical; in a fat-tailed one a few hubs sit far out in the tail, so the average degree is a poor summary. Across. Biology, Medicine & Health: the roundworm Caenorhabditis elegans has 302 neurons, and John White, Sydney Brenner and colleagues mapped the connections among them from electron micrographs (Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 1986). In the same 1998 Nature paper that analysed the power grid, Watts and Strogatz showed that a version of this wiring diagram, with about 280 neurons, is a small world: clustering of about 0.28 against about 0.05 for a random network of the same size and density, yet an average path of 2.65 steps against 2.25. The same clustering-plus-shortcuts mathematics describes a social network and a nervous system; it does not say why the wiring takes this form.

Remember These
  • A network’s structure (degree distribution, clustering, shortcuts) decides how things spread through it and how failures cascade.
  • With random links the typical path is ln N ÷ ln k: about five steps for 8 billion people with 100 acquaintances each.
  • Clustering alone makes a vast world; about 1 percent of links rewired as shortcuts makes it small and leaves clustering nearly intact.
  • Fat-tailed degrees create hubs, which are fast highways and single points of failure.
  • A short path existing is not the same as its being found: Milgram’s finished chains took about six hand-offs, yet 232 of 296 folders never arrived.
