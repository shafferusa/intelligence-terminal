Trump Calls Iran’s Offer “Not Acceptable” as a Week of Prices, Payrolls and Micron Tests a 5% Bond Market

Sunday, September 27, 2026 · 45 min read

Trump said on record Saturday that Iran’s seven-day offer to reopen Hormuz “would not be acceptable,” and Tehran says it will not soften its terms. Markets open Monday into a week that stacks August inflation, quarter-end and Micron on Wednesday and the September jobs report on Friday, with the 10-year near a 19-year high and an October Fed hike already about three-quarters priced.

The Brief

If you read nothing else

  • Markets. The week turns on two numbers. August PCE inflation lands Wednesday at 8:30 AM; core was 3.3% a year earlier in July, and a rough consensus has it rising 0.3% on the month. September payrolls come Friday, with forecasts of 85,000–100,000 jobs. The 10-year yield ended last week at 5.17% after a 5.18% close Thursday, its highest since July 2007. Fed-funds futures, as reported by secondary trackers, put the odds of an Oct. 28 hike at roughly 70–76%.
  • Iran. Trump told reporters Saturday that Iran’s offer to reopen the Strait of Hormuz “would not be acceptable.” That confirms on the record the rejection the Wall Street Journal reported Friday night. Foreign Minister Araghchi says the mediators, Qatar and Pakistan, have conveyed nothing yet, and Iran will not soften its terms. The Journal’s further claim, that Trump plans to resume bombing after the Nov. 3 midterms, is still unconfirmed. Oil futures reopen Sunday at 6 PM ET.
  • Wednesday stacks up. Wednesday brings PCE, a third estimate of second-quarter GDP folded into the start of BEA’s annual revisions, ADP, the end of the quarter and the federal fiscal year, and Micron’s results after the close. Consensus has Micron at about $31.5–32.3 a share on roughly $51–52 billion of revenue. It is the week’s one hard read on AI demand.
  • The weekend. Russia struck Kyiv for a third straight night, and Ukraine says the Ilsky refinery it hit Friday has stopped operating. A projection shows Swiss voters rejecting a strict-neutrality amendment by about 71%. Gunmen killed at least 27 people in two bar attacks in South Africa. China held naval and air drills around Scarborough Shoal.
  • Washington. There is no shutdown this week. The stopgap Trump signed Sept. 2 funds the government through Dec. 11, and the House stays out until after the election. The Senate returns Monday for its last scheduled pre-election week, and Monday is the plaintiffs’ deadline for a preliminary injunction in the press-access case.
  • Local. Harrisburg returns Monday with six session days before the Oct. 13 skill-games deadline. Wednesday is the expiry date for Duquesne Light’s contract with its line workers, the close of public comment on PRT’s bus-network redesign, and Pittsburgh’s deadline for a balanced five-year plan.
  • Biggest unknown. Whether the bond market keeps pricing more hikes on its own. The 20- and 30-year made new highs on Friday, a day with no data surprise and no auction.

Today’s News

Trump says Iran’s Hormuz offer “would not be acceptable”; Tehran says it will not soften its terms

Saturday’s remark puts on the record what the Wall Street Journal reported Friday night. The paper’s other claim, a post-midterm bombing plan, remains unconfirmed, and no counter-offer has been made.

Leaving the White House for the Tennessee–Texas football game in Knoxville on Saturday morning, Trump was asked about Iran’s proposal. He said it “would not be acceptable” and gave no reasons. The White House has posted video of the exchange. A US official, not named in the reports, said the United States “is in a very strong position with control of the Strait of Hormuz, so we are not in a rush.” A White House official said the president remains open to talks. NPR, the Washington Post, NBC and CNN all reported the remark.

Iran’s answer came within hours. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Tehran had seen Trump’s “first reaction” but that “nothing has been conveyed to us from the mediators yet.” The mediators are Qatar and Pakistan. He added that “Iran does not accept coercion, threats or intimidation.” Bloomberg reported Sunday that Iran will not soften its conditions. It wants the US naval blockade of Iranian ports lifted, sanctions eased and frozen assets released, and an end to the war “on all fronts,” which includes Lebanon. President Pezeshkian told the UN last week there would be no free navigation while the blockade and sanctions stay in place.

What has not moved is the second half of the Journal’s Friday report. By that account Trump expects to resume bombing Iran after the Nov. 3 midterms, with the scale undecided. Nothing on the record confirms or denies it. Trump has made no statement about timing, and there has been no Pentagon briefing and no war-powers notice to Congress. No new tanker incident was reported over the weekend.

Why it matters An offer rejected on the record, with no counter-offer, means Hormuz stays closed for now. Traffic through the strait has been a small fraction of its normal 80-plus tankers a day for weeks. Front-month WTI fell 7.9% last week, to $92.41, largely on hopes of a phased reopening. Sunday night’s futures open is the first chance to price the rejection. Higher oil feeds straight into this week’s central question, which is inflation.
What’s next A formal US reply through the mediators; any revised Iranian offer; any statement on timing from the White House or Pentagon. Watch Brent and WTI at Sunday’s 6 PM ET open, and the tanker-tracking counts through the strait.

Go deeper

The two sides’ positions are now public and far apart. Iran’s plan is a seven-day sequence: first steps on days one to five, the strait reopening on day six, and talks on a final deal from day seven, all conditional on the blockade and oil sanctions going first. Washington’s stated position is that it controls the strait and can wait. The US line, in other words, is that time is on its side. Two things argue against that. The Fed is fighting inflation with oil above $90. And the political calendar the Journal described would keep the strait closed at least through the election. In the Gulf, the paper’s open forecasts are for another kinetic incident in the strait by Sept. 30 and a confirmed attribution of September’s two unclaimed tanker strikes by Oct. 6. Neither has come to pass.

Russia hits Kyiv for a third straight night with 170 drones; Ukraine says the Ilsky refinery has stopped operating

The energy-strike truce discussed at the UN on Tuesday has not taken hold, and no date has been set for the Abu Dhabi technical talks Washington proposed.

Ukraine’s Air Force says Russia launched 170 Shahed-type drones overnight into Sunday, 76 of them jet-powered, along with decoys. It says 147 were shot down or jammed, with hits recorded at 18 locations. Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko reported one person injured and a fire in an uninhabited residential building in the Shevchenkivskyi district. Strikes also hit the Odesa, Kharkiv, Sumy, Mykolaiv, Kherson, Poltava and Zhytomyr regions. Ukrainian officials count eight killed and 42 injured across five regions from Saturday into Sunday. That total overlaps the seven deaths reported in Saturday’s edition. By Zelensky’s tally, Russia fired more than 2,200 drones, about 1,650 glide bombs and 38 missiles over the past week. All of these figures are Ukrainian official counts.

Friday’s Ukrainian strike on the Ilsky refinery in Russia’s Krasnodar region killed three people, the regional authorities say. Fire Point, the Ukrainian maker of the FP-1 drone used, says the attack damaged three primary processing units and a tank farm and that the plant has suspended operations. The shutdown is the manufacturer’s own claim, and Russia has not confirmed it. Separately, Zelensky sanctioned 44 people by decree for organising Russia’s Duma voting in occupied Ukraine.

Why it matters Each week the energy war runs, Russian refining capacity falls and Ukrainian power infrastructure takes more damage heading into winter. The US push for a reciprocal pause on energy strikes has so far produced no measurable change on either side.

Swiss voters reject writing “perpetual and armed” neutrality into the constitution, by about 71% on the projection

The measure would have barred Switzerland from military alliances and from any sanctions the UN Security Council has not approved, including its current Russia measures.

A GFS Bern projection for the national broadcaster SRG on Sunday shows the neutrality initiative losing by about 71% to 29%. Official results come later Sunday. The amendment would have made neutrality “perpetual and armed,” ruled out joining a military alliance unless Switzerland were attacked, and limited Swiss sanctions to those backed by the UN Security Council. Opponents argued it would have forced Bern to drop its alignment with EU sanctions on Russia. Supporters said it would restore a neutrality they believe recent governments have diluted. A second measure on the same ballot, on food self-sufficiency, had been polling well short of a majority; no projection had been published at this writing.

GFS Bern projection for SRG (projection, not the official count) · Al Jazeera · Swissinfo (pre-vote polling).

Gunmen kill at least 27 in two bar attacks in South Africa, five days after a Durban-area shooting killed 11

No arrests had been made by Sunday morning, and police had not said whether the attacks are linked.

At about 9:30 PM Saturday, eight gunmen with assault rifles attacked a bar west of Johannesburg, killing 17 people and wounding 15, according to South African police as reported by AP. Early Sunday, at about 1:45 AM, gunmen opened fire at a grilled-meat venue in Lwandle township outside Cape Town. Five people died at the scene and five more in hospital. Police have launched a manhunt. The attacks follow a township shooting near Durban on Tuesday that killed at least 11. Police have not said who carried out any of the three or why.

South African Police Service (primary) · AP via ABC News and NBC · CNN · ABC Australia.

China stages naval, air and coast-guard drills around Scarborough Shoal, two days after Xi left Washington

The coast guard rehearsed boarding and forced towing. Beijing called the drills a response to “certain countries” undermining stability, and Manila had not responded by Sunday morning.

The People’s Liberation Army’s Southern Theater Command held joint naval and air exercises around the shoal on Sunday. The shoal is claimed by both China and the Philippines and lies inside the Philippines’ exclusive economic zone. The China Coast Guard ran interdiction, boarding and forced-towing drills in the same waters, its spokesman Jiang Lue said. The drills come two days after the Trump–Xi summit ended with a new military crisis-communication memorandum, and with the $14 billion Taiwan arms package still undecided.

Yemen’s government widens its Taiz offensive; Saudi strikes in western Yemen are reported but not confirmed

No new Houthi launches at Saudi Arabia have been reported since Saturday morning’s intercepted salvo.

On Sunday, the Yemeni government’s military media centre claimed air and ground operations against Houthi positions at five locations in Taiz governorate. Haaretz and AFP report Saudi strikes in western Yemen after Saturday’s Houthi missiles and drones aimed at Riyadh and Khamis Mushait; the Saudi-led coalition has not confirmed them in anything this paper has seen. In Lebanon, the Israeli military says Hezbollah flew an explosive drone at its troops in the south overnight without causing injuries, and that its aircraft then struck Hezbollah targets. That account comes from the Israeli military alone. US-mediated Israel–Lebanon talks are postponed to October.

Nubank is reported to be in early talks to buy Britain’s Monzo for up to £10 billion

The report comes from a single outlet, Sky News. Neither company has commented.

Single source Sky News reported Friday night that Nu Holdings (NYSE: NU), the Brazilian digital bank, is in early talks on a cash-and-stock deal that would value Monzo at £8–10 billion. Bloomberg and Reuters carried the report on Sky’s authority. Monzo is also said to be weighing a funding round above £8 billion to pay for European expansion. Top Themes

1. Does the data confirm what bonds already fear? In a week, the 10-year rose 16 basis points to a 19-year high, all of it in real yields. The market has pulled an October hike most of the way into its price. This week delivers the two numbers that can justify or deflate that: August PCE on Wednesday and September payrolls on Friday. A hot pair tests the 10-year’s ceiling and the rate-sensitive sectors already at the bottom of the tape. A soft pair takes air out of the long end and helps the small caps and real estate that have lagged all month.

2. Oil on a political clock. Trump’s on-record “not acceptable” removes the hope that pushed WTI down 7.9% last week. The move in oil since Friday is the first read on how much of a Hormuz discount the market had built in. Oil at $90-plus is also an inflation input the Fed has to price, and Bank of England Governor Bailey said Friday that high energy prices make it harder to keep rates on hold.

3. Micron as the AI trade’s referee. Last week’s index gain was carried by chips and a handful of AI names while the equal-weight S&P fell. Micron reports Wednesday after the close, with options implying a move of about 8% either way. Its guidance on high-bandwidth memory and DRAM pricing is the cleanest outside check on the Goldman note that asked whether hyperscaler spending pays off.

4. Quarter-end plumbing. Wednesday closes the quarter and the federal fiscal year. Funding rates are sitting where the Fed’s hike put them, with no sign of stress. Wednesday’s repo market and Thursday’s Fed balance-sheet release are the tests. Portfolio rebalancing after a strong equity quarter and a weak bond one could add selling pressure on stocks. No reliable estimate of its size has been published, and this paper does not guess.

5. A quiet Congress and a loud campaign. There is no shutdown deadline and the House is out, so Washington’s week happens in the courts, on the campaign trail (Trump rallies Thursday and Friday) and in the Senate’s last pre-election floor days. The generic-ballot average is at a cycle high for Democrats.

The Economy Ahead

Where we start. The Fed raised its target range to 3.75–4.00% on Sept. 16, a unanimous decision, and 16 of 18 policymakers projected at least one more hike this year. Chair Kevin Warsh has said nothing in public since the press conference. Barr and New York Fed President Williams both said last week that another hike is likely. The data behind them has been running warm. The September flash composite PMI was the strongest in more than five years, jobless claims are at 197,000, and core capital-goods orders rose 1.6% in August. On the other side, consumer sentiment is near a four-month low, and households’ one-year inflation expectations jumped to 4.6%. The 30-year mortgage rate has reached 7.45%, a two-year high, according to CNBC’s week-ahead report.

PCE, Wednesday 8:30 AM: the Fed’s own inflation gauge. In July, headline and core PCE each rose 0.2% on the month; core was up 3.3% from a year earlier and headline 3.7%. The one usable consensus, from FinancialJuice, has core rising 0.3% in August and running at about 3.2% a year. That is low confidence, because other aggregator figures in circulation are plainly stale. August consumer and producer prices have already been published, so the PCE translation is largely known to forecasters. The surprise would come from categories that appear only in PCE, such as healthcare and financial services, and from revisions to July. What a surprise would mean: core at 0.4% or higher would cement October pricing and put pressure on the 5.2% area in the 10-year. Core at 0.1–0.2% would be the first soft inflation read since the hike, and the long end would likely rally.

GDP third estimate, also Wednesday. This release begins BEA’s annual update, which can revise several years of history. The revisions to income and saving may matter more than the second-quarter headline. A lower saving rate would say the consumer has been spending from savings rather than income.

The labor market: JOLTS Tuesday, ADP Wednesday, payrolls Friday. July job openings were little changed at 7.3 million. ADP counted only 38,000 private jobs in August, against 162,000 in the government’s payroll survey. Weekly ADP data averaged 20,000 a week through early September. For Friday, Reuters’ poll has 100,000 jobs and 4.2% unemployment. CNBC’s preview has 85,000 and 4.1%. FinancialJuice has average hourly earnings up 0.3% on the month and 3.2% on the year, against 3.1% in August. What a surprise would mean: with an October hike already about three-quarters priced, the asymmetry is on the soft side. A weak print, say under 50,000 or unemployment at 4.3%, would move rates more than a strong one. A wage number at 0.4% or more would matter more than the headline. Friday’s revisions to July and August count too: in August they added a net 55,000.

ISM manufacturing, Thursday. August was 55.2. The flash S&P manufacturing PMI for September jumped to 57.0 from 53.9. If ISM confirms that, the goods side of the economy is re-accelerating into a hiking cycle, which is unusual and hawkish.

The Fed’s calendar. The Federal Reserve Board’s published schedule:

  • Bowman on supervision, Monday.
  • Barr, “Economic Outlook,” Tuesday at 12:40 in Detroit. He is the most important voice of the week, having said further adjustments are “likely to be needed.”
  • Waller on payments, Tuesday, and at the St. Louis Fed’s FRED conference, Thursday.
  • Cook, Wednesday and Thursday.
  • Vice Chair Jefferson on “U.S. Economy and Monetary Policy,” Thursday at 1:30, with questions.

One aggregator lists regional presidents Goolsbee, Williams and Schmid as speaking this week; this paper could not confirm the dates. The next FOMC decision is Oct. 28, with Warsh’s press conference at 2:30 PM. The minutes of the September meeting are expected around Oct. 7, not this week.

Treasury. The week is bills only. Monday brings $95 billion of 13-week and $82 billion of 26-week bills, Tuesday $54 billion of 52-week and $85 billion of 6-week, then 17-, 4- and 8-week bills. There are no coupon auctions until next week’s 3-year (Oct. 6), 10-year reopening (Oct. 7) and 30-year reopening (Oct. 8). Their sizes are announced Thursday, and after last week’s soft 5- and 7-year sales they are the next test of foreign demand. Treasury has bought back nearly $500 billion of its own debt across more than 150 operations. Coverage of Secretary Bessent’s expanded long-end buybacks describes their impact on yields as limited. The quarterly refunding estimates come Nov. 2. The stopgap funding law removes any Oct. 1 shutdown risk, so data releases will not be interrupted.

Abroad.

  • Australia (Tuesday). The Reserve Bank of Australia is widely expected to raise its cash rate 25 basis points to 4.60%. All four big banks forecast it, and markets price it above 90%.
  • Eurozone. The ECB, which hiked in September, hears from President Lagarde at the European Parliament Monday. The euro-area flash inflation estimate for September is due Friday, per Eurostat’s calendar as cited; one source says Wednesday. August was 3.2%, with energy up 14.3% on the year.
  • Japan (Thursday, overnight Wednesday ET). The Bank of Japan’s Tankan survey. Large manufacturers stood at +22 in June and had projected +17 for September. The yen, near 158 to the dollar, is the market to watch around it.
  • China. Official September PMIs come out Wednesday (August: manufacturing 49.8, non-manufacturing 49.0), just before a week-long holiday.
  • India. The Reserve Bank of India decides Oct. 7.

The global long-end selloff is the common thread: Japanese, British and German long yields all made fresh highs last week.

Earnings & Business

Micron (Wednesday, after the close): the one that matters. Consensus for the fiscal fourth quarter is about $31.56 a share on $51.2 billion of revenue (33 analysts, per Alphastreet). Finnhub’s tally is a little higher, at $32.32 and $52.2 billion. That brackets the company’s June guidance of $31.00 ± $1.00 and $50.0 billion ± $1.0 billion. The stock traded near $1,072 last week, up more than 500% in a year, and options imply a move of about 8% either way. The questions are forward-looking:

  • How much high-bandwidth memory is sold out for 2027.
  • Where contract DRAM prices go.
  • Any exposure to chip tariffs.
  • Whether fiscal first-quarter guidance runs above the Street.

After a week in which a Goldman note estimated hyperscaler AI capex at $800 billion this year and $1.2 trillion next, Micron is the supplier-side answer to whether that spending is still accelerating. Micron rose 6.5% last week.

Market Setup

Indices. The S&P 500 finished Friday at 7,743, about 1% below its August record, 0.8% above its 20-day average and 1.3% above its 50-day. The Nasdaq 100 fund (QQQ) sits 3.3% above its 20-day average, stretched going into Micron. The Russell 2000 and the Dow sit below both averages. Reuters notes the S&P is roughly flat in September and up more than 12% on the year, while nine of 11 sectors are down on the month. The rally is narrow and leans on one theme. That leaves it vulnerable to a single disappointing data point, but a flat month under 19-year-high yields also shows it has absorbed a lot.

Sectors. Technology and communication services lead. Utilities, real estate, financials, materials and consumer discretionary are down 5–9% over the month. The rotation map is simple this week. A hot PCE and payrolls pair hurts the laggards most, because they are the rate-sensitive ones. A soft pair likely gives them a relief rally at tech’s relative expense. Energy depends on oil’s response to the Iran rejection. Defense names fell last week even as the Gulf war escalated, which suggests they are trading on budget and rates rather than headlines.

Company catalysts. Micron (Wednesday) for AI infrastructure and semiconductors (SMH +5.9% last week). Nike (Thursday) for the consumer and China. Carnival for fuel costs and travel demand. Jefferies for Wall Street activity. Starship Flight 14 (Monday) for SpaceX (SPCX, $148.68, next lockup release Oct. 9) and the space stocks that rallied last week. Oura’s pricing is a gauge of the IPO window.

Rates and the dollar. The 2s10s curve is at +36 basis points after bear-steepening all week, with the 10-year real yield at 2.85% and the breakeven at 2.34%. The dollar index is at 100.97, and USD/JPY near 158 is the pressure point, since Tokyo last intervened in 2024 with the yen near 160. The VIX is at 14.87 with nine-day volatility below 30-day. Equity options are not pricing this week’s data as an event, which leaves room for a vol pickup on a surprise.

Scenarios

Base case, about 45–55%: warm data, sticky yields, a narrow tape. Core PCE comes in near 0.3%, payrolls near consensus, and wages steady at about 3.2%. The 10-year holds in a 5.05–5.25% range and October-hike pricing stays near three in four. Micron meets high expectations without an upside shock, and the S&P chops within 2% of its record. Oil drifts higher on the impasse without spiking. Indicators: Barr repeats his hike bias without escalating, and ISM lands near 55–57. Confirmers: high-yield spreads between 2.75% and 2.95%, VIX under 17. Invalidators: any print more than one standard deviation from consensus.

Bull case, about 15–25%: the data cools. Core PCE at 0.2% or below, payrolls soft but positive, and unemployment steady at 4.1%. The long end rallies 10–20 basis points and October-hike odds fall below 50%. Laggards such as small caps, real estate and utilities rebound, the dollar eases, and Micron’s guidance extends the AI trade. Confirmers: the 2-year below 4.70%, RSP outperforming SPY. Invalidators: hot wages, or a hawkish Jefferson on Thursday.

Bear case, about 20–30%: hot data meets expensive stocks. Core PCE at 0.4% or higher, a strong ISM, and payrolls well above 100,000 with firm wages. The 10-year breaks above 5.25% and the 30-year above 5.6%. High-yield spreads move toward 3.00%, and October-hike pricing heads toward certainty, with talk of December as well. A Micron miss would turn a rates problem into an equity problem. Confirmers: the S&P below its 50-day average (about 7,640 on the SPY equivalent), VIX above 20, USD/JPY above 160. Invalidators: a soft labor print.

Shock case, about 5%: a Gulf escalation. A tanker strike, a Houthi hit on Gulf oil infrastructure, or US strikes brought forward before the election. Oil jumps above $100 and equities sell off broadly. Treasuries face a genuine cross-current between flight to safety and inflation risk. Gold and the dollar likely gain. Confirmers: a Pentagon announcement, UKMTO incident reports. This scenario would supersede everything else in this edition.

What would change the outlook. A revised Iranian offer that drops the sanctions precondition; a Fed speaker, above all Jefferson, pushing back on October; a payroll print below zero; or a Taiwan arms signature.

The United States

No funding cliff. A premise worth clearing up: Sept. 30 is the end of the federal fiscal year, but not a shutdown deadline. Trump signed the Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027 on Sept. 2 after votes of 370–48 in the House and 90–6 in the Senate. It funds the government through Dec. 11, so the next shutdown fight comes in the lame-duck session after the election. House leaders cancelled the chamber’s remaining September session weeks, and it stays out through October.

The Senate’s last week before the election. The Senate returns Monday at 3 PM. Majority Leader John Thune has said he wants votes on nominations, including Keith Sonderling, the acting labor secretary, for the permanent post. He also wants a possible test vote on the Ratepayer Protection Act, which would make large data centers pay for the grid upgrades they require. The House passed it 417–3, but Democrats blocked fast-track passage in the Senate earlier this month. Thune said a vote “would take some cooperation from Democrats.” The annual defense authorization bill remains stalled after a failed procedural vote in July, and nothing suggests it moves this week.

The press-access case. The three newsrooms, CNN, MS NOW and Politico, must file for a preliminary injunction by Monday. Judge Timothy Kelly’s 14-day order, issued Sept. 24, restoring their access runs to about Oct. 8, and he has said he will rule “expeditiously.” The administration has said it will appeal. On Saturday the White House left CNN out of the Air Force One press pool for the Knoxville trip, and at least one report says it added LindellTV. Trump posted that outlets “shouldn’t be able to constantly write or report FICTION and LIES.” The court order covers White House access; whether it reaches the aircraft pool is the kind of question the injunction fight will settle.
