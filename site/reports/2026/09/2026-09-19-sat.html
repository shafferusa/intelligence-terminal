Today’s News

Trump says a “big decision” on Iran is coming as the Houthis complete their takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb strait

Days after Yemeni Houthi forces finished seizing every position commanding the Red Sea’s southern chokepoint, Trump told Axios he is deciding whether to “go in and annihilate” Iran’s regime or not — a decision he says will take shape at Tuesday’s Gulf-leaders meeting on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly.

President Trump told Axios Thursday that he faces a “big decision coming up” on Iran: “Do I want to go in and annihilate them, or do I not?” He is due to meet leaders of six Gulf states — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and Oman — Tuesday on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly’s high-level session in New York, a meeting reporting frames as likely to shape whether the administration pushes for renewed diplomacy or intensifies military action. The remarks land days after Houthi forces completed their sweep of Yemen’s Red Sea coastline: beyond the port of Mocha and the islands of Perim and Zuqar (already reported earlier this month), Houthi units now hold the Greater and Lesser Hanish islands and the mainland town of Dhubab, giving them control of every position commanding the Bab el-Mandeb strait — a waterway carrying close to a tenth of global trade and 5–7% of seaborne oil. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said this week that passage remains open to all shipping companies “except Saudi ships, which have already been banned.” Al Jazeera reported Friday that Washington appears unwilling to open a new front in Yemen given depleted US military resources and growing domestic opposition to the broader Iran war — a framing this report has not independently corroborated against a second outlet and is labeling accordingly.

Single-reliable-source detail The specific characterization of US reluctance to intervene against the Houthis traces to one outlet (Al Jazeera); the strait takeover itself is corroborated across multiple wire and regional sources including NBC News, NPR, Euronews and the Times of Israel.

Why it matters A non-state actor with control of the region’s second oil-and-container chokepoint, a US president publicly weighing whether to escalate directly against Iran, and a Gulf-leaders meeting four days out are three separate pressure points converging on the same week.
What’s next Tuesday’s Gulf-leaders meeting at the UN General Assembly in New York — the clearest scheduled forcing event for whichever direction the “big decision” takes.

Trump bars CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House, calling their coverage “FAKE NEWS”

The ban, announced Friday on Truth Social, cited a disputed claim that Politico received an “illegal” $8 million government subscription under the Biden administration; reporters from all three outlets were still inside the White House as of Friday afternoon.

President Trump said Friday he is banning CNN, MS NOW (formerly MSNBC) and Politico from the White House “effective immediately,” posting on Truth Social that the outlets “shouldn’t be able to constantly write or report FICTION and LIES” about him. He separately alleged Politico received an approximately $8 million government subscription during the Biden administration that he called “illegal and ridiculous” — federal agencies did purchase Politico Pro subscriptions as part of ordinary service contracts, not editorial grants, a distinction this report is stating plainly rather than repeating the characterization uncritically. Trump warned “other outlets” would “follow” without naming which. The First Amendment issue is real and unresolved: legal analysts widely expect a court challenge, though none had been filed as of Friday afternoon, when reporters from all three outlets remained physically present at the White House.

Why it matters A blanket ban on three national newsrooms’ White House access, if it holds, would be a significant escalation in the administration’s relationship with the press corps, with a likely First Amendment test to follow.

Warren Buffett steps down as Berkshire Hathaway chairman; son Howard takes the seat

“Father Time always wins,” the 96-year-old investor wrote in a letter to shareholders, nine months after handing the CEO role to Greg Abel. Howard Buffett becomes chairman immediately; Abel continues running the company.

Warren Buffett announced Friday he is stepping down as Berkshire Hathaway’s chairman, a post he has held since 1965, taking the title of chairman emeritus and remaining a board member. His son, Howard G. Buffett, 71, a Berkshire director since 1993, becomes chairman immediately. Greg Abel has served as chief executive since the start of the year. “Greg runs the company; Howard will guard its culture and values — both worth more than anything on our balance sheet,” Buffett wrote.

Why it matters The formal end of Buffett’s six-decade chairmanship closes out a leadership transition that began with January’s CEO handoff to Abel, with Howard Buffett’s role explicitly framed around culture rather than operations.

The Week’s Ten Stories

The US-Iran war entered a more dangerous phase, ending with Trump saying he faces a decision to “annihilate” Iran’s regime

A UN mission found reasonable grounds for a US war crime and Iranian crimes against humanity in the same report; Trump spent the week sending contradictory signals about the war’s direction before landing on the starkest framing yet.

The week opened badly: a disputed, lethal strike near Qeshm Island killed one person hours before the war’s first scheduled diplomacy — Iran-GCC talks at Salalah, Oman — was due to start Monday. Those talks were postponed before they began, and Iran’s nuclear chief was separately turned back from a Vienna IAEA meeting the same weekend. Tuesday brought a Pentagon Inspector General report pricing the war at $33.4 billion over five months with a munitions-shortfall warning, contradicting Sec. Hegseth’s public denials, alongside Trump floating renewed talks that Iran’s security chief Rezaei flatly rejected within a day. Wednesday, the House passed a third Iran war-powers resolution 220–204 with a record seven Republican defections, and CENTCOM confirmed destroying two Iranian boats after an attempted seizure of a Navy drone while disputing Iran’s account of a separate tanker incident as a “naval mine.” Thursday’s UN fact-finding mission report found reasonable grounds that a February strike on a school in Minab — more than 150 dead, roughly 120 of them children — was a war crime, in the same document finding Iran’s crackdown on 2025 protests amounted to crimes against humanity. The White House called the council’s findings “unserious nonsense” and said only Iran has committed war crimes; the Pentagon said its own, separate investigation into Minab — which has reportedly reached a softer conclusion of a targeting error — “remains ongoing.” Trump simultaneously claimed “direct” contact with Iran and hope the war is “nearing the end,” a claim Iran rejected within hours. By Friday, he told Axios he faces a “big decision” — “do I want to go in and annihilate them, or do I not?” — to be shaped at Tuesday’s meeting with six Gulf leaders on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly.

Why it matters A UN body naming a likely US war crime and Iranian crimes against humanity in one report, alongside a president publicly weighing outright escalation days later, is a genuinely unusual and dangerous combination this report has not seen at this intensity before.
What’s next Tuesday’s Gulf-leaders meeting at the UN General Assembly — the clearest scheduled event for whichever direction the “big decision” takes.

Houthi forces completed their takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb strait — a second chokepoint under hostile control, with a first confirmed Saudi death by week’s end

A disputed claim of downing a drone aimed at Mecca drew broad condemnation but no military response; Saudi Arabia struck back near Mocha only two days after its own “finish the job” rhetoric, and Washington approved a possible $24.3 billion F-35 sale.

Building on the Sept. 8 Houthi attack that shut Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, Houthi forces completed territorial control of the Bab el-Mandeb corridor this week — Mocha, Perim Island and the Hanish islands all now held — putting a second global chokepoint (roughly 9–12% of world trade) under a hostile, Iran-aligned force alongside Hormuz. The UN put the conflict’s casualties near 2,900 since early August with roughly 94,000 newly displaced. Wednesday, Saudi Arabia claimed it downed a drone aimed at Mecca — Islam’s holiest city, a first alert of its kind since 2017 — which the Houthis called “a worn-out lie.” The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation condemned the claim Thursday, but no military response followed that day; Saudi Arabia’s own coalition did strike Houthi positions near Mocha and Marib Thursday, its first confirmed counterstrikes this report has tracked this week, two days after AP reported (and Turkey and Pakistan both denied) that Riyadh was running low on interceptors and had requested allied air-defense help. Friday brought the war’s first confirmed Saudi death and the State Department’s approval of a possible $24.3 billion sale of up to 48 F-35s to Saudi Arabia, still subject to a 30-day congressional review Israel is expected to contest.

Why it matters A second chokepoint falling under hostile control compounds Hormuz-driven oil risk, and the Mecca-drone claim carries genuine escalation risk toward a target with no precedent for restraint on either side.

The Fed hiked for the first time since 2023, unanimously, over open White House pressure — and the market’s violent first reaction fully reversed within a day

Chair Warsh told his press conference “we stay in our lane” and that fiscal and trade policy should too; sixteen of eighteen officials pencil in at least one more hike this year.

Entering the week, hike odds sat in the mid-80s to low-90s by press and tracker accounts, following hot August CPI and hawkish Jackson Hole rhetoric from Warsh, against open pressure from Trump, Vance, Bessent and Hassett and a dovish holdout in Gov. Waller. Wednesday, the Fed raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75–4.00% — the first hike since July 2023 — unanimously, 12–0, with a genuinely hawkish Summary of Economic Projections: sixteen of eighteen officials see at least one further hike this year, four see two, with a median 2026 dot of 4.1%. Warsh’s press-conference line — “we stay in our lane… we’ll let people that do trade policy and fiscal policy stay in their lane too” — read as a direct, public rebuff of the pressure campaign. Markets sold off hard same-day (the Dow fell 1.21%, financials-led, even though higher rates conventionally help bank margins), but fully reversed by Thursday (S&P +1.14%, VIX −12.8%) once the specific event-risk premium cleared. Trump kept pressuring the Fed through the week — calling for rates “1%, or less” and the FOMC “hostile” — but pointedly spared Warsh personal blame and never responded to the “stay in your lane” line.

Why it matters A hike delivered over open administration objection, by a chair that same administration installed, unanimously and with a hawkish forward path, is a real test of Fed independence the market ultimately treated as credible.

A UN mission found reasonable grounds for a US war crime and Iranian crimes against humanity — then the story nearly vanished from the news cycle

The mission’s findings on the February Minab school strike and Iran’s 2025 protest crackdown drew two days of prominent coverage, no reported congressional reaction, and then dropped out of the top-story rotation.

The UN’s Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran, reporting to the Human Rights Council’s 63rd session Thursday, found reasonable grounds that US forces committed a war crime in a February strike on a school in Minab (more than 150 dead, roughly 120 children, part of a broader campaign the mission says killed at least 2,362 civilians including 383 children), and separately that Iranian security forces committed crimes against humanity suppressing 2025 protests — mass killings across all 31 provinces, torture, tens of thousands arbitrarily detained, 221 children killed, at least 29 men executed since March. The Pentagon’s own preliminary inquiry into the same Minab strike has reportedly reached a softer conclusion — a targeting error from stale intelligence, not a war crime. Government response came late: the White House called the council “unserious nonsense” Thursday/Friday and said only Iran has committed war crimes; the Pentagon said its investigation “remains ongoing.” No congressional reaction was found in any outlet this report checked.

Why it matters A UN human-rights body naming a likely war crime against the country still prosecuting the war — not just its adversary — in the same report as crimes-against-humanity findings against that adversary, is structurally unusual and deserved more sustained coverage than it got; see Overhyped & Undercovered, below.

Missouri’s congressional-map fight resolved for this cycle, closing out a genuine state-vs-federal-court conflict

The full Supreme Court’s Sept. 10 stay of a competing federal order left the state supreme court’s 2022-map ruling as the sole governing order; Secretary of State Hoskins complied and purged his contempt the same day.

Missouri’s Supreme Court had unanimously killed the Trump-backed 2025 map Sept. 3, ordering a November referendum (Proposition A) and reverting to the state’s 2022 lines for this cycle; AG Hanaway’s promised Supreme Court appeal followed and was denied by Justice Kavanaugh around Sept. 8. A separate federal district-court order had ordered the state to use the 2025 map instead, prompting Secretary of State Hoskins to defy the state court and triggering a contempt hearing — but the full Supreme Court stayed that federal order Sept. 10, mooting the standoff; Hoskins complied and purged himself of contempt the same day. As of this week’s editions, the fight remains dormant, with no new filings.

Why it matters A state supreme court invalidating a certified congressional map mid-cycle, resolved by two separate Supreme Court interventions within days, is a live template for the mid-decade redistricting fights playing out elsewhere — worth a check-in near Election Day on the Prop A referendum itself.

What Changed in the World

The Gulf war stopped being a single trackable standoff and became two: a widening US-Iran confrontation now carrying a formal UN war-crimes finding, and a separate Saudi-Houthi war that gave a non-state actor control of a second global chokepoint and produced the conflict’s first confirmed Saudi death. Trump’s own public framing swung within 24 hours from claiming the war is “nearing the end” to weighing whether to “annihilate” Iran’s regime — a genuinely unresolved signal this report is carrying into Tuesday’s Gulf-leaders meeting rather than reading as settled in either direction.

On monetary policy, the Fed answered the week’s central question about its own independence directly: it hiked, unanimously, over open administration pressure, and its chair used his own press conference to publicly rebuff that pressure rather than accommodate it. The market’s violent same-day reaction proved a poor guide to the durable signal — it fully reversed within a day, a pattern this report is now watching for whether it repeats around the Fed’s next meeting.

On AI governance, the gap between industry rhetoric and enforceable consequence widened rather than closed: a public pledge to “pace the frontier” produced the week’s single largest market move (Monday’s AI-infrastructure selloff) and days of escalating congressional rhetoric, but zero compelled testimony, no scheduled hearing and no legislation actually moved. Institutionally, two separate proceedings this week — the Kennedy Center standoff and Missouri’s redistricting fight — showed courts managing high-profile executive-branch confrontations by deferring or narrowing the central question rather than resolving it outright, a pattern worth carrying into how this report frames similar forecasts going forward.

Big bank stocks had a rough week: Bank of America projected third-quarter investment-banking fees of $1.6–1.8 billion Tuesday, well below the roughly $2.04 billion Wall Street had expected, and the warning rippled across the sector — Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley each fell sharply Monday and again Tuesday. Financials absorbed a second hit Wednesday when Chair Warsh’s press conference read hawkish; Goldman Sachs finished the week down roughly 8.5%, Bank of America roughly 7.9%, Morgan Stanley roughly 5.5% and Citigroup roughly 5.1% — a confirmed catalyst (BofA’s own guidance) compounded by a likely contributor (the Fed’s hawkish tone), rather than one clean story. Constellation Energy, which sold off hard in last week’s AI-infrastructure pullback, only partially recovered and finished the week down roughly 10.6%, the tracked watchlist’s worst performer — graded against this report’s own recovery forecast in the Scorecard below. On the other side of the ledger, Coinbase (+10.8%), AeroVironment (+9.0%) and several AI-infrastructure and space names (AMD, Micron, Marvell, Rocket Lab-adjacent small caps) posted the watchlist’s strongest weekly gains, detailed in The Week in Markets below.

The Week in Markets

The S&P 500 finished essentially flat on the week, down 0.08%: 7,656.98 (Fri Sept 11) to 7,650.50 (Fri Sept 18). The Nasdaq Composite rose 0.72% (26,333.04 to 26,522.54); the Dow fell 1.69% (52,573.29 to 51,682.64); the Russell 2000 fell 1.50% (2,903.94 to 2,860.40) — small caps and the bank-heavy Dow underperformed a tech-led Nasdaq. The net numbers hide a real path: the S&P fell three straight sessions into Wednesday’s Fed decision (Mon −0.48%, Tue −0.45%, Wed −0.45%), then rallied sharply Thursday (+1.14%) on no single confirmed catalyst this report can point to, before drifting up a further 0.17% Friday.

Sector leadership was mixed rather than clean risk-on or risk-off: health care (XLV) led at +1.83% and technology (XLK) added 1.03%, while utilities (XLU) lagged at −3.04%, real estate (XLRE) fell 2.05%, materials (XLB) fell 1.88% and financials (XLF) fell 2.43% — a sector tape better explained by the bank-specific selloff (above) and rate-sensitive-sector weakness than by a single market-wide narrative. Growth outperformed value on the week (Vanguard Growth ETF +0.83% vs. Vanguard Value ETF −1.21%), and large caps outperformed small (QQQ +0.92% vs. the Russell 2000’s −1.50%) — both consistent with a week where mega-cap tech and health care carried the index while banks and rate-sensitive sectors dragged.

Rates rose on net despite Friday’s pullback: the 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.01% intraweek (its highest level this report has tracked since at least last week’s 4.96%) before easing to 4.94% by Friday’s close, essentially flat to last week; the 2-year eased slightly to 4.76% (par curve) from 4.67% the prior week’s FRED reading (curve-basis figures; see Market Appendix for the full path), leaving 2s10s at roughly 25 basis points. High-yield spreads ticked up to a midweek peak of 2.76% before easing back to 2.70% by Friday — a round trip rather than a clean widening. Oil was roughly flat on the week: WTI $100.30 (+0.25%), Brent $103.87 (−0.71%) — a notable pause after prior weeks’ Gulf-driven surge, even as the Houthi takeover of Bab el-Mandeb (above) would ordinarily read as bullish for a key chokepoint risk. Gold rose modestly (+0.36% to $4,424.90) and silver rose more sharply (+3.10% to $66.56); the dollar index (DXY) rose 1.11% to 100.22, a hawkish-Fed-consistent move even as EUR/USD fell 0.89%. VIX closed the week at 14.81 (Fri Sept 18), down from 15.84 the prior Friday, but the path was volatile: an intraweek high near 18.94 Wednesday around the Fed decision, then a sharp retreat to 14.81 by Friday — one of the calmer closes this report has tracked recently, with VIX9D (12.27) below both VIX and VIX3M (18.24), ordinary contango rather than the near-inversion seen some prior weeks. Bitcoin rose sharply into the weekend, last near $81,236 (+4.19% in the past 24 hours per CoinGecko), recovering a meaningful share of the prior week’s crash (bitcoin fell as much as 4.6% and Coinbase 10.1% in a single Sept. 15 session, per Monday’s edition) even as Coinbase shares themselves finished the week up 10.8%, the watchlist’s best performer.

Whole-market breadth (Massive grouped-daily, EMA proxy still accumulating history) was negative on four of five sessions: Monday 2,302 advancers vs. 3,079 decliners, Tuesday 1,566 vs. 3,882 (the week’s weakest reading, coinciding with the sharpest bank and AI-adjacent weakness), Wednesday 1,952 vs. 3,441 on the Fed decision itself, Thursday a rare positive session at 3,059 vs. 2,337 alongside the S&P’s best single day, and Friday back to negative at 1,990 vs. 3,506 despite the index closing higher — a genuine divergence this report is flagging rather than resolving: Friday’s gain looks narrower under the hood than the headline index move suggests.

Scorecard

Thirty-six forecasts came due this week: 19 correct, 14 wrong, 3 partial.

This report’s own four-scenario Sunday matrix missed on its differentiating details for a third straight week. The base case (a measured, one-and-done hike; Salalah producing no deal but no collapse; the S&P within 2% of the prior Friday) graded wrong: the Fed was genuinely hawkish, not measured, and Salalah collapsed entirely rather than merely stalling — only the coincidental fact that the S&P’s Friday close landed in range kept this from being a clean miss on every count. The bull case graded wrong on every leg: the Fed signaled more hikes, oil never eased under $95, and any rally was concentrated rather than broad. The bear case graded partial — it correctly called the hawkish hike, hot retail sales and rising yields, but inverted the market reaction: equities recovered sharply rather than giving back gains. The shock case correctly stayed the lowest-probability scenario and correctly did not fire. Worth restating plainly: a scenario matrix whose headline market-level number lands in range while its underlying story diverges sharply is not a hit, and this report is grading it accordingly rather than taking the easy read.

The Fed-hike calls were this report’s most reliable forecasting thread this week — when they called the hike itself. Five separate forecasts across the week correctly called a 25bp hike at Wednesday’s meeting. But every forecast on the hike’s tone missed: both this report’s Monday and Tuesday forecasts that the Fed would signal a measured, one-and-done path graded wrong against a dot plot showing sixteen of eighteen officials expecting further tightening. Individual policymakers’ pre-vote conditioning (Waller, reported Bessent softening) proved a worse guide to the committee’s actual center of gravity than this report weighted it.

Gulf-conflict forecasts were strong on direction, weaker on specifics. Correct: a further direct Iran-US exchange within the week; Iran striking a US-linked target (the Jordan base) within Ghalibaf’s threatened window; Houthi strikes on Saudi cities within five days of the Mocha airstrikes; no Gulf state commenting on the Kharg Island exchange through its horizon; and, on a full-month horizon, further Iran-linked Hormuz incidents (graded correct, though this report’s own August probability estimate of 20–35% significantly undersold how likely this was once the conflict’s actual pace became clear). Wrong or only partial: whether Monday’s Salalah talks would merely be “thin” rather than fully collapse; whether the Qeshm attribution dispute would harden into a formal accusation exchange (it simply faded instead); whether Saudi Arabia would retaliate against Houthi positions within a week of the Sept. 8 attack (it took ten days); and whether a further Gulf incident would produce independently confirmed casualties on a US or allied asset by Friday (still unresolved, graded partial rather than either outcome).

Two forecasts on this report’s own institutional-confrontation predictions graded wrong in the same, instructive way. Neither the Missouri contempt-hearing forecast (which anticipated a clean either/or outcome) nor this week’s Kennedy Center forecast (which anticipated escalation toward contempt) accounted for the actual, most common resolution in both cases: a court or official finding a third path — compliance in Missouri’s case, a deferred sworn status report in the Kennedy Center’s. This report is treating that as a pattern to weight more heavily in future forecasts on live legal confrontations, not a coincidence.

The AI-oversight and CPI/PPI forecasts held up well. Correct: hot August CPI above 3.0% year over year; hot Sept. 10 PPI; both OpenAI and Anthropic missing Tuesday’s deadline; and Casar publicly escalating to demanding sworn testimony. Wrong: whether OpenAI would issue an on-record response to a specific Congressional-testimony allegation (it never did). Partial: whether the AI-infrastructure basket would recover at least half of Monday’s losses within three sessions — true for SMH and Marvell, but Constellation Energy, the forecast’s own named exception risk, proved the rule-breaker rather than the rule, finishing the week down 10.6%.

Overhyped & Undercovered

Overhyped. The Mecca-drone claim led or near-led two straight editions with dramatic framing, but by Thursday this report’s own coverage made clear the claim drew condemnation and explicitly no military response — the story’s actual trajectory undercut its initial billing. The AI labs’ “pacing the frontier” pledge drove the week’s single largest market move and days of coverage, but by Friday nothing concrete had changed: no verified evaluator access, no demonstrated capability slowdown, and zero legislative action on either AI bill introduced that week. The FOMC “one-and-done vs. hawkish” drama consumed enormous forecasting energy of this report’s own; the market’s violent first-day reaction to the actual decision fully reversed within 24 hours, suggesting the initial shock framing was itself overstated.

Undercovered. The UN war-crimes mission’s findings — a UN body finding reasonable grounds for a US war crime and Iranian crimes against humanity in the same report — got two days of prominent coverage and then dropped from the top-story rotation, with no reported congressional reaction found anywhere and no exploration of what a referral process might actually look like. The DRC’s Ebola outbreak, described repeatedly as the fastest-growing on record, climbed from roughly 7,200 to 7,404 confirmed cases and 3,577 deaths this week and never received more than a short paragraph in any edition. Friday’s edition buried a sharp upward revision to Nepal and Tibet’s flood toll — roughly 1,403 dead and 6,150 missing in Nepal alone, plus 43 dead and 519 missing in Tibet, an order-of-magnitude jump from earlier reporting — in a single flagged paragraph. A gold-mine collapse in Sudan that killed dozens, in a country already facing famine conditions affecting an estimated 24.6 million people, got one short item Friday with no planned follow-up.

Pennsylvania

PUC opens a broad review of utility ratemaking, citing affordability and data-center demand

The Pennsylvania Public Utility Commission voted 5-0 to create a Ratemaking Working Group examining return-on-equity methodology, transparency in rate-case settlements and how Distribution System Improvement Charge returns are set, and separately directed staff to draft updated emergency electric load-control rules and plan a technical conference on allocating costs tied to large loads, including data centers. The commission cited rising utility costs, growing customer arrearages and data-center-driven demand growth as the backdrop.

Pennsylvania PUC (primary) · Daily Energy Insider.
