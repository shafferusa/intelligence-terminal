The Baghdad Scholar Who Taught the World to Solve for X

Tuesday, September 29, 2026 · 15 min read

A ninth-century Baghdad textbook gave mathematics its name, algebra, and something more valuable than the name: a general method for finding an unknown number, the same method that collapses today into a single formula you can carry in your head.

Mathematics

Day 2 of 300 · Mathematics 2 of 20 · Algebra: variables, equations, and solving for the unknown

The Big Idea

Arithmetic answers a question once every number in it is already known: what is 15 plus 27? Algebra answers a different kind of question: what number, combined with some known numbers in some known way, produces a known result? That shift — from computing with numbers you already have to reasoning about a number you don’t — is the entire subject in one sentence. The unknown gets a name, usually a single letter such as x, called a variable: a symbol standing for a number that is not yet known, or that is free to change. The rule connecting it to the known numbers becomes an equation: a statement that two expressions have exactly the same value, joined by an equals sign.

Today’s lesson covers the two simplest and most useful shapes an equation can take. A linear equation is one where the unknown appears only to the first power — nothing squared, nothing multiplied by itself — and in its simplest form looks like ax + b = 0. A quadratic equation lets the unknown’s highest power reach 2: ax² + bx + c = 0, with a not zero (drop that condition and it collapses back to linear). Solving either means finding every value of the unknown that makes the equation a true statement — every root, or solution. A linear equation ordinarily has exactly one; a quadratic equation can have up to two.

The method for finding those roots did not arrive as one finished idea. It took roughly two thousand years to go from clever one-off tricks, each solving a single numerical puzzle, to a fully general procedure that works on every equation of a given shape, whatever specific numbers happen to fill it in. The scholar usually credited with making that second leap explicit — and who gave the whole subject the name it still carries — worked in ninth-century Baghdad, and today’s lesson is built around exactly how he did it.

How It Works

People were solving equations, in the loose sense, long before anyone wrote one down the way you would today. Clay tablets from the Old Babylonian period, roughly 1800 BCE, record problems that are, in modern terms, quadratic: given some combination of a square field’s area and its side, find the side. But the tablets don’t state a method the way a modern textbook would. They walk through the arithmetic for one specific numerical case, step by step, the way a recipe walks through one specific dish — no letter standing in for the unknown, and no claim, stated or implied, that the same steps would still work if the numbers changed. Whether the scribes who wrote them privately understood the procedure as something reusable, or simply as the answer to that one field, is a question historians of mathematics still debate; either way, nothing in the tablets themselves generalizes.

A real step toward abstraction came about two thousand years later, from the mathematician Diophantus of Alexandria, working in Roman-era Egypt around 250 CE. His surviving text, the Arithmetica, is sometimes credited as the true starting point of algebra, and Diophantus himself is sometimes called its father — a title this lesson’s Baghdad scholar also claims, on different grounds. Diophantus wrote in what historians call syncopated algebra: still overwhelmingly words, but with standing abbreviations for the unknown quantity and its powers, rather than spelling every term out fresh each time. That is a genuine move toward a symbol like x. What Diophantus did not do is present general methods as methods. The Arithmetica is a long sequence of specific numeric puzzles, each cracked with its own particular trick, usually restricted to positive, rational answers — closer to a very clever puzzle book than to a textbook of technique.

The figure who made that missing move — general technique, stated as technique, detached from any one puzzle — was Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a Persian scholar working in Baghdad’s House of Wisdom, a library and research institution patronized by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma’mun. Sometime around 820 CE, al-Khwarizmi completed a treatise whose title, translated, is roughly The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing — in Arabic, al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wa’l-Muqabala. Two words in that title did more work than their author could have guessed. Al-jabr means roughly “restoration” or “completion”: the step of moving a subtracted quantity to the other side of an equation, turning it positive again so the equation is “restored” to balance. Al-muqabala means “balancing”: canceling equal terms that appear on both sides. Centuries later, transliterated into Latin and then English, al-jabr became the word algebra. (A related-sounding word, “algorithm,” is not from this book at all — it comes from “Algoritmi,” the Latinized form of al-Khwarizmi’s own name, attached to medieval translations of a separate treatise of his on arithmetic with Hindu-Arabic numerals. Same man, two different books, two different words, a coincidence that trips up even careful readers.)

What actually earns al-Khwarizmi his reputation is not the vocabulary. It is the shape of the book itself. Rather than working through a string of unrelated puzzles the way Diophantus had, al-Khwarizmi organized every quadratic and linear equation into six standard types — a sorting that looks strange today but followed directly from one constraint he worked under: his mathematics had no negative numbers and no zero coefficients, so “a square plus a number equals some roots” and “a square equals some roots plus a number” were genuinely different situations, not two signs of the same equation. The six types, in his own terms: squares equal roots; squares equal a number; roots equal a number; squares and roots equal a number; squares and a number equal roots; and roots and a number equal squares. (“Roots” is al-Khwarizmi’s word for what this lesson calls the unknown — a square’s root, in the sense that the square is literally the unknown multiplied by itself.) For each of the six, he gave a rule, in prose, for finding the answer — and then a geometric proof, built from actual squares and rectangles drawn on the page, showing why the rule works. Follow that same rule on any equation of that type, with any numbers in it, and it produces the right answer every time. That is the leap: not a cleverer trick, but a procedure that no longer cares which specific numbers you feed it.

The geometric proof behind those rules is, in modern language, exactly the technique called completing the square — the same technique this lesson’s Equation and Model section below builds into a single formula. Al-Khwarizmi demonstrated it by literally drawing a square standing for the unknown’s square, attaching rectangles to its sides standing for the “roots” term, and then adding a small corner square to complete a larger, tidy square whose area he could calculate directly — geometry standing in for algebra a symbol had not yet been invented to write.

Notice what al-Khwarizmi’s book does not contain: no x, no plus sign, no equals sign. Every equation and every step of every solution is written out in full sentences — what historians call rhetorical algebra, one full step up from Diophantus’s abbreviations, but still prose, not symbols. That was very likely deliberate rather than a limitation: the book was written for a broad, practical audience — merchants, surveyors, and officials settling inheritance disputes under Islamic law, all applications al-Khwarizmi discusses directly — and prose needed no specialized training to follow. The symbolic shorthand this lesson uses without a second thought, ax² + bx + c = 0, was still roughly seven centuries away.

It arrived in two deliberate steps. In 1591, the French mathematician François Viète published a short treatise, In Artem Analyticem Isagoge, that did something no one had systematically done before: used letters for both the known and the unknown quantities in the same equation — consonants for the knowns, vowels for the unknowns. Nearly half a century later, in 1637, René Descartes’s La Géométrie settled on the convention still used today: letters from the start of the alphabet (a, b, c) for known quantities, letters from the end (x, y, z) for unknowns. Between them, Viète and Descartes turned al-Khwarizmi’s six rhetorical rules into something that could be written as a single symbolic line.

One more piece completed the picture. In 1594, the Flemish mathematician and engineer Simon Stevin published a general solution covering every one of al-Khwarizmi’s six cases at once, in a single unified treatment. The trick was simply accepting what al-Khwarizmi’s world had refused to: negative numbers as legitimate quantities in their own right. Once subtraction on either side of an equation is just as valid as addition, the six separate “shapes” of quadratic equation stop being six different problems and become one problem with a sign that can go either way — which is exactly why the modern quadratic formula, built in the Equation and Model section below, needs only one line to do the work of al-Khwarizmi’s six rules combined.

Key Concepts

Variable
A symbol, usually a letter, standing for a number that is unknown or free to change — as opposed to a constant, a number whose value is fixed.
Equation
A statement that two expressions have exactly the same value, joined by an equals sign; whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other to keep it true.
Linear equation
An equation in which the unknown appears only to the first power — no squares, no products of the unknown with itself — with simplest form ax + b = 0.
Quadratic equation
An equation in which the unknown’s highest power is 2, with simplest form ax² + bx + c = 0 and a not equal to zero.
Root / solution
A value of the unknown that makes the equation a true statement. A linear equation ordinarily has one; a quadratic equation can have up to two.

Example / Application

Al-Khwarizmi’s own book works through numerical examples of each of his six cases, and one of them — “what must be the square which, when increased by ten of its roots, amounts to thirty-nine” — is as good a place as any to see his method run, translated into the equation it describes: an unknown square, plus ten times the unknown, equals 39. In modern notation, x² + 10x = 39.

Al-Khwarizmi’s rule for this exact shape of problem, followed exactly as he states it in prose, then in numbers:

Halve the number of roots (10): 10 ÷ 2 = 5

Multiply that by itself: 5 × 5 = 25

Add this to the number on the other side (39): 39 + 25 = 64

Take the root (square root) of the sum: √64 = 8

Subtract the earlier half: 8 − 5 = 3

Al-Khwarizmi’s answer is 3, and the unknown square itself — 3² — is 9. Check it against the original problem: 9, the square, plus ten of its root, 10 × 3 = 30, comes to 39. It holds exactly.

Every one of those five steps is completing the square, carried out in words on one specific number rather than in symbols on a general one. The “draw a square, attach two rectangles worth five roots each, add a small corner square of 25 to complete a 64-area square eight units on a side” picture al-Khwarizmi actually drew is the exact geometric reason those five arithmetic steps work — and it is, step for step, the same procedure the modern quadratic formula below carries out symbolically, once and for all, instead of one number at a time.

Equation / Model

Take al-Khwarizmi’s procedure, replace every specific number with a letter, and run it on the fully general quadratic equation ax² + bx + c = 0. What comes out the other end is the single formula that has replaced his six rules ever since Viète, Descartes and Stevin finished the job:

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a

x equals negative b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all divided by two a.

a
the coefficient (multiplier) of x², the unknown squared; must not be zero, or the equation is linear, not quadratic
b
the coefficient of x, the unknown to the first power (al-Khwarizmi’s “roots” term)
c
the constant term — the number with no x attached at all
x
the unknown; the formula gives its value directly, with no further solving needed
±
read “plus or minus”: the formula gives two roots at once, one from each sign, whenever the quantity under the square root is positive

Here is the derivation, following al-Khwarizmi’s own steps but now on letters instead of numbers. Start from ax² + bx + c = 0 and divide every term by a (allowed, since a is not zero), giving x² + (b/a)x + c/a = 0. Move the constant term to the other side: x² + (b/a)x = −c/a. Now complete the square exactly as al-Khwarizmi did — halve the coefficient of x, which is b/a, giving b/2a, then add its square to both sides:

(x + b/2a)² = (b² − 4ac) / 4a²

x plus b over two a, squared, equals b squared minus four a c, all over four a squared.

The left side is now a perfect square by construction, which is exactly what “completing the square” means: x² + (b/a)x + (b/2a)² factors cleanly into (x + b/2a)², while the two terms moved to the right combine, over a common denominator of 4a², into (b² − 4ac)/4a² — the same expression, in symbols, as al-Khwarizmi’s “add the square of half the roots to the number” step. Take the square root of both sides — and here is where the single symbol ± quietly does the work al-Khwarizmi needed six separate rules to do, because a positive number always has two square roots, one positive and one negative, both equally valid:

x + b/2a = ±√(b² − 4ac) / 2a

Subtract b/2a from both sides — al-Khwarizmi’s final “subtract the earlier half” step, done once symbolically instead of once per number — and the two fractions on the right combine over their shared denominator to give exactly the formula stated above: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. The quantity under the square root, b² − 4ac, is called the equation’s discriminant: positive, and the equation has two distinct real roots; exactly zero, and the two roots coincide into one; negative, and you are asking for the square root of a negative number — something al-Khwarizmi’s world had no answer for at all, and the subject of Day 7’s lesson.

Why It Matters

Two things from today carry forward, and both do real work later in this block.

The first is the idea of a general method itself — a procedure you trust because it was derived once, for every case, rather than checked case by case. That is a different kind of confidence from yesterday’s lesson on proof, but a closely related one: al-Khwarizmi didn’t prove the quadratic formula would always work by trying it on enough examples (however many he tried, a stranger equation could always turn up next); he proved it by showing, geometrically, that the completing-the-square construction has to work for any square and any pair of rectangles you attach to it, whatever their actual size. Every later lesson in this Mathematics block leans on methods built exactly the same way — derived once in general, not verified case by case.

The second is where algebra leads next. Today’s equations pin down one number: solve x² + 10x = 39 for a single value of x, and you are done. Let the right-hand side vary instead of sitting fixed at one number, and the equation stops describing a single unknown and starts describing a relationship — y = x² + 10x, one value of y for every value of x you feed in. That relationship is a function, the organizing idea of Day 3, and every one of today’s tools — variables, equations, the vocabulary of roots — carries straight over into it. And the discriminant flagged above, b² − 4ac, going negative is not a dead end; it is the doorway to Day 7’s complex numbers, which give a real, well-defined answer to “what is the square root of a negative number” — a question this lesson’s formula can ask but not yet answer.

Remember These
  • Algebra reasons about an unknown number using a variable and an equation, instead of computing directly with numbers you already have.
  • Al-Khwarizmi’s roughly 820 CE Baghdad textbook gave algebra its name (from al-jabr, “restoration”) and, more importantly, its first genuinely general methods — one rule per equation type, proven geometrically, rather than one trick per puzzle.
  • His six separate rules existed because his mathematics had no negative numbers; accepting negative numbers (Stevin, 1594) collapsed all six into one formula.
  • The quadratic formula is completing the square, done once in symbols instead of once per number — the same five steps al-Khwarizmi wrote out in prose for a single equation.
  • Algebra was written entirely in words for roughly seven centuries after al-Khwarizmi; the symbols x, y, z and a, b, c arrived only with Viète (1591) and Descartes (1637).
Think About It

Al-Khwarizmi’s book was written in prose, on purpose, so that merchants and surveyors with no specialized training could follow it. Viète and Descartes’s symbols made the same mathematics far more powerful and compact — but also far less readable to anyone who hasn’t learned the notation. What do you think a general method gains, and what does it lose, when it moves from being written in ordinary language to being written in symbols only specialists can read?
