Today’s News

Houthi missiles reach Riyadh for the first time; Trump cuts short Camp David, weighs strikes in Yemen

Saudi Arabia’s capital came under direct attack before dawn Saturday for the first time since the war widened in August; the president is now weighing the strike option he has twice declined since Riyadh started asking.

Houthi forces fired what military spokesman Brig. Gen. Yahya Saree called “two successful military operations using a large number of ballistic and cruise missiles and drones” at “sensitive sites” in Riyadh and at Saudi Aramco facilities in Yanbu before dawn Saturday. Saudi Arabia’s coalition said air defenses intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at the capital; a plume of black smoke rose from a fuel-storage depot near King Khalid International Airport. No casualties or damage were confirmed. Houthi media described the strike as retaliation for an earlier Saudi strike on Sanaa. It is the first time Riyadh itself has come under attack since the renewed fighting began, and the first air-raid alert reported in the capital.

Trump cut short a planned weekend at Camp David and returned to the White House Saturday as the administration weighed possible strikes on Houthi positions in Yemen, according to reporting this outlet could not independently corroborate beyond the original account. The State Department separately expanded its Middle East travel guidance for Americans, warning the conflict “has the potential to escalate rapidly.” US officials met Houthi representatives at the embassy in Muscat, Oman over the weekend — contact, not negotiation, so far as either side has said publicly.

Developing, single-source cluster Saudi Arabia struck back at Sanaa early Sunday, reportedly hitting weapons depots. The counter-strike itself is reported by a regional wire and secondary trackers rather than a major international wire at this writing; casualty and damage specifics are unconfirmed and should be treated as provisional until AP or Reuters carries them directly.

This sits on top of a month of escalation this outlet has tracked closely: Houthi forces completed their takeover of the Bab el-Mandeb strait around Sept. 11–12, forcing major shippers onto Cape of Good Hope routes twenty-plus days longer than the strait; Iran’s navy struck a Togo-flagged tanker attempting to transit Hormuz Thursday night; and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has now asked Trump twice for direct US strikes on the Houthis, both times rebuffed in favor of intelligence support only. Trump said last week he has had “direct” contact with Iran and hopes the war is “nearing the end”; Iran’s Supreme National Security Council secretary Mohsen Rezaei disputed that framing on the record, saying Tehran sent conditions through Qatar and is still waiting on Trump’s reply. Vice President Vance said Monday that the war’s “first phase” — destroying Iran’s nuclear program and conventional military capability — is complete, and a “much different phase” begins within a month or two, without specifying whether that means more force or a diplomatic shift.

Why it matters Every prior request for direct US involvement has been declined. A strike on the Saudi capital itself — not a tanker, not a border town — is a different order of provocation, and it lands the same week Trump himself described weighing a “big decision” on whether to move against Iran’s regime directly.
What’s next Watch for any Pentagon briefing, a war-powers notification to Congress, or a Trump statement naming a US strike rather than describing hopes for a deal. Oil (WTI, via the USO proxy on the appendix) is the cleanest market tripwire.

The pattern since August has been escalation the US has stayed adjacent to rather than inside: Saudi Arabia loses territory and takes casualties, asks Washington for direct force, and Washington offers intelligence and diplomacy instead. A strike on Riyadh changes the political cost of continued restraint — both for MBS, who has to explain to his own public why the capital was hit, and for Trump, who has spent weeks describing an end to the war that keeps not arriving. Nothing in the public record indicates a decision has been made either way as of Sunday afternoon ET.

Russia’s first wartime Duma election concludes amid weak turnout and fraud claims, a day after Trump signs a sweeping new Russia sanctions law

Polls close tonight in a vote already showing turnout well below 2021’s and allegations of ballot-stuffing at thousands of stations; the outcome is not in doubt.

Russia’s State Duma election — running Sept. 18–20 and the first since the war began, and the first to include the four annexed Ukrainian territories — concludes with polls closing 8 p.m. Moscow time Sunday (roughly 1 p.m. ET); official results were not yet published as this edition went to press. As of 6 a.m. Moscow time Sunday, turnout stood at 44.75%, below the comparable point in the 2021 election (final turnout 51.7%). Exit polling put United Russia at 53.5%, the Communist Party at 14.5% and the LDPR at 10.1% — consistent with the ruling party easily retaining the constitutional supermajority (300 of 450 seats) it already holds. Independent monitors and Russian journalists reported allegations of ballot-stuffing at roughly 4,500 polling stations; the government has not responded to those specific claims.

Friday, Trump signed the “Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026” — named for the late senator, and passed with large bipartisan margins (86–11 in the Senate, 262–159 in the House) — targeting Russia’s energy and defense sectors and its sanctions-evading “shadow fleet.” Trump’s signing statement was unusually critical of his own bill, calling parts of it “significantly flawed” and objecting to what he described as unconstitutional limits on his authority to negotiate with Moscow.

Why it matters A supermajority outcome was never in doubt; what is worth watching is whether turnout and fraud allegations feed into further Western sanctions rhetoric just as Trump has signed the broadest Russia sanctions bill of the war — over his own public objections to it.

Trump bars CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House

Passes for all three outlets were confiscated or denied over the weekend; the White House Correspondents’ Association is calling for immediate reinstatement.

Trump announced via Truth Social Friday that CNN, MS NOW (formerly MSNBC) and Politico would be barred from the White House, citing what he called “FICTION and LIES” in their coverage. The ban was enforced Saturday: reporters from all three outlets — CNN’s Betsy Klein, MS NOW’s Akayla Gardner and Politico’s Cheyenne Haslett — separately reported being turned away or having credentials confiscated. The White House Correspondents’ Association called the move a First Amendment violation and demanded immediate restoration of access; the affected outlets have signaled they are weighing a legal challenge, without confirming one has been filed.

Crypto gives back Friday’s rally over the weekend

Bitcoin round-tripped roughly $700 lower since Friday’s close as broader altcoins fell further than the majors.

Bitcoin traded at $80,543 as of Sunday early afternoon ET, down 0.82% over 24 hours and down from Friday’s $81,236 read — giving back most of Friday’s 4.19% rally. Ether, Solana and Cardano each fell more sharply, down 2.1–2.9% over 24 hours. Total crypto market capitalization fell to $2.740 trillion from Friday’s $2.795 trillion, a broader-market decline (−4.77% per CoinGecko’s aggregate) steeper than the major-coin moves alone, pointing to weaker breadth beneath bitcoin’s relative stability. Bitcoin dominance held at 58.9%.

Why it matters Friday’s rally had lifted Coinbase (+11.66%) and Robinhood (+9.12%) sharply into the close; a weekend pullback in the underlying asset, if it persists into Monday’s open, sets those two names up for a mismatch between Friday’s equity move and crypto’s own direction since.

DRC’s Ebola outbreak keeps climbing, now past 7,400 cases

The fastest-growing outbreak on record continues to widen even as WHO reports transmission easing in its original epicenter.

The World Health Organization’s most recent update (Sept. 17, the latest available; the next is due Monday) puts the Democratic Republic of Congo’s Ebola outbreak at 7,475 confirmed cases and 3,605 deaths, up 71 cases and 32 deaths from the prior report. Ituri province remains hardest hit, with 5,792 cases and 2,642 deaths across 28 of its 36 health zones. Roughly 905 patients are currently hospitalized in isolation and 1,798 have recovered. Doctors Without Borders launched a new ERVEBO vaccine-effectiveness study among 20,000 frontline health workers Saturday.

Java Sea ferry search shifts to salvage as divers reach the wreck

Crews began an operation to right the capsized hull Friday; the confirmed death toll and the number still missing continue to be reported inconsistently across outlets.

Toll figures vary by outlet A week after the ferry Virgo Transport 8 lost contact with roughly 243 aboard, Indonesian search-and-rescue crews began an operation Friday to right the capsized hull and aid recovery after five days of rough seas blocked underwater search. Divers have retrieved three bodies from inside the wreck; roughly 126 people remain missing. Total confirmed dead across the full search has been reported inconsistently between six and nine depending on outlet and whether earlier recoveries are combined with the divers’ more recent finds — this outlet is not resolving that discrepancy until an official cumulative count is issued.

The Economy Ahead

Last week’s FOMC decision set the frame: a unanimous 25bp hike to 3.75–4.00%, the first since 2023, with the dot plot’s median 2026 year-end projection rising to 4.125% from 3.8% in June and 16 of 18 officials seeing room for at least one more move. Chair Warsh’s tone — inflation risks “tilted upward,” a preference for trends over noisy releases — read to markets as more hawkish than the dot plot itself; the 10-year pushed above 5% in response and has stayed there. This week has no scheduled Fed decision (the next FOMC meeting is Oct. 27–28, and minutes from the Sept. 15–16 meeting are not due until early October), so it functions as a digestion week: Wednesday’s flash Manufacturing and Services PMIs (consensus 53.4 and 56.0) and Friday’s durable goods orders are the clearest incoming tests of whether the hawkish path is justified by the data actually arriving.

A notably dense Fed-speaker slate runs through the week — Goolsbee, Williams, Jefferson, Barkin and Vice Chair for Supervision Barr are all scheduled, per secondary aggregation this run could not fully verify against the Federal Reserve’s own calendar page, which returned no machine-readable schedule. Wall Street itself is split on what comes next: some banks (JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, Deutsche Bank) expect a second hike in December, while at least one house has flagged a cut as far out as next June. A market-implied probability near 55% for a further near-term hike surfaced in this run’s research from a single source and should be checked against CME FedWatch directly before it appears in a future edition as anything more than an estimate.

Thursday’s jobless claims (consensus roughly 201,000 against last week’s 196,000) and new home sales, and Friday’s durable goods and final Michigan sentiment, round out the calendar. None of it lands with the weight of a CPI or payrolls print, but coming one week after a hawkish surprise, each has more capacity to move the rate path than it would in an ordinary week.

Scenarios

Base case. The Houthi-Saudi war continues to escalate incrementally without direct US strikes; Trump’s Muscat contacts continue without a public breakthrough; markets digest a mixed data week without a fresh hawkish or dovish surprise; Costco reports in line to a modest beat; the UN General Assembly produces rhetoric and side meetings but no breakthrough on Ukraine or the Gulf. Indicators this holds: no Pentagon briefing on strike options, PMIs landing near consensus, oil holding its recent range.

Bull case. A genuine diplomatic opening emerges — Iran engages substantively via the Qatar channel, or the Muscat contacts produce a Houthi-Saudi de-escalation step. Oil eases, risk assets extend September’s AI-infrastructure-led gains, and a Trump–Zelensky UNGA meeting materializes with at least a symbolic outcome. Confirmers: a named diplomatic session, any confirmed ceasefire-adjacent statement from Iran or the Houthis. Invalidators: any further strike inside Saudi Arabia.

Bear case. Flash PMIs and durable goods both surprise hot, reinforcing October-hike pricing; the 10-year pushes further above 5%, credit spreads widen meaningfully, and already-weak breadth (space names, small caps, REITs) worsens without a broader market crack. Confirmers: PMI prints above consensus, HY OAS through 3.00%. Invalidators: a soft data surprise or a dovish Fed-speaker tone shift.

Shock case. The US enters the Houthi-Iran conflict directly — strikes in Yemen or against Iranian assets. Oil spikes sharply, equities sell off broadly, and Treasurys likely rally on flight-to-safety even against the hawkish Fed backdrop, a genuine cross-current worth watching if it happens. Gold and the dollar both likely gain. Confirmers: a Pentagon strike announcement or war-powers notification. This scenario would immediately supersede everything else in this edition.

What would change the outlook. Any confirmed US strike decision on Iran or Houthi targets; a hot flash-PMI surprise; a genuine Trump–Zelensky meeting with substance beyond a photo; or a credit-spread break through levels that would mark the first real stress signal since the Fed hiked.
