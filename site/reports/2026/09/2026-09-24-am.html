Trump and Xi meet today after an overnight trade-truce extension and a new AI-safety channel

The two leaders hold their main bilateral meeting, a Rose Garden ceremony and a state dinner this afternoon and evening; the $14 billion Taiwan arms package China wants shelved is still unresolved going in.

Overnight, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced that the US and China had agreed to extend their “Busan Agreement” trade truce — due to expire November 10 — through January 10, 2027, after an unscheduled meeting between Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng in Washington. The two sides also agreed to open a communications channel for discussing significant AI-safety incidents, the first concrete outcome of Xi’s three-day state visit, his first to Washington in over a decade.

Today’s schedule brings the substance of the visit: a South Lawn arrival ceremony, the two leaders’ main bilateral meeting, and an evening state dinner in the East Room, with tech executives including Jeff Bezos, Sundar Pichai, Sam Altman, Tim Cook, Elon Musk and Jensen Huang reportedly invited. The unresolved flashpoint is a pending $14 billion Taiwan arms package: Reuters reports Xi is expected to press Trump to shelve it, citing the 1982 US-China communiqué’s language on reducing arms sales to Taiwan over time, while the US side can point to the same year’s declassified “Six Assurances,” which commit to no set end-date and no prior consultation with Beijing. Neither the trade-truce extension nor the AI-safety channel bears on that question, which remains open as this edition goes to press.

Why it matters First Xi visit to the White House in over a decade, and the third Trump-Xi meeting in eleven months; the trade-truce extension removes an immediate cliff, but the summit’s central substance — Taiwan, chip export controls, rare earths — is still to come.
What’s next The bilateral meeting, ceremony and dinner all happen today. Treat any claim about the Taiwan package’s outcome as unverified until a joint statement or readout is issued.

Judge orders White House to restore CNN, MS NOW and Politico’s access

A 14-day restraining order finds the administration likely violated due process in revoking the outlets’ credentials without notice or a hearing.

US District Judge Timothy Kelly ruled early Thursday, granting CNN, MS NOW and Politico a 14-day temporary restraining order requiring the White House to immediately restore their hard-pass credentials. Kelly — a Trump appointee who ruled for CNN’s Jim Acosta on nearly identical due-process grounds in 2018 — found the outlets likely to succeed on the merits: credentials were revoked without notice, without an opportunity to be heard, and without pre-existing standards for revocation. He also found the administration’s national-security justification, which cited coverage of the Iran war and White House ballroom construction, unsupported by the record before him.

The ruling is preliminary, not a final decision on the underlying constitutional claim; Judge Kelly set a schedule for further briefing over the 14-day window. It is not yet reported whether the administration will comply immediately or seek emergency appellate relief.

What’s next Compliance and any appeal are the first test; the due-process claim itself still has to be litigated on the merits.

Russia hits Ukraine with one of its largest barrages in weeks

Overnight strikes killed at least six in the Kharkiv region, directly undercutting Zelensky’s push for a Trump-brokered three-way meeting with Putin.

Russia launched more than 280 drones plus ballistic and, Ukraine says, anti-ship Zircon missiles at Ukraine overnight into Thursday; Ukraine’s military says it intercepted 227. A strike on an agricultural storage facility in Stara Hnylytsia, in the Kharkiv region, killed six people and wounded twelve. Kyiv itself was hit by ballistic missiles in three separate waves; Zelensky put the trailing 24-hour national toll at 16 dead and 59 injured, with residential buildings, a maternity hospital and energy infrastructure among the targets hit over the two most recent nights combined. Single-sourced detail The specific claim that Russia used anti-ship Zircon missiles against inland targets comes from Ukrainian military sources alone; Zircon is nominally an anti-ship, hypersonic system, which is reason enough to flag the detail rather than repeat it as settled. Casualty totals across the two nights have not been independently reconciled outside Ukraine’s own reporting and should be read as approximate.

The barrage lands directly against Zelensky’s own diplomacy: he has proposed a Trump-brokered trilateral meeting with Putin, which Kremlin spokesman Peskov dismissed Wednesday as a “waste of time” absent prior expert-level talks, while reaffirming that Putin still expects Zelensky to travel to Moscow instead. Separately, the Kremlin said it welcomes a US invitation for Putin to attend December’s G20 summit in Miami — a lower-tier diplomatic thread, and not a confirmed attendance.

Why it matters The scale of the overnight barrage is hard to square with any near-term de-escalation track, even as diplomatic proposals keep circulating.

Kennedy Center’s status report cites storm damage, not demolition, for its closure

A court-ordered filing describes ceiling collapse, water intrusion and unsafe rigging as the reason the building is shut — a narrower rationale than the demolition talk that had driven the public fight.

The Kennedy Center’s court-ordered status report and a sworn declaration from executive director Matt Floca, filed Wednesday, describe specific storm-related life-safety hazards — a partial ceiling collapse, water intrusion into electrical transformers, and unsafe theater rigging and equipment — tied to a September 4 severe-weather event, as the actual basis for the building’s closure. The filing contains no recommendation to demolish the building, a narrower rationale than the demolition framing that had driven weeks of public controversy after an AFP photo showed Trump aboard Air Force One viewing a “Kennedy Center DEMOLISHED” poster.

Separately, Judge Christopher Cooper declined a request from board member Rep. Joyce Beatty for an emergency hearing on the board’s renewed push to add Trump’s name to the building, but the standing order requiring 30 days’ written notice before any change to renovation plans — explicitly including demolition — remains in force. No reopening date has been given; the closure is being reassessed weekly. Beatty’s counsel has said they will “likely soon” seek a receivership over the building.

What’s next Watch for a receivership filing, and for whether the weekly reassessment produces a reopening date.

A second Fed official calls another hike likely, as the 10-year holds near a 19-year high

NY Fed President Williams says a further rate increase by year-end is “reasonable,” echoing Governor Barr two days after a hot PMI and a weak Treasury auction sent yields to their highest since 2007.

NY Fed President John Williams said Wednesday it would be “reasonable” to expect another rate hike by year-end, calling it “likely” appropriate — while stopping short of committing to an October move, saying the Committee needs more incoming data first. He separately said the era of explicit forward guidance is “over,” echoing an approach Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has taken since replacing Jerome Powell in May. Williams is the second Fed official in 48 hours to flag further tightening, after Governor Michael Barr said Tuesday that additional hikes are “likely to be needed.”

The comments land two sessions after Tuesday’s flash PMI (composite 58.4, the fastest growth in five-plus years) and Wednesday’s weak five-year Treasury auction (2.212 bid-to-cover, the weakest since December 2018, with a 3.1-basis-point tail) pushed the 10-year Treasury yield to 5.11% on the official par curve, its highest close since July 2007. The 2s10s spread has widened to +26 basis points and the 10-year TIPS yield, a proxy for real rates, sits near 2.63% — both consistent with a market pricing a more persistent tightening path rather than repricing a single data point.

Why it matters Two consecutive Fed officials naming another hike as likely, within 48 hours of each other, is a more consistent signal than either comment alone; this outlet is not citing new market-implied hike odds beyond what has already been reported, since a live pull of futures-implied pricing was not part of this run’s gather.
What’s next Today’s 7-year Treasury note auction ($44B, 1:00 PM ET) is the next demand test in a week that already produced the weakest 5-year auction since 2018.

Anthropic’s IPO marketing reportedly slips to November

Multiple outlets now describe a listing target a month later than the October date reported in July, timed to post third-quarter results first.

Multiple outlets — Calcalist/Ctech, Forbes, the-decoder and BigGo Finance — now report that Anthropic has pushed its IPO investor marketing to mid-October, with an actual listing targeted for November, a month later than the October target reported this summer. Meta unveiled a palm-sized “Muse Charm” hardware device for its Muse AI agent on Wednesday. Critical F5 zero-day’s federal patch deadline lands tomorrow

CISA’s remediation order for the actively exploited BIG-IP flaw expires Friday; no named victims have surfaced yet.

CVE-2026-94127, a critical (CVSS 9.8) heap-based buffer overflow in F5’s BIG-IP Access Policy Manager that allows unauthenticated remote code execution when APM is configured as an OAuth authorization server, remains under active exploitation. F5 has shipped hotfixes for the affected 17.1.x, 17.5.x and 21.x branches, plus a temporary iRule-based mitigation, after CISA added the flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog Tuesday with a Friday, September 25 federal remediation deadline. Security researchers are tracking more than 14,700 internet-facing hosts carrying a BIG-IP APM fingerprint, not all of them necessarily vulnerable or unpatched.

What’s next Tomorrow’s federal deadline. No attacker has been named, and no source has disclosed a specific victim as of this edition.

DRC’s Ebola outbreak sends a mixed signal: down at its epicenter, up elsewhere

WHO’s latest count tops 7,700 cases, but new infections are falling in Ituri even as North Kivu’s caseload climbs sharply.

The World Health Organization’s most recent figures, as of September 22, put the outbreak — still the fastest-growing Ebola epidemic on record — at 7,773 confirmed cases and 3,759 deaths, roughly 100 more cases and 60 more deaths than two days earlier. For the first time in this outlet’s tracking of the outbreak, the trend is genuinely mixed rather than uniformly worsening: Ituri, the epicenter, has seen new cases fall 26% over the trailing 21 days, and Haut-Uélé is down 15% over the same window, while North Kivu’s caseload has risen 73% in that period — suggesting the outbreak’s center of gravity may be shifting north even as the original hot zone improves.

Why it matters A declining epicenter alongside a sharply rising secondary province is a different, and in some ways more difficult, containment problem than uniform growth.

World Health Organization Disease Outbreak News (primary) · UN News.

The confirmed death toll from the September 13 capsizing of the ferry Virgo Transport 8 in the Java Sea has risen to 28, with 107 of the original 243 people aboard still missing; 108 have been rescued, a figure that has not changed in over a week. Indonesia’s search-and-rescue agency, Basarnas, has extended the operation multiple times, most recently through September 27.
