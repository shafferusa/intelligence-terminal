Costco Collected $5.68 Billion in Membership Fees. Its Books Only Call $5.32 Billion of It Revenue.

Friday, September 18, 2026 · 26 min read

A membership fee, a six-month insurance bill, and a kitchen remodel finished but not yet invoiced are the same problem wearing three different disguises — the moment cash changes hands and the moment value changes hands are two different moments, and this lesson builds the machinery for keeping them straight, tested against a real depreciation schedule and Costco’s own 2025 annual filing.

A membership fee that isn’t revenue yet, and a job that already is

Walk into a Costco for the first time this January, hand over $65 at the membership counter, and walk out twenty minutes later with a cart of Kirkland Signature everything. In your own mental ledger that $65 is spent, done, gone — money that used to be yours and now belongs to Costco, the instant the card cleared. Costco’s own books disagree with you, and they disagree in a very specific, very deliberate way: the company will not call one cent of that $65 “revenue” today. Not on the day it clears your bank account, not on the day you first swipe your card at self-checkout, not for most of the year. Costco’s own accounting policy states it plainly: membership fee revenue is recognized “on a deferred basis, ratably over the one-year membership period” — a little more of it counted as earned with each week that passes, never all at once. Join in January, and by its own accounting Costco is still sitting on more than half of your $65, untouched, as late as July.

Now flip the situation entirely. A general contractor finishes a $40,000 kitchen remodel on the 28th of the month — the cabinets are hung, the client has walked through and signed off, the job is done. The invoice goes out the same afternoon, but the client’s bank does not release payment for another three weeks, comfortably into the following month. The contractor’s own books, kept properly, already show $40,000 of income earned as of the 28th — weeks before a single dollar has actually moved into the business’s account.

Which of these two businesses has it right? Trick question: both do, and neither is bending a rule, rounding a number in its own favor, or hiding anything from anyone. Both are applying the exact same formal distinction, honestly, in opposite directions. The moment cash changes hands and the moment value changes hands are, in general, two different moments — sometimes by days, sometimes by months, sometimes, as day twenty-two’s lesson showed inside a single company’s own filing, by tens of billions of dollars in a single year. Every business, from a Costco warehouse to a one-person contracting outfit, has to make a deliberate, disciplined choice about which of those two moments its books are going to track. This lesson builds that choice properly: what each side actually measures, the four specific shapes the gap between them can take, and the single arithmetic identity, tested here against Costco’s own 2025 annual filing, that guarantees the two views can always be reconciled to the exact dollar.

Two honest answers to two different questions

Strip away every term of art for a moment, because the whole distinction rests on separating two questions that sound almost identical and are not. The first question is: how much richer did this business get during this particular stretch of time — how much more value did it create than it used up? That is what accounting calls profit, or net income, and day twelve and day seventeen of this curriculum already built the two statements, the balance sheet and the income statement, that exist to answer it. The second question is completely different: how much more money is sitting in the business’s bank account now than was sitting there before? That is simply cash, the plainest, least debatable number in the entire filing, and day twenty-two’s lesson built the one statement, the cash flow statement, that tracks it directly.

A business can answer “yes, clearly” to the first question and “not yet” to the second, in the very same month, without a single misstatement anywhere in its books. The contractor from this lesson’s opening created $40,000 of value the moment the remodel was finished and accepted — that part is not in dispute, is not an estimate, and does not depend on when the check arrives. But the contractor cannot deposit “value created.” Payroll, the lumber yard, and the contractor’s own rent all need actual dollars, on actual dates, and “the client owes me $40,000” does not cover a single one of them until it converts into cash. Costco’s new member has the mirror problem from the other side: Costco has your $65 in hand, unambiguously, but it has not yet delivered fifty-two weeks of the thing you paid for — access to the warehouse, the return policy, the whole bundle of privileges a membership year buys — so by its own accounting it has not yet earned the fee, only collected it. Two different, equally honest measurements of two different, equally real things. What accrual and cash accounting disagree about is not whether these numbers are real. It is which one belongs on this period’s income statement.

Two entire systems of bookkeeping, and who gets to choose

Cash-basis accounting is the older and by far the simpler of the two systems, and its rule fits in one sentence: record revenue when cash is received, record an expense when cash is paid, full stop. There is no estimating, no allocating a cost across future periods, and no judgment call about when a performance obligation was satisfied — the bank statement is the accounting system, which is exactly why it is the natural choice for a sole proprietor, a freelancer, or a very small business with a simple enough set of transactions that timing gaps rarely matter.

Accrual-basis accounting asks a harder question of every transaction: not when did money move, but when did the underlying economic event actually occur. On the revenue side, that question already has a name from day seventeen’s lesson — the revenue recognition principle, which says revenue is recognized when a performance obligation is satisfied, roughly when the customer has actually received what it paid for, independent of when payment clears. On the expense side, the traditional name is the matching principle: record an expense in the same period as the revenue it helped produce, and for a cost that does not tie cleanly to any one sale — rent, insurance, an office manager’s salary — record it in the period the underlying benefit was actually used up, again independent of when the cash left the building. Together the two principles are trying to answer this lesson’s first question, “how much richer did the business get this period,” as accurately as the available information allows, even when that means separating the accounting from the bank statement by weeks or months.

Neither principle arrived fully formed. The clearest single statement of the matching idea in American accounting comes from a 1940 monograph, An Introduction to Corporate Accounting Standards, written by the accounting scholars William Andrew Paton and Ananias Charles Littleton for the American Accounting Association. The two had been asked to rework a five-page 1936 committee statement into something more rigorous, and Paton, by his own account, turned around a full first draft within a month; the finished monograph, ready by December of that year, argued that reported income should reflect costs matched against the revenue they helped produce, rather than simply following whichever “particular” gains or losses happened to land within an arbitrary calendar period. It was not a government rule and carried no legal force of its own, but Paton and Littleton’s framing shaped how the accounting profession, and eventually the standard-setters who wrote GAAP, thought about the relationship between revenue and expense for decades afterward — a case, like Taylor’s 1715 theorem in day twenty-four’s lesson, of an idea that needed one clear, usable statement before it could actually be built upon.

It is worth admitting a real boundary here rather than presenting “matching” as a clean, unbreakable rule. Modern standard-setters have grown genuinely wary of leaning on “matching” as free-standing justification for a specific accounting choice, precisely because it can be stretched: if an expense can be waved through simply by asserting it will help produce revenue someday, almost any cost can be talked into an asset instead of an expense, deferred rather than recognized. That is exactly the reasoning WorldCom’s executives leaned on, however dishonestly, when day twenty-two’s lesson found them capitalizing ordinary line costs as if they were long-lived assets. The rule that actually governs today is narrower and stricter than folk-“matching”: a cost only belongs on the balance sheet as an asset, to be expensed later, if it independently meets the formal definition of an asset — a resource the company controls that is expected to produce future economic benefit — not merely because some accountant can tell a story about which future revenue it relates to.

The choice between the two systems is not left entirely to preference. Any company issuing audited financial statements under US GAAP, and any public company reporting to the SEC, must use accrual accounting — Costco’s 10-K and the roastery’s bank loan covenant alike would be unreadable, and legally noncompliant, if built on a cash basis. Tax law draws its own, separate line: under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 (Internal Revenue Code §448(c)), a business — including a C corporation — whose average annual gross receipts over the prior three years fall under a threshold can use the simpler cash method for its federal tax return, even if it keeps accrual-basis books for its lender or its own management. That threshold is inflation-adjusted every year from a 2017 base of $25 million; for the 2025 tax year it stands at $31 million. A great many real, substantial businesses — comfortably too large to be called a “small business” in ordinary conversation — are legally entitled to file cash-basis with the IRS while presenting accrual-basis statements to everyone else, and both filings are simultaneously, entirely honest.

The four shapes a timing gap can take

Every timing gap between cash and accrual reduces to one of exactly four shapes, and they come from crossing two independent yes/no questions: is this a revenue item or an expense item, and did the cash move before the accounting event or after it?

When cash arrives after revenue is earned, accountants call the earned-but-uncollected amount accrued revenue — the contractor’s $40,000, sitting as an account receivable (or, for work that has not even been billed yet, an “unbilled receivable” or “contract asset”) until the client’s payment finally lands. When cash arrives before revenue is earned, the same gap runs in the other direction and gets a different name: deferred revenue (also called unearned revenue) — Costco’s $65, sitting as a liability, because the company has taken your money but still owes you the membership year it was paid for. Symmetrically, on the expense side: when cash goes out before the expense is incurred, that is a prepaid expense (or deferred expense) — six months of insurance premium paid in one lump sum, sitting as an asset until each month’s coverage is actually used up. And when cash goes out after the expense is incurred, that is an accrued expense — a week of wages a business owes its staff for work already performed, sitting as a liability until the next payday actually arrives.

Notice the pattern underneath all four: whichever side arrives first, cash or the underlying economic event, that same side’s entry sits as a receivable or a payable on the balance sheet until the other side catches up to it. That single mechanical fact is what lets the two accounting systems be reconciled exactly, transaction by transaction, rather than merely approximately:

NIaccrual − NIcash = ΔAR − ΔDR + ΔPP − ΔAE
NIaccrual
net income for the period measured on an accrual basis — revenue earned minus expense incurred, regardless of cash timing.
NIcash
net income for the same period measured on a cash basis — cash collected minus cash paid, and nothing else.
ΔAR
the change in accrued revenue receivable during the period — a rise means more was earned than was collected, pushing accrual income above cash income by that amount.
ΔDR
the change in deferred revenue during the period — a rise means more cash was collected than was earned, pulling accrual income below cash income by that amount.
ΔPP
the change in prepaid expense during the period — a rise means more cash was paid out than was actually used up as expense, pushing accrual income above cash income by that amount.
ΔAE
the change in accrued expense payable during the period — a rise means more expense was incurred than was actually paid in cash, pulling accrual income below cash income by that amount.

Look closely at that identity and it should feel familiar: it is exactly the ΔWC term day twenty-two’s cash-flow-statement lesson subtracted as a single lump sum — “the combined change in accounts receivable, inventory, accounts payable and similar accounts,” that lesson called it, without opening the lump up. Today’s equation is that same lump, pried apart into its four named pieces, which is exactly why day twenty-two could reconcile Apple’s $112.010 billion of net income down to its $111.482 billion of operating cash without this lesson first existing to explain why the reconciliation was legitimate in the first place. It was always four separate, individually sensible timing gaps, added together.

Worked example 1 — one month at the roastery, four adjustments, two honest answers

Day twenty-two’s small coffee roastery is back for a second month, and this time every one of the four timing gaps shows up in its books at once. Work out cash-basis income and accrual-basis income for the month using only these four items — every other dollar in and out of the roastery is ordinary cash revenue and cash expense, identical under both systems, and does not change the gap between them.

  1. Accrued revenue. In the last week of the month the roastery delivers a large wholesale order and invoices the client $6,000; payment is due, and expected, early next month. Cash basis: $0 recognized this month. Accrual basis: $6,000 recognized this month, because the coffee was delivered and the performance obligation satisfied. Accrual income is $6,000 higher.
  2. Deferred revenue. A cafe client pays $3,000 up front for a three-month standing weekly-delivery contract; only the first month’s deliveries have actually happened. Cash basis: the full $3,000 counted this month, because that is when it landed in the bank. Accrual basis: only $1,000 counted — one of the three months earned so far — with the remaining $2,000 sitting as deferred revenue on the balance sheet. Accrual income is $2,000 lower.
  3. Prepaid expense. The roastery pays $1,200 in one lump sum for six months of liability insurance covering this month and the next five. Cash basis: the full $1,200 expensed this month, because that is when the check went out. Accrual basis: only $200 expensed — one-sixth of the policy, the portion of the coverage actually used up this month — with $1,000 sitting as prepaid insurance, an asset, to be expensed $200 at a time over the next five months. Accrual income is $1,000 higher.
  4. Accrued expense. Part-time staff work the last week of the month but are not paid until the first payday of the following month; wages earned but unpaid total $2,200. Cash basis: $0 expensed this month. Accrual basis: $2,200 expensed this month, matched against the revenue that week of labor helped produce. Accrual income is $2,200 lower.
  5. Sum the four adjustments using the identity above: NIaccrual − NIcash = ΔAR − ΔDR + ΔPP − ΔAE = $6,000 − $2,000 + $1,000 − $2,200 = +$2,800. For this particular month, accrual-basis net income runs $2,800 higher than cash-basis net income would show for the identical set of underlying transactions — not because either number is wrong, but because they are timed differently, item by item, in four different directions that happen to net out positive this month.

Follow each of those four numbers into the roastery’s next month and the reason accrual accounting is trusted to reconcile exactly, not approximately, becomes visible in practice rather than just in the formula. The $6,000 wholesale invoice, once the client actually pays it, is recorded as a cash collection against an existing receivable — not as new revenue, because the revenue was already recognized last month, so next month’s cash-basis income jumps by $6,000 with no matching rise in accrual-basis income at all, exactly cancelling last month’s gap in the opposite direction. The same happens, on schedule, to the other three: the deferred cafe revenue is recognized $1,000 at a time as each remaining month’s deliveries occur, without any further cash changing hands; the prepaid insurance is expensed $200 at a time with no further cash paid; the $2,200 in wages, once actually paid on the next payday, reduces cash-basis income with no accompanying reduction in accrual-basis income, because that expense was already recognized. Every one of the four adjustments this lesson built is temporary, by construction, and its reversal is not a correction of an error — it is the scheduled second half of the same entry, arriving exactly when the cash side finally catches up to the accrual side.

Depreciation is a prepaid expense stretched across years

Every example so far has resolved itself within a month or two. Stretch a prepaid expense out far enough and it turns into one of the most consequential numbers on any income statement: depreciation. Buy a piece of equipment for cash and, from a purely cash-basis view, the entire cost hit the bank account on the day of purchase, all at once, in a single lump. But under accrual accounting that equipment is going to help produce revenue for years to come, and the matching principle says its cost should be spread across exactly those years rather than dumped entirely on the year of purchase — the same “pay once, expense gradually” logic as the roastery’s six-month insurance policy, just running for five or ten years instead of six months, and governed by a formal schedule instead of simple division.

The simplest such schedule, straight-line depreciation, is exactly that simple division: (cost − salvage value) ÷ useful life, expensed in equal amounts every year. Many companies instead use an accelerated schedule, front-loading more depreciation into an asset’s early years on the reasoning that new equipment tends to be most productive, and most prone to obsolescence, soonest. The most common accelerated method, double-declining balance (DDB), works by a rule that should look immediately familiar from yesterday’s lesson:

BVt = BV0 (1 − 2/n)t      until BVt would fall below salvage value, or straight-line on the remainder overtakes it
BVt
the asset’s book value after t years of depreciation — original cost minus all depreciation expensed so far.
BV0
the asset’s original cost, the book value at t = 0, before any depreciation.
n
the asset’s useful life in years, over which its cost is meant to be fully depreciated.
2/n
the DDB rate — double the straight-line rate of 1/n, applied each year to the declining book value rather than to the original cost.

Depreciation was not always treated this way, and the fight over how to treat it is old enough to predate the income tax. For most of the 19th century American railroads — the era’s most capital-intensive businesses by far, sinking enormous cash into track, bridges and rolling stock meant to last decades — largely used what accounting historians call “betterment accounting”: expense the cost of repairing or replacing worn-out equipment only in the year the repair or replacement actually happened, and otherwise carry the original asset at its full original cost indefinitely, with no annual charge for the wear it was visibly accumulating. That approach is cash-basis reasoning applied to a multi-decade asset, and it let a railroad’s reported income swing wildly from year to year depending on nothing more meaningful than whether a stretch of track happened to need replacing that particular year, while investors had no systematic way to see the asset base quietly wearing out underneath a run of good-looking years. The Interstate Commerce Commission, the railroads’ federal regulator, ordered the industry to abandon betterment accounting in favor of formal annual depreciation charges in June 1907 — a regulator forcing an entire capital-intensive industry onto the accrual view of its own equipment decades before FASB existed to do the same for cash flow statements industry-wide, the parallel day twenty-two’s lesson traced through Penn Central and SFAS 95.

Read that formula again and the shape underneath it is exactly day twenty-four’s geometric series, wearing an accountant’s clothing: BVt = BV0 rt with r = 1 − 2/n, the identical a rn structure that lesson’s Zeno and Archimedes examples were built from, here multiplying a starting value by a fixed ratio, year after year, forever getting smaller and never, on its own, reaching exactly zero. A purely geometric decline approaches its target asymptotically and never quite arrives — which is precisely why accrual accounting cannot leave the schedule running on pure DDB and call it done, and precisely why the formula above carries a stopping condition rather than running forever.

Worked example 2 — a depreciation schedule that has to land exactly on zero

A piece of roasting equipment costs $50,000, with a five-year useful life and zero salvage value. n = 5, so the DDB rate is 2/5 = 40% of the declining book value each year. Compare it, year by year, against straight-line depreciation on whatever book value remains, and switch to whichever method gives the larger expense that year — the standard convention, because it depreciates the asset fastest while still guaranteeing the schedule reaches zero exactly on schedule.

  1. Year 1: DDB = $50,000 × 0.40 = $20,000. Straight-line-on-remainder = $50,000/5 = $10,000. DDB is larger — use it. Book value falls to $30,000.
  2. Year 2: DDB = $30,000 × 0.40 = $12,000. Straight-line-on-remainder = $30,000/4 = $7,500. DDB still larger. Book value falls to $18,000.
  3. Year 3: DDB = $18,000 × 0.40 = $7,200. Straight-line-on-remainder = $18,000/3 = $6,000. DDB still larger, barely. Book value falls to $10,800.
  4. Year 4: DDB = $10,800 × 0.40 = $4,320. Straight-line-on-remainder = $10,800/2 = $5,400. Straight-line has finally overtaken DDB — switch. Depreciate $5,400. Book value falls to $5,400.
  5. Year 5: the entire remaining $5,400 is depreciated, straight-line, in the asset’s final year. Book value falls to exactly $0.
  6. Total depreciation expensed across all five years: $20,000 + $12,000 + $7,200 + $5,400 + $5,400 = $50,000 — exactly, to the dollar, the cash originally paid for the equipment, and not a cent more or less, because the mandatory switch to straight-line forced a geometric process that would otherwise creep toward zero forever to actually reach it, on schedule, in year five.

Rebuilding it on Costco’s own filing

Every figure below is drawn directly from Costco’s fiscal 2025 Form 10-K (filed with the SEC for the year ended August 31, 2025) and from Costco’s own accounting-policy footnote, which states plainly: “The Company accounts for membership fee revenue, net of refunds, on a deferred basis, ratably over the one-year membership period.” That single sentence is this lesson’s deferred-revenue rule, verbatim, out of a real audited filing.

Worked example 3 — how much cash Costco actually collected from members this year

Costco’s income statement reports $5,323 million of membership fee revenue recognized for fiscal 2025 — the accrual-basis number, earned ratably as memberships were used up over the year. Its balance sheet reports deferred membership fees (cash collected but not yet earned) of $2,501 million at the start of the fiscal year and $2,854 million at the end. Use the roll-forward logic behind ΔDR from the identity above to find the one number Costco does not report directly: how much cash it actually collected from members during the year.

  1. A liability roll-forward always balances: beginning balance + cash collected − revenue recognized = ending balance. Beginning deferred fees + Cash collected − $5,323 million = Ending deferred fees.
  2. $2,501 million + Cash collected − $5,323 million = $2,854 million.
  3. Cash collected = $2,854 million − $2,501 million + $5,323 million = $5,676 million.
  4. Check it against ΔDR directly: the deferred membership fees liability grew by $2,854 million − $2,501 million = $353 million over the year — and $5,676 million collected minus $5,323 million recognized is $353 million, exactly matching. Costco took in $353 million more cash from memberships this year than it recognized as revenue, and every dollar of that gap is sitting, visibly, as the increase in a single balance-sheet liability.

Notice the direction of that gap, because it is the opposite of the one day twenty-two’s lesson found. There, Apple’s accrual-basis net income of $112.010 billion ran well ahead of the cash its business actually generated, once genuine capital spending and shareholder returns were accounted for. Here, Costco’s cash collections from membership fees alone ran ahead of the accrual revenue it was willing to recognize for that same money — $5,676 million banked against only $5,323 million called “earned.” Both companies are behaving identically, applying the identical rule; the gap simply points in opposite directions depending on which of the four shapes from this lesson’s taxonomy happens to dominate a given company’s business in a given year.

The direction of that $353 million gap is not just an accounting curiosity; professional analysts covering subscription-style businesses treat the growth rate of a deferred-revenue balance as a leading indicator, because it tends to show up on the balance sheet before the corresponding change reaches recognized revenue. A membership or subscription business collecting cash for periods not yet earned is, in effect, pre-announcing its own near-term revenue every time that liability grows faster or slower than the year before; large enterprise-software companies routinely report deferred revenue and the closely related remaining-performance-obligation figure alongside earnings for exactly this reason. It is a genuine forward signal, with a genuine caveat this lesson’s misconception is about to sharpen: it only holds up if customers actually keep renewing, since a deferred-revenue balance inflated by unusually long prepaid contract terms, rather than by real growth in new and renewing members, can tell the opposite story from the one its headline growth rate seems to suggest.

Where it shows up: the price you actually pay for a bond

Accrual accounting is not confined to corporate filings; it is quietly built into the plumbing of the bond market this newspaper’s own closing editions quote every day. A Treasury note’s quoted market price — the “clean price,” the number that moves on a screen and the one this paper reports as US 10Y or in a fund’s NAV — deliberately excludes something real: the interest that has been quietly accruing, day by day, since the bond’s last coupon payment. A bond pays interest only twice a year, but it earns interest continuously, the same accrued-revenue logic as the contractor’s unfinished invoice, just running on a fixed government calendar instead of a construction schedule.

When a trade actually settles, the buyer does not get to pocket that accrued interest for free simply because the government has not mailed a check yet. Using the Actual/Actual day-count convention standard for Treasury securities, accrued interest is the semiannual coupon payment multiplied by the fraction of the current coupon period that has already elapsed: for a $100,000 face note with a 4% annual coupon (a $2,000 payment every six months) settling 45 days into a 182-day coupon period, accrued interest is $2,000 × (45/182) ≈ $494.51. The buyer pays the quoted clean price plus that $494.51 — the “dirty” or invoice price — and the seller receives it immediately at settlement, in cash, compensating the seller for the interest already earned but not yet paid out by the Treasury. It is accrued revenue changing hands directly between two private parties, settled in real dollars, on a real date, entirely independent of when the actual bond issuer gets around to sending its own coupon.

The misconception

The mistake this lesson exists to correct is assuming the gap between accrual and cash always runs in one predictable direction — usually, the assumption that accrual income is inflated, sitting comfortably above a more “honest” cash number, because that is the shape the most famous examples tend to take. Day twenty-two’s Apple story fits that pattern: $112 billion of reported profit against a mere $6 billion of actual cash growth, a gap that certainly looks, at a glance, like accrual accounting flattering the real number. But that pattern is a fact about Apple’s specific mix of transactions that year — enormous buybacks and dividends drawing cash out through financing activities — not a structural property of accrual accounting itself.

Costco’s own membership-fee line, rebuilt above from Costco’s own filing, runs the other way entirely: $5,676 million of cash collected against only $5,323 million of revenue Costco was willing to call earned. If accrual income were structurally inflated relative to cash, this line could not exist. It exists because deferred revenue — cash arriving before the accounting event, the second row of this lesson’s four-way taxonomy — pulls accrual income below cash income, precisely the opposite direction from accrued revenue or a growing prepaid-expense balance. The identity this lesson built, NIaccrual − NIcash = ΔAR − ΔDR + ΔPP − ΔAE, has two terms that push the gap positive and two that push it negative, and which pair wins in any given period is a fact about that specific business’s specific transactions that quarter — a subscription business collecting cash up front will typically show accrual income below cash income; a project-based business like the opening’s contractor, billing after the work is done, will typically show the reverse. Neither shape is more honest than the other. Reading the direction of the gap correctly, rather than assuming it always runs one way, is the entire skill.

The callback

Day twenty-four’s lesson built the geometric series a + ar + ar2 +… from Bernoulli’s compound interest and Archimedes’ parabola, and it spent real effort on the boundary condition |r| < 1 that keeps such a process from running away to infinity, plus the discipline — Lagrange’s remainder — that turns an infinite, never-quite-finished approximation into a number with a provable, bounded error. Today’s double-declining-balance schedule is the identical mathematical object wearing an accountant’s clothing: a book value multiplied by a fixed ratio r = 1 − 2/n every single year, decaying geometrically toward zero and never, left to run on its own formula forever, actually arriving there — exactly the asymptotic behavior day twenty-four proved a geometric sequence has whenever its ratio sits strictly between −1 and 1. What accrual accounting adds is not a different kind of mathematics; it is the same kind of boundary condition day twenty-four leaned on to make an infinite process usable: a mandatory rule, the switch to straight-line the instant it overtakes DDB, that forces a naturally never-quite-finished geometric decline to land exactly on a known, predetermined target — zero book value, in exactly n years, summing to exactly the original cash paid for the asset, not approximately. And the same discipline scales up past any one asset’s depreciation schedule to the whole of accrual accounting: every accrued or deferred item this lesson named is temporary by construction, guaranteed to reverse itself in a later period once the cash side finally catches up to the accrual side, which means that summed over the entire life of any single transaction — or the entire life of a business, from its first dollar to its last — total accrual-basis income and total cash actually collected minus cash actually paid must converge on the identical number. Accrual accounting does not create profit that cash accounting somehow misses, any more than a geometric series conjures value from nothing; both are exact bookkeeping of the same underlying quantity, arriving at their agreed total on two different schedules.

Where this leaves you
  • You can state precisely what cash-basis and accrual-basis accounting each measure, why GAAP requires the second for any audited filing, and why tax law still permits many real businesses — up to $31 million in average gross receipts for 2025 — to file the first.
  • You can name and recognize all four shapes a timing gap can take — accrued revenue, deferred revenue, prepaid expense, accrued expense — and you have used the identity connecting them to reconcile a month of a small business’s books and a real company’s real 10-K to the exact dollar.
  • You can build a double-declining-balance depreciation schedule, including the mandatory switch to straight-line, and you know precisely why that switch is necessary: a pure geometric decline never reaches zero on its own.
  • You know the direction of the accrual-cash gap is not fixed — Apple’s and Costco’s own filings prove it runs both ways — which sets up a later lesson on earnings quality, where analysts turn the size and direction of exactly this gap into a formal warning signal.
