The Line on Apple’s Balance Sheet That Looks Like a Red Flag

Tuesday, September 1, 2026 · 26 min read

Read one line of Apple’s own audited balance sheet and the company looks unable to cover its bills for the next year — the accounting equation that produces that number, and the reason it doesn’t mean what it looks like it means, is today’s lesson.

Accounting

Day 12 of 150 · Accounting 3 of 18 · Foundations

A company that says it can’t pay its bills

Open Apple’s own annual report to the balance sheet for the fiscal year that ended September 27, 2025, and look at exactly two numbers. The company reports $147.957 billion in assets it expects to turn into cash within the next twelve months — cash on hand, short-term investments, money customers still owe it, inventory sitting in warehouses. Against that, it reports $165.631 billion in obligations coming due in that same twelve months — money it owes suppliers, wages and taxes accrued but not yet paid, deferred revenue on services and warranties it still has to deliver, and the current slice of its long-term debt. Subtract one from the other and Apple is short by $17.674 billion. In the next year, on paper, it owes more than it can currently lay its hands on.

For almost any business on Earth, a banker who found that gap would treat it as an emergency. Working capital — the cushion of near-term assets over near-term obligations — is one of the first things a lender checks, and a negative number is the classic early warning sign of a company about to have trouble making payroll, paying a supplier, or rolling over a loan. Small businesses fail over exactly this kind of shortfall constantly: profitable on paper, unable to cover the bills due this month. And yet nothing about Apple’s finances in fiscal 2025 resembled distress. It ended that same fiscal year with $112.01 billion in net income, more than it had ever earned in a single year up to that point, and it continued borrowing in the bond market and returning cash to shareholders at a pace few companies on the planet can match.

So the same document, read two different ways, tells two contradictory stories: one line says trouble, the whole picture says none. Reconciling those two readings requires knowing exactly what a balance sheet is, what it is not, and what it was never designed to tell you — which is today’s subject, and the third lesson in this curriculum’s Accounting track.

A photograph, not a movie

Lesson seven of this track built the accounting equation and the double-entry system that keeps it true, using it mostly to describe how a single transaction — a sale, a loan, a purchase — gets recorded on both sides of a ledger at once. Today’s lesson is about the document that equation ultimately produces: the balance sheet, the formal statement of everything a company owns, everything it owes, and what is left over for its owners, all measured at one specific moment.

That last phrase — one specific moment — is the single most important thing to understand about a balance sheet, and it is the thing its name is trying, a little obscurely, to tell you. A balance sheet does not describe a year, or a quarter, or any stretch of time at all. It describes a single instant: the close of business on one particular day, most often the last day of a fiscal quarter or fiscal year. Everything on it is phrased in the present tense of that one instant — not “Apple earned $147.957 billion in current assets this year” but “Apple held $147.957 billion in current assets at the close of business on September 27, 2025.” Move the clock forward one day, and every number on the page is already slightly out of date; a sale closes, a bill gets paid, and the photograph no longer matches the room.

International accounting rules are, on this one point, more honest about the document’s own nature than American ones: where U.S. GAAP calls it a “balance sheet,” the international standard (IFRS) calls the identical document a statement of financial position — a name with no poetry in it at all, but one that says outright what the balance sheet has always actually been: a position, at a stated instant, not a summary of activity. Both names describe the same three-part structure this lesson builds below; the IFRS name is simply less likely to be misread as covering a stretch of time the document was never built to cover.

This distinguishes the balance sheet sharply from the other main financial statement a company publishes, the income statement, which this lesson has not yet covered directly (that is day seventeen of this track) but will lean on for contrast throughout. An income statement covers a period — a quarter, a year — and reports what flowed through the business during that stretch: revenue earned, expenses incurred, profit left over. If the balance sheet is a photograph, the income statement is closer to a home movie: it shows motion, accumulated over an interval, rather than a frozen instant. You cannot ask an income statement “what did the company own at 5:00 PM on Tuesday” any more than you can ask a photograph “how fast was the runner moving between frame one and frame two.” Each statement answers a question the other one structurally cannot, and a great deal of financial-statement literacy is nothing more exotic than keeping straight which question you are actually asking.

Inside that one frozen instant, a balance sheet organizes everything into exactly three buckets, and the vocabulary is worth being precise about because casual usage blurs it. Assets are everything of economic value the company owns or controls as a result of a past transaction — cash, inventory, buildings, equipment, patents, money owed to it by customers. Liabilities are everything it owes to somebody else as a result of a past transaction — loans, unpaid bills, wages accrued, taxes owed, bonds it has issued. Equity — sometimes called shareholders’ equity, sometimes net worth, sometimes book value — is neither of those things directly; it is what is left over, on paper, after every liability has been subtracted from every asset. It represents the owners’ residual claim: if every asset were somehow sold at exactly the value the balance sheet lists and every liability paid off with the proceeds, equity is what the owners would be left holding.

Notice the word residual there, because it does a lot of quiet work. Equity is not an independently measured quantity the way assets and liabilities are — nobody goes out and appraises “how much equity” a company has the way an appraiser values a building. It is defined entirely in terms of the other two: whatever assets add up to, minus whatever liabilities add up to, is equity, by definition, with no third measurement involved. That single fact is the whole of today’s formalism, stated in one sentence before a single symbol appears: on any balance sheet that has actually been prepared correctly, what a company owns always equals what it owes plus what belongs to its owners, because the third category is defined to make that true.

None of this machinery is exclusive to corporations. A personal net worth statement — the kind a bank asks for on a mortgage application, or the kind a financial planner draws up for a client — is the identical three-bucket structure at a household scale, even though almost nobody calls it a “balance sheet” out loud. A house, a car, a checking account, and a retirement account are assets; a mortgage balance, a car loan, and whatever sits on a credit card statement are liabilities; net worth, the number every personal-finance calculator ultimately produces, is exactly the residual, assets minus liabilities, with no independent measurement of its own. It is worth pausing on this equivalence, because it means the misconception this lesson spends the most time on later — that equity is not the same thing as market value — already has a familiar personal analogue: a homeowner’s net worth statement lists a house at what a bank’s appraiser or the homeowner’s own estimate says it is worth today, not at its original purchase price, which is one of the genuine differences between personal net worth accounting and corporate balance sheets and a useful thing to keep straight rather than assume carries over.

Writing the photograph down

Put the sentence above into the notation this track will use for the rest of its run. Call total assets A, total liabilities L, and total equity E, all measured at the same instant — the same balance-sheet date. The relationship described above, usually called the accounting equation, is this:

A  =  L + E

A
total assets at a given instant — everything of economic value the company owns or controls, measured in the reporting currency at that moment
L
total liabilities at that same instant — everything the company owes to parties outside the ownership group: lenders, suppliers, employees, tax authorities, bondholders
E
total equity at that same instant — the residual claim of the company’s owners, defined as whatever is left once L is subtracted from A; never measured independently of A and L

Lesson seven called this same relationship the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping: every transaction a business records changes at least two of these three quantities, by matching amounts, specifically so that this equation stays true after every single entry, not just at the moment someone bothers to check. A balance sheet is nothing more than this equation, evaluated at one instant and broken out into detail — not just the three totals, but every individual account that sums to them.

Two further pieces of structure appear on every real balance sheet, and both matter for reading one correctly. First, assets and liabilities are each split into current and non-current (sometimes called long-term). A current asset is one the company expects to convert to cash, sell, or consume within one year (or one normal operating cycle, if that is longer than a year — rare outside a few industries like homebuilding). A current liability is one the company expects to settle within that same one-year window. Everything else — a factory, a fifteen-year lease, a bond due in 2035, a patent with a decade left on it — is non-current. This split exists because the timing of a claim matters as much as its size: a $10 billion bill due next Tuesday is a completely different problem from a $10 billion bill due in 2040, even though both are, in isolation, ordinary liabilities.

Second, most assets are recorded at historical cost — what the company actually paid for them — rather than at what they could be sold for today, with a handful of well-defined exceptions (marketable securities and derivatives are generally marked to current fair value; assets that lose value are written down for impairment; inventory is carried at the lower of cost or market). A factory Apple built a decade ago sits on its balance sheet, minus accumulated depreciation, at roughly what it cost to build, not at what a buyer would pay for it today, and a brand Apple built through decades of its own marketing and product design does not appear on the balance sheet as an asset at all, because the company never paid an outside party a specific price for it. This is not sloppiness; it is a deliberate choice, favoring a number that can be verified against an actual receipt over one that would require a subjective appraisal every quarter. It is also the single most important thing the misconception section of this lesson returns to.

Where does this document come from, institutionally? A balance sheet is not merely a convenience a company chooses to publish. Every company that sells stock to the American public is legally required to file one, audited by an independent accounting firm, with the Securities and Exchange Commission — at least once a year in the annual report called a 10-K, and again, unaudited, each quarter in a 10-Q. That requirement traces back to the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, passed in direct response to the 1929 crash and the discovery, in its aftermath, that a great many investors had bought stock in companies whose published finances bore little relationship to reality. The auditor’s job on the annual filing is not to verify that every number is exactly right in some absolute sense — it is to test, by sampling transactions and checking internal controls, whether the statements are free of material misstatement and fairly present the company’s position under the accounting rules that apply, and to say so in a short, formal opinion letter bound into the filing. An auditor who cannot reach that conclusion says so explicitly, in a qualified or adverse opinion, which is itself one of the most consequential sentences that can appear in a company’s annual report. Apple’s balance sheet in the worked examples below is not a summary this newspaper compiled from a press release; it is the exact figure Apple itself filed with the SEC, examined by an outside auditor willing to put its own name behind it, and available to anyone through the SEC’s own public EDGAR database.

Building one from scratch

Worked example 1 · a balance sheet from a handful of accounts

Imagine a friend opens a small coffee cart business and, at the end of its first three months, hands you a scrap of paper listing seven account balances, in no particular order, and asks whether the business is in decent shape. The accounts: cash $8,400; equipment (the cart, espresso machine, grinder) $22,000; a supplier invoice not yet paid, $1,600; inventory (beans, cups, milk) $2,100; a bank loan used to buy the equipment, $18,000, of which $4,000 is due within the next year and the rest over the following three years; money a nearby office owes for a standing weekly order, $900; and whatever your friend originally put into the business and has since left in it as retained profit, which was never written down anywhere on its own.

  1. Sort every account into one of the three balance-sheet buckets. Assets: cash ($8,400), equipment ($22,000), inventory ($2,100), and the amount owed by the office customer — an account receivable ($900). Total assets: $8,400 + $22,000 + $2,100 + $900 = $33,400.
  2. Split those assets into current and non-current. Current (convertible to cash or consumed within a year): cash, inventory, and the receivable — $8,400 + $2,100 + $900 = $11,400. Non-current: the equipment, $22,000, since a cart and espresso machine are not going to be sold or consumed within the year; they are used, and depreciated, over several years.
  3. Sort the liabilities the same way. The unpaid supplier invoice, $1,600, is a current liability — an account payable — due almost immediately. The loan splits: $4,000 of it is due within the next twelve months (the current portion of long-term debt) and the remaining $14,000 is due later (non-current). Total liabilities: $1,600 + $4,000 + $14,000 = $19,600.
  4. Equity is not on the scrap of paper, because nobody kept a running count of it — but the accounting equation does not need it to have been tracked separately, because equity is defined as whatever is left over. E = A − L = $33,400 − $19,600 = $13,800. That is your friend’s stake in the business as of this specific instant: not cash sitting anywhere, but the value of everything owned once every debt is accounted for.
  5. Check the answer the other direction, the way any balance sheet should always be checked: L + E = $19,600 + $13,800 = $33,400, exactly matching total assets. The sheet balances, which is not a coincidence or a lucky arithmetic accident — it is the accounting equation holding by construction, the same guarantee lesson seven’s double-entry system exists to provide.

Notice what this small example already demonstrates that the coffee cart’s owner could not see just by staring at the $8,400 cash balance: current assets ($11,400) comfortably exceed current liabilities ($5,600, the payable plus the current loan slice), so the business can cover what it owes over the next year even though most of its value — the $22,000 of equipment — is locked up in something it cannot quickly turn back into cash. That comparison, current assets against current liabilities, is exactly the one this lesson opened with for Apple — and exactly the one that came out negative there.

Checking the photograph against a company you’ve actually seen

Worked example 2 · Apple’s balance sheet, fiscal year ended September 27, 2025

Apple’s own 10-K, filed with the SEC, reports total assets of $359.241 billion and total liabilities of $285.508 billion for the fiscal year ended September 27, 2025. Confirm the accounting equation holds, and then check the specific comparison this lesson opened with.

  1. Compute implied equity from the accounting equation: E = A − L = $359.241B − $285.508B = $73.733 billion. Apple’s own reported total stockholders’ equity for that date is exactly $73.733 billion — the equation checks out to the dollar, as it must on any correctly prepared balance sheet.
  2. Break assets and liabilities into current and non-current, the same split worked example 1 used. Apple reports current assets of $147.957 billion and current liabilities of $165.631 billion.
  3. Compute working capital: current assets minus current liabilities = $147.957B − $165.631B = −$17.674 billion. Negative — exactly the gap this lesson opened with.
  4. Compute the current ratio, a standard shorthand for the same comparison expressed as a proportion rather than a dollar gap: current assets ÷ current liabilities = $147.957B ÷ $165.631B ≈ 0.89. A current ratio below 1.0 means, by definition, that a company’s near-term obligations exceed its near-term convertible assets — textbook language for describing exactly the situation many introductory finance courses treat as a liquidity warning sign.

Every number in that sequence is real, drawn directly from Apple’s own audited annual filing, and the arithmetic is not in dispute. What is genuinely open to question is what that 0.89 means — and the honest answer, which the next two sections build toward, is: for Apple specifically, considerably less than the same number would mean for the coffee cart in worked example 1, or for almost any other company on the planet.

Where did sixty billion dollars of equity go?

Worked example 3 · why equity can fall in a company’s best year

Apple’s equity has not moved in one direction over the years; it peaked at $134.047 billion at the end of fiscal 2017, then fell for five straight years to a low of $50.672 billion at the end of fiscal 2022 — a decline of $83.375 billion — before partially recovering to the $73.733 billion seen in worked example 2. Fiscal 2022 is worth walking through in detail, because it was also one of the most profitable years in Apple’s history, which is precisely the combination that trips up an intuition built on the phrase “shrinking net worth.”

  1. Start from equity at the beginning of fiscal 2022 (equal to the end of fiscal 2021): $63.090 billion.
  2. Add fiscal 2022’s net income, the profit the income statement reported flowing to the company over that year: $99.803 billion. If net income were the only thing that ever touched equity, ending equity should be $63.090B + $99.803B = $162.893 billion.
  3. Apple’s actual reported ending equity for fiscal 2022 was $50.672 billion — $112.221 billion lower than that projection. Something removed $112.221 billion from the equity account over the year, more than the entire net income the year added.
  4. Apple’s own cash flow statement for fiscal 2022 shows two of the largest candidates directly: $89.402 billion spent repurchasing its own shares, and $14.841 billion paid out as dividends — $104.243 billion between the two, accounting for roughly 93% of the $112.221 billion gap. The remainder is smaller items this lesson will not itemize in full — principally unrealized movements in the value of Apple’s investment portfolio and the mechanics of employee stock compensation — each individually too small to change the picture.

Read plainly: Apple earned nearly $100 billion in fiscal 2022, and its balance-sheet equity fell anyway, because it handed shareholders more than $104 billion of cash in buybacks and dividends — a deliberate capital-allocation choice, decided in boardrooms, not a sign that the business lost money or lost value. A buyback retires shares by paying cash out of the company and, on the balance sheet, reduces both cash (an asset) and equity by the same amount, which is exactly why it can drive reported equity down even in a year of record profit. Nothing about this transaction touches the income statement at all — net income does not fall because a company bought back stock — which is exactly why reading the balance sheet and the income statement together, rather than either one alone, is unavoidable if the goal is to actually understand what happened to a company in a given year.

Why a bank will still lend Apple money

The number worked example 2 produced — a current ratio of 0.89, or negative working capital of $17.674 billion — is exactly the kind of figure a credit analyst is trained to flag first when evaluating whether to extend a loan or buy a company’s bonds. And credit analysts do, in fact, look at it for Apple, every time the company raises debt in the bond market, which it does regularly and at some of the lowest borrowing costs available to any corporate issuer on Earth. The reason a number that would sink a smaller company’s credit application barely registers as a concern for Apple’s lenders comes down to three things a single balance-sheet ratio cannot see on its own, because a balance sheet, remember, is a photograph of one instant: it has no memory of what happened before that instant and no way to show what is about to happen after it.

First, a company’s ability to pay next year’s bills does not actually depend only on the assets already sitting on this quarter’s balance sheet; it depends heavily on the cash the business is expected to generate during that year, which the balance sheet, by design, cannot show — that information lives in the income statement and the cash flow statement, the “movie,” not the photograph. Apple’s operating business throws off tens of billions of dollars of cash every quarter, reliably enough that lenders treat next year’s expected cash generation as functionally certain, in a way nobody could treat the coffee cart’s future sales. Second, some of what appears as a “current liability” on Apple’s balance sheet, particularly deferred revenue on services and warranties already paid for by customers, will be settled by delivering a service Apple has already been paid for, not by finding new cash — a liability in the accounting sense, but not one that competes for cash the way a supplier invoice does. Third, and most simply, a company as large and as reliably profitable as Apple can access more cash on short notice — through commercial paper markets, undrawn credit lines, or simply issuing more bonds — at a moment’s notice and at a very low cost, something the balance sheet also does not show, because access to credit is a capability, not an asset anyone books at a dollar value.

This is also why the credit-rating agencies that grade corporate bonds — the firms lenders actually rely on to translate a company’s finances into a letter grade — do not stop at a single quarter’s current ratio, or at any single balance-sheet snapshot at all. Their published methodologies weigh multiple years of cash flow, the stability and predictability of the specific industry a company competes in, and its demonstrated access to capital markets, precisely because a photograph of one instant, however precisely it balances, cannot by itself distinguish a company having an unusual quarter from a company in genuine decline. A ratio below a textbook threshold is a prompt to look further, in other words, not a verdict on its own — and it is worth being honest that a lender skips that further look at its own risk. None of that makes the current ratio a useless number; it makes it an incomplete one, exactly as incomplete as a single photograph is at describing a football game. A single frame can show you the ball is in the air, but not who threw it, where it is going, or whether the team throwing it is winning. The skill this lesson is teaching is not “ignore the balance sheet” — it is “read the balance sheet for exactly what it can tell you, and reach for the other statements, and for context about the specific company, before drawing a conclusion the photograph alone cannot support.”

The mistake almost everyone makes

The single most common misreading of a balance sheet, and the one this lesson has been building toward correcting the whole way through, is treating equity — often called “book value” or loosely, “net worth” — as an estimate of what a company is actually worth, the price a buyer would pay for the whole thing. It is not, and it was never designed to be, for two separate reasons that reinforce each other.

The first reason is the historical-cost principle from the formalism section above. Assets sit on the balance sheet at what the company paid for them, adjusted for depreciation and occasional write-downs, not at what they would fetch if sold today or what it would cost to replace them. A company’s most valuable asset is very often something that never touched a purchase invoice at all: a brand built over decades, an engineering culture, relationships with suppliers, the accumulated knowledge of its employees, the ability to design a chip nobody else can currently match. Accounting rules call these “internally generated intangibles,” and with narrow exceptions they are simply absent from the balance sheet — not undervalued, absent, at a book value of exactly zero, because no outside transaction ever put a price on them. Apple’s balance sheet lists $73.733 billion of equity, but that number reflects a company that has spent decades reinvesting in a brand and a product design capability that appear nowhere on the page at any value at all.

The second reason is worked example 3, generalized: equity moves for reasons that have nothing to do with how much value a business is creating or destroying. A large stock buyback lowers equity by exactly the cash spent, regardless of whether the company had a great year or a mediocre one. A company that borrows heavily to fund a buyback can push equity toward zero or even negative — several well-known, financially healthy companies report negative book equity for exactly this reason, a fact that reliably alarms readers encountering it for the first time and reliably fails to alarm anyone who has actually read those companies’ cash flow statements. Equity, in other words, is not a scoreboard of value created; it is an accounting residual, sensitive to financing decisions made in a boardroom as much as to anything a customer paid for a product.

A related version of the same mistake shows up whenever someone compares a “debt-to-equity” ratio — total liabilities divided by equity — across companies in different industries as though the number means the same thing everywhere. A bank or a utility routinely carries far more debt relative to equity than a software company does, not because banks and utilities are worse-run or closer to trouble, but because their entire business model runs on borrowed money (a bank’s core product is lending out money it has itself borrowed, in the form of deposits) or on financing enormous, decades-long infrastructure with debt rather than shareholder cash. A debt-to-equity ratio that would be alarming for a software company is simply normal, structural, and expected for a regulated utility. The number is only meaningful compared against similar companies in the same industry, not against a single rule of thumb imported from somewhere else — another instance of a balance-sheet figure being real, correctly calculated, and still capable of being badly misread without context the balance sheet itself does not supply.

The number that actually attempts to answer “what would someone pay for this company” is a company’s market capitalization — its share price multiplied by shares outstanding — and it is worth stating plainly, without citing a specific figure here since it changes by the minute and belongs to a market report rather than an accounting one, that for a company like Apple, market capitalization and book equity are not in the same neighborhood; they can differ by a factor of many multiples, and neither one is “the real number” with the other being wrong. They are answers to two different questions, asked by two different disciplines, using two entirely different methods — one counting historical receipts, the other pricing a claim on an uncertain future. Confusing them is the single most consequential mistake a beginner makes when first handed a balance sheet, and it is worth returning to every time a headline uses the phrase “the company is worth” without saying which of the two numbers it means.

The callback

Yesterday’s lesson, day eleven of this curriculum’s Mathematics track, spent its entire length on one careful distinction: the difference between an average rate of change — a secant slope, computed over some finite window, like the twenty-session and fifty-session price averages that lesson pulled from a real market report — and a true instantaneous derivative, which requires a genuinely continuous process sampled over a window shrinking all the way to zero. That lesson’s central warning was not to call the twenty-day and fifty-day comparisons “the market’s derivative,” because daily closing prices are single dots with nothing smooth connecting them; there is no such thing as half a trading day to sample, so no true limit can honestly be taken.

The relationship between a balance sheet and an income statement is exactly that same distinction, wearing accounting’s clothes instead of mathematics’s. A balance sheet gives you individual dots — equity of $134.047 billion at the end of fiscal 2017, $63.090 billion at the end of fiscal 2021, $50.672 billion at the end of fiscal 2022, $73.733 billion at the end of fiscal 2025 — one snapshot per reporting date, with nothing observed in between. An income statement, and worked example 3’s reconstruction of what happened to equity during fiscal 2022, is the accounting equivalent of a secant slope: an average rate of change of the business’s financial position, computed over the finite window between two balance-sheet dates, never a true instantaneous rate. There is no meaningful notion of Apple’s exact rate of profit at 2:14 PM on a Tuesday in March, any more than yesterday’s lesson found a meaningful instantaneous derivative hiding inside a daily stock close — a business’s finances are reported at discrete points, by design, and every number describing the space between two of those points is, honestly, an average over that whole interval, not a limit that has been properly taken.

This is not a coincidence of vocabulary; it is the same underlying idea — a stock quantity, measured at instants, versus a flow quantity, measured as a rate of change between instants — recurring in a field that has nothing to do with calculus on its surface. Physics has position and velocity; a water tank has its water level and its flow rate in or out; and accounting has the balance sheet, a stock, and the income statement, a flow between two balance-sheet snapshots. Wherever that pattern shows up, the same discipline from yesterday’s lesson applies: state clearly whether the number in front of you is a level at an instant or a rate of change over an interval, and never let a chart’s smooth appearance or a report’s confident tone smuggle in more precision about the interior of that interval than the underlying data actually supports.

Where this leaves you
  • You can state the accounting equation, A = L + E, explain why equity is a residual rather than an independently measured quantity, and read any balance sheet by sorting its accounts into assets, liabilities, and current versus non-current within each.
  • You can build a balance sheet from a raw list of account balances, as worked example 1 did, and verify it balances by checking both directions of the accounting equation.
  • You can compute working capital and the current ratio from real figures, as worked example 2 did with Apple’s own fiscal 2025 10-K, and you know these numbers describe only what a single instant’s photograph can show — not a company’s access to credit, its future cash generation, or the composition of what its liabilities actually represent.
  • You can explain why a company’s equity can fall in its most profitable year, using Apple’s fiscal 2022 buybacks and dividends as a worked, sourced example, and you know book equity is not the same question as market value and was never designed to answer it.
  • You know where this is going: day seventeen of this track builds the income statement itself — the “movie” this lesson kept gesturing at without yet defining — and shows exactly how it connects, account by account, back to the balance sheet on either side of it.
