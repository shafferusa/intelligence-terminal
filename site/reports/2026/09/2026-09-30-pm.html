Top Stories

A rewrite of history puts core inflation at 3.0%, not 3.3%, and October hike bets fall again. The 30-year closes at its highest since 2002 anyway.

August itself was ordinary: core prices rose 0.25%, close to forecasts. Most of the “big downside surprise” in the headlines is a revision. The bond market treated it that way.

At 8:30 AM the Bureau of Economic Analysis published August personal income and outlays together with the first results of its 2026 annual update of the national accounts. We read the index values directly from FRED this afternoon. The core PCE price index, which excludes food and energy and is the Fed’s preferred gauge, rose 0.25% in August and 3.01% over the year. Headline PCE rose 0.31% in the month and 3.42% over the year. Consensus had been 0.3% for monthly core and 3.3–3.4% for annual core.

So the monthly number was roughly on forecast. The annual number was 0.3–0.4 point lower than expected, and FRED’s vintage history shows why. Yesterday, July’s core rate stood at 3.34% over the year. Today, after the revision, it stands at 2.98%. July’s monthly core increase was cut from 0.25% to 0.13%. BEA did not report an inflation surprise this morning. It reported that inflation had been lower all along than it previously thought.

The same revision made the economy look stronger. Second-quarter real GDP growth was raised to 2.2% at an annual rate from 1.5%, and first-quarter growth to 2.5% from 2.1%, according to CNSNews and Haver Analytics accounts of the release. Real gross domestic income grew 2.6% in the second quarter. August spending rose 0.9% and income only 0.2%, leaving the saving rate at 4.1%, according to Fox Business and FXStreet. Put together, the new history has faster growth, lower inflation and a consumer spending out of a thin cushion. We did not read BEA’s full revision tables, and we have not verified reports that the update changed how some prices are measured.

The rate market read it as a reason to wait. Reported CME FedWatch odds of an October 28 hike fell further, but the level depends on the snapshot: CNBC cited 37%, and a crypto exchange’s market note cited readings of 33% and 47% at different times. All agree the odds are now below half, down from about 70% on Monday. On Treasury’s own curve, the 3-month bill fell 5bp to 4.20% and the 2-year 1bp to 4.88%.

The long end sold off. The 10-year closed at 5.29%, up 3bp, its highest close of this run. The 30-year closed at 5.64%, up 5bp and above Tuesday’s 5.59%, which Bloomberg and CNBC described as the highest since 2002. The gap between the 2-year and 10-year widened to 41bp from 37bp. Market narrative CNBC’s account says yields fell on the release and then turned up as traders looked ahead to Friday’s jobs report. Stronger growth data also arrived: ADP payrolls beat forecasts, and the Chicago PMI jumped. We cannot separate those effects from quarter-end flows. TLT traded more than twice its normal volume.

Why it matters This is the second day running that the Fed’s end of the curve eased while the long end rose. On Tuesday the trigger was Williams, and today it was inflation revised lower. When good inflation news cannot pull the 30-year down, the pressure is in the term premium: supply, fiscal worries and global yields, with Europe’s inflation jumping this week. A patient Fed does not fix that, and mortgage rates and corporate refinancing are priced off the long end.
What’s next ISM manufacturing Thursday at 10:00, then September payrolls Friday at 8:30. Vice Chair Jefferson speaks Thursday at 1:30 PM, the first leadership voice after the revision.

Micron beats its own record guidance: $54.2 billion in revenue, an 87% gross margin, and a $61.5 billion outlook

The largest US memory maker guided about $4.5 billion above consensus for the current quarter. The only soft spot was a margin guide a hair below one estimate.

Micron’s earnings release, filed with the SEC at 4:02 PM and read directly for this edition, reported the following for the fiscal fourth quarter ended September 3:

  • Revenue: $54.23 billion, against guidance of $50 billion ± $1 billion. That compares with $41.46 billion last quarter and $11.32 billion a year ago.
  • Non-GAAP earnings: $33.42 a share, against guidance of $31 ± $1 and consensus of about $31.6 (LSEG via CNBC).
  • Non-GAAP gross margin: 87.0%, above the roughly 86% guide.

Every business unit grew. Core data center revenue rose to $18.0 billion from $11.5 billion last quarter. Full-year revenue was $133.2 billion, against $37.4 billion in fiscal 2025. Operating cash flow was $44 billion in the quarter, and the company ended the year with $73.5 billion of cash and investments.

Guidance for the November quarter:

  • Revenue: $61.5 billion ± $1.5 billion, against about $57.0 billion expected (LSEG).
  • Non-GAAP EPS: $38.15 ± $1.00, against about $35.1–35.4 expected.
  • Non-GAAP gross margin: about 86.25%. That is slightly below this quarter’s 87.0%, and FXStreet says it is slightly below a 86.4% consensus.

Chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra said the company expects “an even stronger fiscal 2027” and cited its long-term “Strategic Customer Agreements.” The release also adopts the White House’s new term for AI (see The United States), saying “AI is becoming Super Intelligence (SI).” The shares closed at $1,065.11, unchanged on the day. Yahoo Finance showed them near $1,088 at 4:34 PM, up about 2%, after a choppy first half-hour that one live blog put as low as about $1,040. Options had priced a move of about 6–7% either way.

Why it matters Micron is the cleanest public read on AI hardware demand. A beat this size, with a guide this far above consensus, says the memory shortage is not easing into 2027. The muted after-hours reaction says investors had already paid for much of it: the stock is up 273% this year. A slightly lower margin guide is the first sign that pricing may have peaked, and it is small.

Europe’s inflation jump spreads to Germany: 3.3% in September, and the euro area may be near 3.8%

Energy is doing the damage. German core inflation was unchanged at 2.4%, which is what the ECB will lean on.

Germany’s statistics office reported flash consumer-price inflation of 3.3% for September, up from 2.9% and above the 3.1% expected. Prices rose 0.6% in the month. Energy prices were up 14.9% on the year, from 10.5% in August, while core inflation held at 2.4%. That follows this morning’s prints from France (3.4% on the EU measure) and Italy (4.1%). MUFG estimates the euro-area figure, due later this week, at about 3.8%, which would be the highest since September 2023. That estimate is one bank’s.

European stocks gave up an early 0.7% gain. Reuters-sourced accounts differ on whether the Stoxx 600 finished flat or down about 0.5%. The index lost about 2% in September, its first monthly fall in six months. In US trading, the Vanguard Europe fund VGK fell 1.2%. The euro ended near $1.135, and Reuters says the dollar’s 2.2% gain against it in September was its biggest monthly gain in 14 months.

Hiring picks up: ADP counts 90,000 private jobs in September, and the Chicago PMI jumps

It is the first acceleration in ADP’s count since May, two days before the official report.

ADP’s National Employment Report showed private payrolls up 90,000 in September, against about 70,000 expected. August was revised to +36,000. The gains were led by education and health (+55,000), leisure and hospitality (+22,000), manufacturing (+17,000) and construction (+15,000). Financial activities (−16,000) and professional and business services (−11,000) cut jobs. Pay for workers who stayed in their jobs rose 3.2% on the year. CNBC, Bloomberg and Fox Business all reported the same figures.

The Chicago business barometer is harder to pin down. Newsquawk and MarketScreener report 58.8 for September, up from 47.1 and far above the roughly 51 expected. Figures conflict A third source reports 55.8. Either way it is a large jump. The Chicago survey is small and volatile, and the national ISM on Thursday is the better test.

The Economy

Inflation, real and nominal. August headline PCE was 3.42% over the year and core 3.01%, read from FRED. Both are rates of change in price levels, and the revision lowered the level path, not just this month. Real consumer spending rose about 0.6% in August while real disposable income barely moved, per the release as reported. That is how the saving rate fell to 4.1%. It cannot continue indefinitely.

Growth, revised up. Second-quarter real GDP was revised to 2.2% from 1.5% and gross domestic income grew 2.6%, averaging about 2.4%. These figures come from secondary accounts, and Haver Analytics called the benchmark revision “small” overall. Revisions went up for growth and down for inflation, and together they tell the Fed its policy has been working with less damage than it feared.

The Fed. Governor Lisa Cook, speaking in Asheville on the rural economy, said “inflation has been too high for too long” and that she is “committed to returning inflation to our objective while preserving the strength in the labor market,” per Reuters. She gave no explicit signal on October. Tuesday’s split between Barr (more hikes “likely”) and Williams (“no need for urgency”) now tilts toward Williams. Today’s data give him what he asked for: time and a lower starting point.

The Treasury. The federal fiscal year ends tonight, and the government is funded through December 11 under the continuing resolution Congress passed weeks ago, so there is no shutdown. The 3-month bill fell 5bp to 4.20%, while 20- and 30-year yields rose 4–5bp. That is the shape of a market worried about duration supply rather than the next Fed move. We did not find new Treasury auction or refunding announcements today. The quarterly refunding statement comes in early November.

Rates and credit. The ICE BofA high-yield spread (FRED) was 3.08% on Tuesday, up from 3.02%, and has widened 42bp in six sessions. HYG and LQD each finished within about 5 and 14 cents of their 52-week lows on double their usual volume. The 10-year TIPS real yield was 2.91% on Tuesday and the 10-year breakeven 2.35%, so the rise in long yields continues to come mainly through real rates.

Global. German inflation was 3.3%, and the euro area looks set for a print near 3.8% when Eurostat reports. China’s official PMI returned to growth this morning ahead of the October 1–7 Golden Week holiday. The dollar index rose 0.13% to 101.50 and finished September up 2.1%.

The United States

Senate blocks a congressional stock-trading ban and, for a second time, a data-center power-cost bill

Both cloture votes fell short of 60 on the last day before the election recess.

The motion to proceed to the Stop Insider Trading Act (H.R. 7008) failed 53–47 on a party-line vote. The Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 9340), which would let states set standards so that data centers bear their own grid costs, failed 57–43. Democratic Senators Hassan, Klobuchar, Ossoff and Warnock voted yes. Supporters say the bill protects household power bills from data-center demand. Opponents, including Democratic leader Chuck Schumer, called it a “toothless messaging bill,” according to Roll Call. The Senate was also scheduled to vote on cloture for Labor Secretary nominee Keith Sonderling. We did not find the result. The House was not in session.

The last US troops leave Iraq’s Erbil base, ending the anti-ISIS mission there

The handover carries out the 2024 US–Iraq agreement on the coalition’s drawdown.

Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi attended the handover, according to AP, NPR and Stars and Stripes. Kurdish officials have raised security concerns. Science

A study of 1.5 million Change.org petitions, published today in Nature Human Behaviour, found that petitions drafted with AI help were longer but no more likely to succeed. Duquesne Light’s union says it has lost confidence in management as its contract runs out at midnight

IBEW Local 29, which represents more than 600 Duquesne Light workers, issued a public no-confidence statement a day before its contract expires, according to the Post-Gazette. It says it will strike when the contract ends at midnight Wednesday unless it gets a “fair and equitable contract.” Business manager Josh Ewing cited unresolved safety and staffing concerns, OSHA complaints and outage-causing errors. Members authorized a strike 592–14 in mid-September. The company’s reported offer is a 4% raise on October 1, 3.25% in each of 2027 and 2028, and a $3,000 ratification bonus. The utility says it has a backup plan to keep power on.

Search results also surface stories of a tentative agreement reached one day before a September 30 expiry. Those match the 2023 contract, and we could not tie any of them to this year. As of publication we have no confirmed 2026 deal and no confirmed strike.

Ken Griffin gives Carnegie Mellon $3 billion: $2 billion for a Miami campus, $1 billion for Pittsburgh

CMU announced the gift today and described it as the largest by an individual in the history of higher education. Two-thirds will build a 35-acre campus in Miami’s Wynwood district, with first students expected in 2028. The remaining $1 billion goes to the Pittsburgh campus. Griffin joins CMU’s board. For the region, CMU is one of its biggest employers and research engines. The question is whether the Miami campus competes with Pittsburgh for faculty and programs or adds to them. CMU has not detailed how the Pittsburgh billion will be spent.
