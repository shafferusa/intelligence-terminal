Top Stories

Trump and Zelensky meet in New York as Russia and Ukraine trade deadly overnight strikes

The two leaders sit down for the first time since Washington’s new Russia sanctions law, hours after Kyiv struck a refinery deep inside Russia and Moscow hit Zaporizhzhia.

Trump and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky agreed by phone Sunday to meet in New York today, where Trump is also expected to address the UN General Assembly. Zelensky, who arrived in the US on Sunday, said roughly twenty other meetings are planned around the sit-down; he described hoping to discuss “ideas for de-escalation steps and for addressing fundamental security issues — energy security, food security, and the protection of human life.” Trump’s address is expected to touch the Iran war, AI, and the US role at the UN. Neither event had occurred as of this edition’s publication.

The meeting comes after, not before, a fresh exchange of strikes. Russia hit at least four Ukrainian regions overnight with anti-ship, ballistic and cruise missiles plus more than 200 drones, killing four people, per Ukraine’s air force. Ukraine says it struck the Kuibyshev oil refinery in Samara, deep inside Russia, with drones overnight, sparking a large fire. That follows Monday’s Russian retaliatory strike on Zaporizhzhia — 172 drones across eight Ukrainian cities, destroying the city’s oldest university — itself a response to Ukraine’s own record barrage of more than 1,000 drones on Moscow a day earlier, timed to Russia’s Duma election. That Moscow attack killed two people, damaged the Moscow Oil Refinery and a residential building, and cut power to more than 20,000 residents. Exact casualty counts from Zaporizhzhia diverge across wires — figures from one dead/31 wounded to two dead/five wounded have been reported — and should be read as preliminary pending an official Ukrainian tally.

Why it matters This is Trump’s first sit-down with Zelensky since signing a sweeping new Russia sanctions law last Friday with an unusually critical signing statement — and it opens with both sides escalating, not de-escalating.
What’s next Any readout from the meeting and the text of Trump’s UN address, both expected later today.

G7 demands the Houthis stand down as the Yemen war widens and a tanker is hit again near Hormuz

A joint statement today calls on the group to stop attacking shipping, as it pushes into new Yemeni territory and Trump reportedly called off US strikes at the last minute after Saudi pressure.

Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the UK and the US issued a joint statement today calling on Houthi forces to “immediately cease all military actions,” end attacks and threats against civilian shipping, and return to the political process, while calling on Iran to end its support for the group as a violation of UN Security Council resolutions. It follows Saturday’s Houthi ballistic-missile attempt on Riyadh — intercepted, but debris ignited a fire at an Aramco-owned fuel depot near King Khalid International Airport — and a claimed strike on an Aramco facility in Yanbu. A Yemeni resident in Saudi Arabia was killed by intercepted-drone debris, the first reported civilian death of this round of escalation.

Separately, five Yemeni military sources told Reuters the Houthis are now pushing to seize the Kahboub Mountains — heights in Taiz and Lahij provinces separating the Red Sea coast from the government-held south — with fighting centered near Al-Wazi’iyah district and Ras al-Ara, following the group’s earlier seizure of the Red Sea port of Mokha. Single source That account rests on one Reuters sourcing chain and has not been independently corroborated. On the US side, Trump reportedly called off planned airstrikes on the Houthis at the last minute — munitions already loaded onto fighter jets — after Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman pressed him roughly ten days ago to strike; US officials instead met Houthi representatives at the embassy in Muscat, Oman, where the group reportedly agreed to stop targeting American vessels in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Single source That stand-down account, too, remains unconfirmed by a second reporting chain.

A tanker transiting into the Strait of Hormuz was struck by an unclaimed projectile yesterday, injuring two crew members; the vessel continued under its own power. UKMTO, which reported the strike, said it also learned of a separate, earlier hit on a gas tanker. No group has claimed either. It is the second such unattributed incident this month in a Strait that carries more than a fifth of global oil trade and is effectively closed to routine commercial shipping — roughly eight tanker transits on September 13 against a normal count near 85 a day. Meeting Macron in New York, Trump said the two “decided to act together to ease tensions in the energy markets, by protecting critical infrastructure” and safeguarding freedom of navigation in the Strait; Macron described it as a step toward diplomatic options rather than a resolved plan.

Why it matters This sits on top of the still-unresolved US-Israel-Iran war that began in February: the June ceasefire framework collapsed by mid-August, and the Houthi campaign and the Hormuz strikes are downstream of that unresolved conflict, not a separate one.

Al-Monitor · Al Jazeera · Irish Times · NPR · PBS · UKMTO · Reuters wire pickups (Confirmed-multiple on the G7 statement and Riyadh strike; Single-reliable-source on the Kahboub push and the Muscat stand-down).

Trump prepares to host Xi as China calls a pending Taiwan arms sale a possible dealbreaker

Xi’s first White House visit in a decade opens Wednesday, days after Beijing objected to Trump’s new Russia and Iran sanctions law.

Xi Jinping’s visit runs Wednesday through Thursday, with a White House state dinner Thursday — his second meeting with Trump this year after Trump’s May trip to Beijing. Trade, AI and critical minerals lead the agenda, with Taiwan and the Iran war also expected to surface. China’s foreign ministry has called its opposition to a Congress-authorized, still-unsigned $14 billion Taiwan arms package “consistent, clear-cut and resolute,” and has signaled the summit itself could be at risk if the package proceeds. Single source One South China Morning Post report, citing a single source, says Trump may hold off signing until year-end — treating it as a negotiating chip — a claim not yet corroborated elsewhere. Separately, China’s foreign ministry dismissed as “illicit” the sweeping Russia and Iran sanctions law Trump signed last Friday, a fresh irritant days before the visit.

Treasury Secretary Bessent, in Sunday talks in New York with Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng, proposed a new US-China AI dialogue — effectively an incident-notification channel — for the two leaders to consider at the summit. Beijing’s official readout of the Bessent-He talks mentioned AI only briefly, giving no clear signal of its reception.

Why it matters The US-China tariff truce is due to lapse November 10, and rare-earth shipments and AI chip export controls sit alongside Taiwan as the summit’s hardest open questions.

Russia’s Duma election finalizes with a record majority amid fraud and hacking claims

United Russia is projected to take roughly 355 of 450 seats as independent outlets report a hacker’s claimed breach of the election system and an analysis suggesting the real vote share is far lower.

With about 95% counted, United Russia holds 57.83–57.86% of the party-list vote and leads in 208 of 225 single-member districts, on pace for roughly 355 of 450 seats — a new record, surpassing its 343-seat result in 2016. Official results are not due until October 5. Single source Novaya Gazeta Europe’s analysis, reported by the independent outlet Meduza, argues that without ballot-stuffing, United Russia would have won only about 34% of the vote — implying the official figure runs roughly 1.7 times an unmanipulated result. A separate, anonymous hacker group calling itself “CikLeak” claims to have breached the IT infrastructure of Russia’s Central Election Commission and of contractors building “Vybory 2.0,” the state’s automated election system, days before the vote, downloading server configurations and internal documents. Both claims rest on Meduza’s reporting chain and have not been independently confirmed; the Central Election Commission has not responded publicly to either. Meduza also reports nearly 1,300 Communist Party and Yabloko observers were barred from polling stations in various regions, and that observers in some regions were offered inducements to stay away.

Why it matters This is Russia’s first wartime Duma election, and the fraud and hacking claims — unverified but from a consistent independent-media sourcing chain — are the first hard allegations to surface since the vote began Friday.

Moscow Times · Al Jazeera · Euronews · Time · Meduza (Confirmed-multiple on the vote tally; Single-reliable-source on the fraud-scale estimate and hacking claim).

CNN, MS NOW and Politico sue over the White House press ban; the administration launches a 24/7 “Trump TV” stream

A federal judge — a Trump appointee who once ruled for CNN on similar grounds — has ordered the administration’s response today, as five networks suspend pool coverage in solidarity.

Trump announced the ban on Truth Social Friday, citing “FAKE NEWS”; credentials were pulled Saturday. The three outlets sued Monday morning in US District Court for DC, alleging First Amendment viewpoint discrimination and due-process violations, and naming Trump, Communications Director Steven Cheung, Secret Service Director Sean Curran and Chief of Staff Susie Wiles as defendants. The case was assigned to Judge Timothy Kelly, a Trump appointee who ruled for CNN’s Jim Acosta on due-process grounds in 2018. Kelly ordered the administration’s response to the outlets’ restraining-order request by today; a hearing has been widely reported for Wednesday afternoon, though that date should be treated as reported rather than docket-confirmed. ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox News and NBC suspended television press-pool coverage of presidential events in apparent solidarity. The White House responded with a statement titled “White House Access Is a Privilege — Not a Right,” citing Obama- and Biden-era press restrictions as precedent, and separately launched a 24/7 YouTube stream branded “Trump TV” Monday, opening with a rebroadcast of Trump’s Mount Rushmore speech.

What’s next The administration’s court filing today, and the reported Wednesday hearing on the restraining-order request.

ICE officer shoots Venezuelan delivery driver in Austin, drawing protests and a federal investigation

Hundreds rallied at the scene by Monday, demanding the release of body-camera footage after Sunday’s shooting.

An ICE officer shot and wounded Wilber Rafael Garcés Pérez, 28, a Venezuelan national delivering for DoorDash, near Research Boulevard and Anderson Square in north Austin on Sunday afternoon, after a foot pursuit; Austin police were not involved in the shooting itself. Garcés Pérez remains in federal custody at the Pearsall detention facility; DHS and ICE say they are leading the investigation “with assistance from the FBI” and describe him as in stable condition, held pending removal proceedings. Single source His attorney, Kate Lincoln-Goldfinch, alleges a bullet remains lodged near his spine and that he was denied pain medication and made to sleep on the floor — claims DHS has neither confirmed nor denied. The officer’s identity has not been released, and no bodycam footage is public. Rep. Greg Casar has demanded release of body- and dash-camera footage and recorded agent communications; more than 100 protesters gathered at the scene Sunday, dispersed by police using chemical agents. LULAC, the NAACP and county commissioners held a joint news conference Monday calling for accountability.

Paramount Skydance settles with 12 states, clearing the last hurdle to its $111B Warner Bros. Discovery merger

A consent decree commits the merged studio to $1.5 billion in added US film production and protections for California studio jobs.

Paramount Skydance (Nasdaq: PSKY — the post-merger ticker; earlier reporting citing the pre-merger “PARA” is stale) reached a settlement with California and eleven other state attorneys general, who had sued two months ago to block its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery. Under the consent decree, filed in the Northern District of California, the company commits to an additional $300 million a year in US film production — roughly $1.5 billion over five years — and a minimum release slate of 30 films a year for two years, rising to 32, or risks penalties including a possible forced divestiture of Miramax. It also commits, for five years, not to sell the Paramount or Warner Bros. studio lots in California, to negotiate cable-affiliate fees separately for each brand rather than sharing confidential rate data, and to honor existing union agreements and fund workforce training, under an independent monitoring trustee.

The deal, announced in February at $31.00 a share cash to WBD shareholders and already approved by shareholders in April, had targeted a third-quarter close; Paramount Skydance owes WBD shareholders a $7 million-a-day fee for every day past September 30 the deal remains open, which explains the settlement’s timing.

What’s next Court approval of the consent decree and an actual closing-date announcement.

Fed’s Bowman: supervisors missed SVB’s warning signs by early 2022; a new stress-test framework is coming “in the coming weeks”

An outside review found examiners should have flagged Silicon Valley Bank’s vulnerabilities more than a year before its 2023 collapse — and that social media, contrary to earlier post-mortems, did not meaningfully fuel the run.

In a September 18 speech, Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman detailed an outside review, by Starling Advisory Group, of SVB’s failure. Its findings: SVB collapsed from a “confluence of vulnerabilities” — unrealized securities losses exceeding capital, concentrated uninsured deposits, no discount-window readiness — that examiners should have flagged as early as March 2022; social media did not meaningfully fuel the run; and regulatory “tailoring” played no role. Bowman said a plausible recession scenario would have shown SVB’s capital falling below minimums as early as the fourth quarter of 2021. She outlined stress-test reforms already in motion: disclosing the model equations, variables and assumptions behind the tests; averaging two years of results to roughly halve swings in required capital buffers; a fixed balance-sheet freeze date; two distinct global market-shock scenarios; and 2027 revisions to how noninterest income is modeled. The Board will “consider final revisions…in the coming weeks,” aiming to finalize the reforms before year-end.

Why it matters This shapes how much capital banks must hold against future shocks, and lands the same week the Fed’s post-hike messaging faces its own test — see today’s calendar.

Federal Reserve speech text (primary) · Banking Dive · American Banker · ABA Banking Journal.

AMD crosses $1 trillion market value on an AI-chip rally that partly reversed before Tuesday’s open

A runaway app debut from Meta fed a broad chipmaker rally Monday; premarket Tuesday gave some of it back as long-end yields hit an 18-year high.

AMD (Nasdaq: AMD) rose roughly 10% Monday to close at $615.52, crossing $1 trillion in market value for the first time — a five-day rally of about 24% — becoming the fourth US chipmaker over $1 trillion after Nvidia, Broadcom and Micron. The rally followed Meta’s new “Muse” AI agent becoming the top free app on Apple’s App Store, with roughly 730,000 downloads in five days, feeding a broader narrative that AI agents need more inference compute; Intel (Nasdaq: INTC) jumped about 12%, Arm (Nasdaq: ARM) about 17%, and Meta (Nasdaq: META) itself rose more than 11%. Nvidia, AMD, Intel and Marvell all pulled back in Tuesday premarket trading, a move that coincided with the 30-year Treasury yield climbing to roughly 5.33% — its highest since 2007 — and with fading hopes for a near-term US-Iran settlement keeping oil elevated. Several outlets describe the pullback as macro profit-taking rather than a reassessment of AI spending plans; that reading is a likely contributor, not a confirmed single cause.

CNBC · Semafor · Bloomberg · Benzinga (Confirmed-multiple).

Pentagon’s own review reportedly points to stale intelligence and AI-assisted targeting in the deadly Minab school strike

Weeks after a UN mission found reasonable grounds that the strike was a war crime, US Central Command has reportedly completed an internal review Congress worries could be classified rather than released.

A UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission reported September 17 that it found reasonable grounds to believe US strikes on the Tree of Tayyiba Elementary School in Minab, Iran — which killed more than 150 people, including roughly 120 children — and on a sports complex in Lamerd constituted possible war crimes; the same mission found Iran committed crimes against humanity in its own protest crackdown. The White House rejected the findings, saying only Iran had committed war crimes; the Pentagon initially declined comment. Single source Anonymous-official accounts reported by NBC News and The Hill now say US Central Command has completed its own internal investigation into the Minab strike, describing findings of stale intelligence, staffing cuts and over-reliance on AI-assisted targeting as contributing factors — characterized by officials as “a cascade of preventable failures” rather than one decision. Those accounts also describe concern in Congress and within the Pentagon that the findings could be classified rather than made public. None of this has been confirmed by an on-record Pentagon statement.

Technology & AI

The “Plugin4Shell” zero-click flaw in AI coding agents, disclosed September 17, remains only partly patched: Anthropic’s Claude Code and OpenAI’s Codex fixed it weeks ago, but GitHub Copilot still has no patch, and Google is reportedly discontinuing the consumer Gemini CLI product entirely rather than fixing it (its enterprise Gemini Code Assist is unaffected). No exploitation in the wild has been confirmed. Separately, OpenAI published proposals Sunday urging US-led international technical standards for frontier AI systems, timed to this week’s UN AI discussions — a policy statement, not a product or regulatory action.

Two cybersecurity items worth flagging: Gyazo, the Japanese image-hosting service, disclosed a breach exposing 23.6 million user records and 490 million image-metadata records after an attacker exploited its upload server on September 11; no payment data was affected, but users who reused passwords should change them. And Japan’s National Police Agency joined the FBI and allied agencies in a September 18 advisory on “WaterPlum,” a long-running North Korea-linked campaign that poses as recruiters offering fake developer jobs to steal cryptocurrency — roughly 30,000 devices infected and $10.71 million stolen, suspected tied to North Korea’s military intelligence.
