How a Square Root Broke a Religion of Numbers

Monday, September 28, 2026 · 13 min read

One irrational number wrecked a two-thousand-year-old belief that the universe runs on whole-number ratios — and forced mathematics to invent the one tool, proof, that can make a claim true forever.

Mathematics

Day 1 of 300 · Mathematics 1 of 20 · Numbers, infinity, and the birth of proof

The Big Idea

Every number you use today didn’t arrive as a finished set. It was built in layers, over thousands of years, and each layer exists because the one before it couldn’t answer a question people actually needed answered. Counting sheep or coins needs nothing more than 1, 2, 3, and onward. Recording a debt, or a temperature below freezing, needs a way to go past zero in the other direction. Splitting a loaf into thirds needs a way to name a quantity sitting between two whole numbers. For a long stretch of history, a reasonable person could believe that was the whole story — that any quantity worth measuring was either a whole number or a simple ratio of two of them.

That belief is wrong, and proving it wrong is one of the oldest surviving arguments in mathematics. Some lengths cannot be written as a ratio of whole numbers, no matter how large a numerator and denominator you allow — not “nobody has found the right fraction yet,” but “no such fraction exists, and this can be shown with total certainty.” The first number shown to have this property was uncovered by a Greek mathematical sect that had built its entire worldview on the opposite assumption, and the discovery reportedly frightened its members enough that they tried to bury it.

What actually makes this worth fifteen minutes isn’t the number itself. It’s the method used to pin it down: mathematical proof, a way of establishing that a claim holds in every case that could ever exist, not just every case checked so far. That distinction — between “true so far” and “true, permanently, by necessity” — is the single habit of mind every later lesson in this block will lean on.

How It Works

Follow the number system through its actual stages, because the order matters more than it looks.

The natural numbers — 1, 2, 3, and on — are the counting numbers. They have no inventor; every counting culture in history has arrived at some version of them independently.

The integers extend the naturals with zero and the negative numbers, so a system can represent “none” and “owed” as well as “some.” The clearest early source for doing this rigorously is Indian: the astronomer and mathematician Brahmagupta, writing around 628 CE in his treatise the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, gave explicit arithmetic rules for what he called dhana (“fortune,” positive quantities) and rina (“debt,” negative quantities), alongside shunya, zero — the first known systematic treatment of both. European mathematics took much longer to accept the idea: negative numbers were still being dismissed by some working mathematicians as “absurd” or “fictitious” more than a thousand years later. A useful idea doesn’t spread on a predictable schedule.

Extend again with fractions — any integer divided by a nonzero integer — and you get the rational numbers: 1/2, 22/7, and every integer itself, since 5 is the same as 5/1. Rational numbers cover essentially everything a person measuring by hand will ever need, and Egyptian, Babylonian, Chinese and Greek mathematicians all used them confidently, long before anyone suspected there was more to the number line.

That is where a Greek brotherhood called the Pythagoreans — founded by the philosopher and mathematician Pythagoras in the Greek colony of Croton, in southern Italy, around the 6th century BCE — believed the story ended. Their guiding conviction, often summarized as “all is number,” held that every relationship in the universe reduced to a ratio of whole numbers. It wasn’t blind faith: they had real evidence. Plucking two strings whose lengths stand in a simple ratio — 2 to 1, 3 to 2 — produces the intervals a musical ear finds most consonant, the octave and the fifth. Reality really did seem to run on tidy fractions.

Then someone inside the school — later tradition names Hippasus of Metapontum, though the sourcing is thin and semi-legendary — examined the simplest shape available: a square with sides of length 1. Its diagonal, related to the sides by the theorem that bears the school’s founder’s name (though Babylonian scribes were using the same relationship over a thousand years earlier), has a length equal to the square root of 2. What fraction is that?

None. Not “none found yet” — none exists, for any pair of whole numbers whatsoever, and the argument that nails this down is in the Equation / Model section below. A length sitting inside the most basic shape in geometry turned out to violate the Pythagoreans’ entire picture of reality. Later legend has Hippasus thrown overboard at sea for revealing the secret — a story most historians treat as embellishment rather than documented fact, though the mathematical shock behind it was genuine regardless of how the brotherhood actually responded.

The fix that stuck wasn’t “invent irrational numbers” — that step was still two thousand years away, arriving with 19th-century mathematicians such as Richard Dedekind, who gave the real numbers a fully rigorous construction in 1872. The Greeks’ own solution, worked out roughly a century after the crisis by Eudoxus of Cnidus and preserved in Book V of Euclid’s Elements, was a theory of proportion: a way to compare any two geometric lengths, whether or not they shared a common unit of measure, without ever having to name the troublesome length as a number at all. It was an elegant workaround, and it held Greek geometry together for roughly two millennia.

Only later did mathematicians fold these awkward lengths fully into the number system. Numbers that cannot be written as a ratio of integers — the square root of 2, pi, the constant e — are irrational numbers. Combine the rationals and irrationals and you get the real numbers: every point on the number line, with nothing left out. That is the number system nearly all of modern mathematics, science and engineering is built on.

The second and more consequential thing this story delivers is mathematical proof itself: a chain of logical steps, starting from statements already accepted, that forces a conclusion to hold with no exceptions permitted, ever — not “true in every instance examined,” which is how ordinary evidence-based reasoning works, but true in every instance that could ever exist. That is precisely why the Pythagoreans couldn’t wave the diagonal away as an unusually sloppy measurement. A proof doesn’t care how carefully anyone measures afterward. Every theorem this Mathematics block covers over its remaining nineteen lessons rests on exactly this kind of argument.

Key Concepts

Natural numbers
The counting numbers, 1, 2, 3 and onward — the oldest layer of the number system, with no single inventor.
Integers
The natural numbers extended with zero and the negative numbers, letting a system represent “none” and “owed” as well as “some.”
Rational numbers
Any number expressible as one integer divided by another nonzero integer — a ratio, such as 3/4 or −7/2.
Irrational numbers
Real numbers that cannot be written as any ratio of two integers, however large — the square root of 2 and pi are the two most famous examples.
Real numbers
The rationals and irrationals together: every point on the number line, with no gaps.
Mathematical proof
A chain of logical steps from accepted starting points that establishes a claim as true in every possible case, permanently — not merely true in every case checked so far.

Example / Application

Here is an irrational number doing real, audible work: the tuning of every piano, guitar fret, and synthesizer built to Western standard pitch.

The Pythagoreans were onto something real when they linked simple whole-number ratios to pleasant-sounding intervals — a 2:1 length ratio really does give an octave, and 3:2 really does give what musicians call a perfect fifth. The trouble is that a keyboard tuned so every interval hits its pure whole-number ratio exactly cannot also let a musician play in every key and move freely between them; the pure ratios don’t stack up evenly around the octave, so an instrument tuned that way sounds correct in some keys and audibly sour in others. The solution modern instruments use instead is equal temperament: divide the octave into twelve equal steps (semitones), where each step is the same frequency ratio as every other step. That ratio has to satisfy one condition — stack twelve of them and you land exactly on the octave’s 2:1 ratio — which makes it the twelfth root of 2, an irrational number close to 1.059463.

Two people worked this out independently, roughly two thousand years after the Pythagoreans, without knowledge of each other’s work: the Ming-dynasty Chinese prince and music theorist Zhu Zaiyu, who published a precise calculation of the twelfth root of 2 (worked out by hand to 24 decimal places) in 1584, and the Flemish mathematician and engineer Simon Stevin, who derived the same formula around 1605 — though his manuscript went unpublished and unnoticed until 1884, roughly three centuries later.

Take the note A above middle C, standard concert pitch at 440 Hz. Equal temperament fixes every adjacent semitone’s frequency ratio at 2^(1/12) ≈ 1.059463. The next semitone up, A♯, is:

440 Hz × 1.059463 ≈ 466.16 Hz

Now compare the equal-tempered fifth (seven semitones up from any note) against the Pythagoreans’ pure 3:2 ratio. Seven equal-tempered steps multiply out to 2^(7/12) ≈ 1.498307 — not 1.5. That is a real, measurable gap: about two cents flat (a cent is 1/100th of a semitone), small enough that most listeners don’t consciously notice it, large enough that a trained ear or a tuning meter can. Every fifth on a modern piano is quietly, deliberately, a little out of tune with the ratio the Pythagoreans considered mathematically perfect — the price paid so the instrument can play equally well in any of the twelve keys, instead of being pure in one key and dissonant in the rest.

Equation / Model

Here is the actual argument that turned “no one has found the right fraction” into “no fraction can exist” — one of the oldest surviving examples of proof by contradiction: assume the opposite of the claim you are trying to establish, reason forward using only steps everyone already accepts, and if that chain of reasoning lands somewhere impossible, the assumption you started with has to be the thing that is false.

Assume, for the sake of argument, that the square root of 2 is rational — that it equals some fraction a/b, where a and b are whole numbers reduced to lowest terms (no common factor left between them; if there were one, you’d have already divided it out, the way 4/6 reduces to 2/3).

a² = 2b²

a squared equals two times b squared.

a
a whole number, the numerator of the assumed fraction a/b equal to the square root of 2
b
a whole number, the denominator of that fraction, sharing no common factor with a

That line follows from squaring both sides of “the square root of 2 equals a/b” and clearing the fraction (multiply both sides by b²). Now trace where it leads. The right-hand side, 2b², is 2 times a whole number, so it’s even — which forces a² to be even as well. A whole number’s square is only even when the number itself is even (multiply two odd numbers and the result is always odd, never even), so a itself must be even. Write a as 2k for some whole number k, and substitute it back into a² = 2b²: (2k)² = 2b² works out to 4k² = 2b², which simplifies to b² = 2k².

That is the identical shape of statement this proof started with — which means, by the same reasoning just used on a, b must be even too.

And there is the contradiction: a and b are both even, meaning they share a factor of 2 — directly contradicting the starting condition that a and b share no common factor at all. Nothing about the reasoning that got here was in dispute; every step followed by ordinary logic from the step before it. The only thing left that can be false is the original assumption. The square root of 2 cannot be written as a/b for any whole numbers a and b. It is irrational, permanently — no amount of searching, no faster computer, no cleverer fraction will ever turn one up, because the proof shows the fraction cannot exist in the first place.

Why It Matters

Two things from today are worth keeping, and both resurface constantly for the rest of this block and beyond it.

The first is the number system’s layered structure itself. Naturals, then integers, then rationals, then reals — each layer built specifically to hold a quantity the previous layer couldn’t represent. Every later lesson in this Mathematics block quietly assumes you know which of these systems it’s operating in, and why that choice was necessary. It matters past pure mathematics too: an ordinary computer stores numbers with a fixed number of binary digits, so it can only ever approximate an irrational number — the identical limitation that keeps it from storing many everyday fractions, like 0.1, exactly either.

The second, larger thing is proof itself. Nearly everything you’re told with confidence — in the news, in business, in most of experimental science — is a claim resting on evidence: it has held up in every case examined, so far. That is genuinely valuable, but it is provisional; one new case can overturn it tomorrow. A mathematical proof is a different category of claim entirely. Once the square root of 2 is shown irrational, no future discovery can ever reverse it, because the claim was never about how the world has behaved in the past — it is a claim about what necessarily follows from the definitions themselves. Telling those two kinds of “true” apart — true in everything observed, versus true, full stop, forever — is the habit of mind this whole block exists to build.

It also sets up two specific things ahead in this block. The real number line built today — a line with no gaps left in it — is what Day 9’s formal treatment of the limit will lean on: without a real number to actually arrive at, a sequence of ever-closer fractions approaching the square root of 2 has nowhere solid to land, and calculus can’t get started. And the deeper question of what proof itself is capable of settling, and what it provably cannot, returns with much larger stakes on Day 20, when this block closes by looking at Cantor’s work on the sizes of infinity and Gödel’s incompleteness theorems.

Remember These
  • The number system grew in layers — naturals, then integers, then rationals, then reals — each layer built to represent a quantity the last one couldn’t.
  • An irrational number, like the square root of 2, cannot be written as any ratio of two whole numbers — not undiscovered, but proven impossible.
  • A mathematical proof establishes a claim as true in every possible case, permanently, from pure logic — not by checking examples, however many.
  • Proof by contradiction: assume the opposite of the claim, follow the logic, and if it leads somewhere impossible, the original claim has to be true.
  • Irrational numbers aren’t just an abstraction — the twelfth root of 2 is quietly built into the tuning of every modern piano and guitar.
Think About It

The Pythagoreans considered the pure 3:2 ratio mathematically perfect, and equal temperament deliberately abandons it — every fifth on a modern piano is slightly “wrong” by their standard. Yet equal temperament is what the overwhelming majority of instruments actually use today. Given what you now know about why equal temperament was adopted, what does that trade-off suggest about when exact mathematical purity is the right goal for a real system to have, and when a good-enough approximation that works everywhere is actually the better choice?
