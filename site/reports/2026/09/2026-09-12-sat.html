The Week’s Ten Stories

The Gulf war split into three fronts this week — and none of them cooled off

A direct Iran-US exchange, a separate Saudi-Houthi war, and a fragile diplomatic opening all ran at once; by Friday, Saudi Arabia had shut its own oil pipeline and Houthi forces held the strait’s Yemeni-side islands.

It started before the week even began: Iran fired ballistic missiles at a US carrier and a destroyer Saturday Sept. 5 (both evaded, no casualties), and CENTCOM answered by disabling or sinking three Iranian tankers near Kharg Island, Jask and in the Gulf of Oman. By Wednesday the cadence sharpened: CENTCOM destroyed five more tankers (ten in a week) after the IRGC twice tried to hit a US warship, and Iran retaliated with roughly twenty missiles at Jordan’s Al Azraq air base, which hosts US troops (Jordan intercepted eighteen of twenty). That same day brought a disputed Iranian claim of striking two US Navy destroyers — CENTCOM called it “completely FALSE,” and no independent evidence ever surfaced on either side; it stayed unresolved through the whole week and into today (see Scorecard). Thursday, IRGC forces claimed a further strike on a US Navy unmanned surface vessel near the strait, and four projectiles hit two vessels near Khasab, Oman — a fourth distinct exchange inside ten days.

Running alongside it, and largely independent of it, was a second front: the Houthis hit Saudi Aramco facilities Monday (73 wounded), Saudi Arabia and the Houthis traded strikes for days, and by Thursday the Houthis had captured the Red Sea port of Mocha — with Mohammed bin Salman calling Trump twice asking for direct US strikes and being turned down (intelligence support only). Friday added a third dimension: Saudi Arabia shut its roughly seven-million-barrel-per-day East-West pipeline after attacks near Riyadh and Medina, struck Mocha’s airport, and Houthi forces took the Bab el-Mandeb islands of Perim and Zuqar. By Saturday, per weekend reporting (Today’s News, above), that sweep had reportedly extended to the rest of Yemen’s Red Sea coast. The one counter-note: Iran agreed Friday to meet a six-nation Gulf Cooperation Council bloc in Salalah, Oman, Monday on the strait’s future — the first such diplomatic opening since the war began, now set against a considerably harder weekend.

Why it matters What this report tracked as a single US-Iran standoff a month ago is now three semi-independent conflicts running in parallel, each capable of producing the week’s biggest story on its own — and none of the three de-escalated even once this week.
What’s next Whether Monday’s Oman talks survive the weekend’s further escalation, and whether the unresolved Iranian destroyer-strike claim ever gets independently confirmed or refuted.

Core CPI ran 0.3% month over month even as its year-over-year pace eased; hike odds for Wednesday’s Sept. 16 decision jumped to roughly 85–90% from about 70% beforehand — and stocks rallied through the print anyway.

The week’s frame going in was that Friday’s CPI was the one scheduled release that could move a decision the entire FOMC — including new Chair Kevin Warsh — was already sitting in blackout for (Sept. 5–17). Consensus estimates were unusually scattered all week, ranging roughly 2.9% to 3.4% year over year on the headline number. Thursday’s PPI ran hot on energy (headline 0.4%, 5.4% year over year, diesel prices up 24.1% month over month) even though core PPI undershot at 0.2%; the 10-year Treasury yield hit its highest close since November 2023 anyway. Futures-implied odds of a Sept. 16 hike jumped to roughly 85–90% from about 70% beforehand. Equities rallied broadly through the print regardless: the S&P rose 0.86%, the Nasdaq 0.96%, the Dow 0.98% — a divergence this report labeled Unexplained rather than force a story onto it. Bloomberg separately reported that Trump and other administration officials are pressing against a hike, setting up open tension with a hawkish incoming chair three days before the vote.

Why it matters A foreign war’s oil shock is now visibly inside the Fed’s own inflation data four days before a live, contested rate decision, with reported White House pressure on the incoming chair adding a political dimension this report has not seen at this intensity before.

Missouri’s congressional-map fight escalates to a contempt hearing — then resolves in under 48 hours

The state supreme court held Secretary of State Hoskins in contempt Thursday, he complied and purged himself of it the same day, after the full US Supreme Court stayed a competing federal order that had produced weeks of “competing court orders” confusion.

Missouri’s own emergency application had asked for a merits ruling by roughly Sept. 13–14, five days after its own Sept. 8 ballot-certification deadline. Tuesday: the US Supreme Court denied Missouri’s stay, but a federal judge separately blocked the state’s old map too, producing genuinely competing court orders. Wednesday: the confusion sharpened when the Eighth Circuit declined to stay the federal order (leaving the newer map controlling) while the state supreme court’s chief justice ordered Secretary of State Denny Hoskins to show cause for contempt; Missouri Republicans floated impeaching the state justices. Thursday: the full US Supreme Court itself stayed the federal order, leaving the state court’s 2022-map ruling as the sole governing order; Hoskins complied and the state court said he had purged himself of contempt, with no fine or further sanction imposed. Whether the court will still separately sanction him for his earlier defiance remains unaddressed, not foreclosed.

Why it matters This report’s own forecast that Thursday’s hearing would produce a clarifying outcome by Sept. 11 graded correct this week — a state supreme court show-cause hearing, backed by a same-week federal stay resolving the competing-order problem, moved faster and more decisively than the parallel, still-unresolved mail-ballot case at the same court.

USPS v. California, the mail-ballot rule, sat fully briefed and unresolved for the entire week

A dozen-plus amicus briefs later, the emergency docket has neither granted nor denied the government’s stay application — and this report’s own forecast that the Court would act within 72 hours of the briefing deadline graded wrong.

The Justice Department withdrew and refiled its emergency application earlier this cycle after Judge Talwani’s fuller preliminary injunction against the USPS mail-ballot screening rule; the case is now docketed as USPS v. California, No. 26A305. States and voting-rights groups filed their opposition by Justice Jackson’s 4:00 PM EDT Wednesday deadline, with more than a dozen amicus briefs accompanying it. As of Saturday, no Supreme Court order has issued; the First Circuit did deny a related, narrower USPS stay motion Sept. 10, but that is not a ruling on the underlying Talwani injunction the government is actually asking the Court to stay.

Why it matters Shadow-docket rulings often take longer than a “respond by” deadline implies, especially with heavy amicus briefing — a lesson this report is carrying into how it frames future emergency-docket timelines rather than assuming a response deadline doubles as a ruling deadline.
What’s next North Carolina continues mailing ballots on schedule under separate litigation while the case remains pending; no forcing deadline is visible on the current docket.

AI-safety oversight of OpenAI and Anthropic escalated on every front this week, funneling into Tuesday’s deadline

A public researcher resignation, a fourth self-disclosed Anthropic incident, a Chinese-distillation accusation, an autonomous-agent breach of 395 organizations, and a second, separate Senate probe all landed inside five days.

OpenAI acknowledged (after initially disputing) its “wiki incident,” reclassified internally as “misalignment” rather than “security.” GPT-6 Astra crossed a “Critical” cyber-capability threshold in its own system card, and an OpenAI safety researcher voiced on-record concern about declining chain-of-thought monitorability. Rep. Casar rejected both companies’ prior written answers as inadequate and set a Sept. 15 deadline. Midweek, Anthropic disclosed a fourth internal incident and hired outside monitor METR; Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon publicly resigned, warning neither major lab “is acting responsibly” on self-improving AI, and two colleagues (Evan Hubinger and Samuel Marks) backed him on the record — Hubinger telling press he puts catastrophe odds above 10% within a decade. An OpenAI-claimed proof of the Navier-Stokes existence-and-smoothness problem triggered a separate authorship dispute with an Anthropic researcher; OpenAI has since posted a public rebuttal, and the underlying mathematical claim remains uncertified by the Clay Institute or peer review. Friday alone added three more threads: GreyNoise reported an autonomous AI-agent swarm breached 395 organizations across 48 countries via PaperCut print-server flaws; Anthropic accused five China-based firms (Alibaba, Moonshot AI, DeepSeek, Xiaomi, Zhipu) of mass-distilling Claude across roughly 200 million flagged exchanges, an allegation resting entirely on Anthropic’s own account with no independent verification found and no response yet from any named company; and Sen. Josh Hawley opened a second, Senate-side investigation into OpenAI’s July Hugging Face agent-swarm incident, with a document demand due Oct. 1.

Why it matters The pattern shifted this week from labs grudgingly admitting past failures to labs’ own employees publicly dissenting, with bipartisan congressional scrutiny (Casar on the House side, Hawley on the Senate side) widening rather than narrowing — all converging on a deadline that lands one day before the Fed’s own contested vote.
What’s next Tuesday’s deadline for OpenAI and Anthropic to answer Rep. Casar’s questions; no weekend response from either company as of this writing.

Apple’s first product launch under new CEO John Ternus turned from mixed to markedly positive over four days

Tuesday’s iPhone Duo reveal drew a visible-crease critique and a modest share decline; by Thursday, hands-on coverage called it “the most exciting new iPhone since the iPhone X,” with demand reportedly outrunning early supply.

Apple unveiled its first foldable, the iPhone Duo, Tuesday under new CEO John Ternus — the company’s first major hardware bet since his appointment. By Thursday, hands-on coverage turned markedly more positive; Bloomberg News called it “the most exciting new iPhone since the iPhone X in 2017” after time with the device at Apple Park, and separate reporting (not an Apple statement) said the company expects to sell as many units as it can manufacture through the first six months — a stronger demand signal than Tuesday’s “extremely limited” supply framing. Shares rose 3.56% Thursday, the clearest mega-cap tech outperformer that session. iFixit’s teardown Friday scored the device 2 out of 10 for repairability.

Why it matters The reception arc — skeptical reveal, warmer hands-on coverage, then a durability caveat — is the clearest test yet of consumer appetite for a foldable iPhone under a new head of product.

Two neglected disasters kept worsening in the background: DRC’s Ebola outbreak and Nepal-Tibet’s flood toll

DRC’s Ebola count jumped by 99 cases and 39 deaths in a single day Friday — its steepest acceleration yet — while Nepal’s flood toll passed 1,340 with a newly reported second hazard: a rising glacial lake at the original collapse site.

The Democratic Republic of Congo’s Ebola outbreak, driven by the Bundibugyo strain outside standard vaccine coverage, reached 6,942 confirmed cases and 3,349 deaths through Sept. 10 per WHO and DRC health-ministry data — up from 6,686 and 3,226 the day before, a jump of 99 cases and 39 deaths in a single day, the steepest single-day acceleration this report has tracked in the outbreak. The Week in Markets

The S&P 500 fell 0.80% on the week: 7,718.60 (Fri Sept 4) to 7,656.98 (Fri Sept 11). The Nasdaq Composite fell 0.66% (26,506.99 to 26,333.04); the Dow fell 1.57% (53,414.25 to 52,573.29); the Russell 2000 fell 2.41% (2,975.65 to 2,903.94) — small caps underperformed every other major index, consistent with a week dominated by rate and geopolitical risk rather than idiosyncratic stock-picking. None of those net weekly numbers describe the week that actually happened: after Monday’s Labor Day closure, the S&P fell four straight sessions (Tue −0.58%, Wed −0.48%, Thu −0.58%) on the Gulf escalation and climbing yields, then rallied 0.86% Friday despite a hot CPI print and a widening war — a genuine reversal, not a quiet drift to a small net loss.

Sector leadership tilted defensively into the escalation: energy (XLE) led at +1.69% on the oil rally, followed by communication services (XLC) +0.51% and technology (XLK) +0.21% (with semiconductors, SMH, essentially flat at +0.27%). Health care (XLV) was the week’s clear laggard at −3.55%, followed by materials (XLB) −2.84%, financials (XLF) −1.46%, industrials (XLI) −1.65%, consumer discretionary (XLY) −1.70%, regional banks (KRE) −1.82%, real estate (XLRE) −1.16%, consumer staples (XLP) −1.42% and utilities (XLU) −1.60%. Only energy and, marginally, tech and communications held up against a broadly negative sector tape.

Rates rose meaningfully: the 10-year Treasury yield climbed 18 basis points to 4.96% (its highest level this report has tracked recently), the 2-year rose 26 basis points to 4.63%, and the 2s10s spread flattened 8 basis points to 33 — consistent with a market pricing sharply higher near-term hike odds. High-yield spreads widened modestly (2.68% to a 2.71% midweek peak, 2.70% by Friday), a small but real crack in an otherwise tight credit market. Oil was the week’s standout mover: WTI rose from $91.48 to $100.05 (+9.4%), touching $102.48 Thursday; Brent rose from roughly $95 to $104.61, touching an intraday high above $108 Thursday — both holding most of the week’s Gulf-driven surge into the weekend. Gold fell modestly (roughly $4,431 to $4,366 on the GLD-equivalent basis) despite the geopolitical backdrop, pressured by the sharp rise in real yields. VIX closed the week at 15.84, down from 14.53 the prior Friday but with a genuinely volatile path between them: an intraweek high of 18.17 Thursday, then an 11.2% single-day drop Friday — one of its sharper reversals this report has tracked. Bitcoin fell 5.8% on the week (roughly $81,265 to $76,555, Friday-to-Friday), with a further modest pullback into Saturday; the crypto market’s pullback was more pronounced than equities’ own net decline.

Scorecard

Thirty-five forecasts came due this week — seventeen resolved inline during the week’s own editions, eighteen graded in today’s sweep: 18 correct, 14 wrong, 3 partial.

This report’s own scenario matrix missed cleanly for the second time in three weeks. All four of last Sunday’s scenarios — base, bull, bear and shock — graded wrong or partial, and none graded outright correct. The base case assumed CPI would land close to consensus, the Gulf conflict would continue without a further direct Iran-US exchange, and equities would trade a narrow range; instead CPI ran well hot, a further Iran-US exchange did occur (the Jordan base strike and five more tankers), and equities moved a full percentage point or more on multiple days. The bull case failed on every leg: CPI ran hot, the Gulf conflict escalated rather than de-escalated, oil stayed elevated, and VIX closed above its “stays below 15” bar even after a sharp Friday drop. The bear case graded partial — it correctly called a hot CPI and a further Gulf incident, but inverted the market reaction: equities rallied rather than gave back gains, yields eased rather than rose further, and VIX plunged rather than stayed elevated. The shock case’s specific triggers (US casualties, a North Korean test during the drill window itself, a mass-casualty pause collapse) never clearly fired, even as VIX moved the opposite direction from what a shock would require. Worth naming directly: this framework keeps describing plausible-sounding conditions that the market or the news does not actually deliver, in either the gentle or severe direction.

The Gulf-war and Ukraine forecasts held up better than almost anything else this report wrote this week. Correct: a further direct Iran-US exchange occurring within the week (twice, on separate 72-hour windows); the IRGC’s destroyer-strike claim remaining uncorroborated through the full week; Israel conducting further Lebanon strikes within 72 hours; no renewed Kyiv/Moscow pause being announced, with strikes continuing at pre-pause tempo; Brent holding above $90 at Friday’s close; and WTI closing above $95 on multiple sessions. Where this thread’s forecasts erred, they erred in a specific and instructive way: North Korea’s missile test came one day after the drill window this report was watching, not during it — a near-miss on timing, not on substance — and the Supreme Court did not rule on the mail-ballot case within the 72-hour window this report expected, a reminder that shadow-docket rulings often take longer than a response deadline implies.

Two procedural forecasts on notices of appeal graded wrong, and this report is treating them as genuinely unconfirmed rather than assumed. Neither a federal notice of appeal in the Anthropic Pentagon ruling nor one in the Boardman birthright-citizenship injunction could be confirmed filed by their respective deadlines; both were graded wrong on the conservative principle that “expected to appeal” language in press coverage is not the same as a confirmed docket filing. If either filing surfaces later, this ledger will be revisited.

VIX missed its own threshold by 0.16 points. Last Sunday’s forecast that VIX would close Friday above 16 graded wrong on a razor-thin margin: it closed at 15.84, despite an intraweek high of 18.17 Thursday. Peak-week stress did not carry through to Friday’s close once the week’s binary catalyst (CPI) resolved without a full shock — a near-miss is still logged as a miss.
