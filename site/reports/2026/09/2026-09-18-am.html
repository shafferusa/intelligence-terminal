Top Stories

Trump Says He Faces a “Big Decision” on Whether to “Annihilate” Iran’s Regime

The comment lands the same week he claims “direct” contact with Tehran and hope the war is “nearing the end” — and as the Pentagon’s own investigation into February’s deadly school strike remains unresolved.

In a Thursday interview with Axios, Trump described an approaching, unresolved choice on Iran: “I have a big decision coming up. Do I want to go in and annihilate them or do I not?” Officials cited by Axios said the US military “can’t stay in its current holding pattern much longer,” and pointed to a meeting with Gulf leaders — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and Oman — expected on the sidelines of next week’s UN General Assembly in New York as a likely moment of clarity.

The remark sits in direct tension with what Trump has said publicly this week: that he has been in “direct” contact with Iran and hopes the war is “nearing the end.” Iran’s Supreme National Security Council secretary, Mohsen Rezaei, rejected that framing within hours, accusing Trump of sending “mixed signals” and saying there will be no talks until Iran’s own conditions — reparations, sanctions relief — are met. Vice President Vance said Wednesday the war’s “first phase,” the destruction of Iran’s nuclear program and conventional military capability, is finished, and a “fundamentally different phase” begins within a month or two — without saying whether that means more fighting or less.

The backdrop is Tuesday’s UN fact-finding mission report, which found reasonable grounds for a US war crime in February’s Minab school strike and for Iranian crimes against humanity in its own protest crackdown. The White House dismissed the council Thursday as having “accomplished nothing for human rights while spouting unserious nonsense,” and the Pentagon says its own Minab investigation “remains ongoing.” Single source One unnamed US official told AP the strike was likely the result of outdated targeting coordinates supplied by the Defense Intelligence Agency — a specific explanation this report is not treating as confirmed.

Why it matters The same man, in the same week, has publicly described both hope for an imminent end to the war and a live decision on whether to escalate it — a genuine, unresolved contradiction in the government’s own stated position, not a rhetorical one.
What’s next The Gulf-leaders meeting expected around next Tuesday’s UN General Assembly opening is the clearest scheduled moment where this could resolve one way or the other.

The Saudi-Houthi War Rattles Oil Markets as Riyadh Notches a First Death and Seeks F-35s

Oil fell toward $100 a barrel even as the war worsened — investors weighing fresh Saudi supply against a conflict that just claimed its first confirmed Saudi-side casualty.

Saudi Arabia’s civil defense said Thursday that a Yemeni resident was killed by debris from a drone intercepted over Taif — the first death Riyadh has announced since Houthi attacks on Saudi cities escalated last week, on top of more than 80 people already reported wounded. Oil prices fell for a third straight session Friday, with reporting putting West Texas Intermediate down roughly 1.9% to about $100.05 a barrel, briefly dipping under $100, and Brent down about 2.2% to roughly $102.57, as investors weighed the fresh strikes against signs that additional Saudi crude could reach global markets and ease supply concerns even as the fighting itself intensifies. Data note This report’s own market-data feed showed an anomalous, far larger overnight move in the same contract that multiple outlets’ reporting did not corroborate; the figures above rely on that outside reporting rather than this report’s usual direct feed.

Separately Thursday, the State Department approved a possible $24.3 billion Foreign Military Sale of 48 F-35A fighters and 49 F135 engines to Saudi Arabia — still subject to congressional review, where opposition is expected since Israel remains the only regional operator of the jet and has voiced concern. Treasury’s OFAC also sanctioned BitBank, an Iran-linked crypto exchange accused of processing hundreds of millions of dollars in Bitcoin payments this summer from a self-styled “Hormuz Safe Marine Services Authority” — described as an extortion scheme charging tankers $1–2 million for safe transit through the Strait, with proceeds routed to the IRGC, under the administration’s “Operation Economic Outcast.”

The widening war already includes a disputed Saudi claim of intercepting a drone aimed at Mecca, a disputed AP report that Saudi Arabia asked France, the UK, Pakistan and Egypt for air-defense help after running low on interceptors (which Turkey and Pakistan continue to deny), and Houthi forces’ completed control of the Bab el-Mandeb corridor.

Why it matters A conflict simultaneously being fought and having its oil-market consequences hedged by traders is unusual on its own; a $24.3 billion fighter-jet sale mid-conflict is a major strategic signal of US backing for Riyadh.

Trump Says He Told Warsh He “Might As Well” Vote With the Board; Warsh Won’t Discuss It

Two days after the Fed’s hawkish hike, Trump adds detail to his pressure campaign while sparing the chairman personal blame — and Warsh declines to say anything about their conversation at all.

At Wednesday’s post-meeting press conference, Chair Kevin Warsh declined to discuss any conversation with Trump: “I’ve got nothing for you on a discussion with the president,” he said, reiterating that “the plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long.” Trump posted on Truth Social that “Interest Rates in the United States should be 1%, or less,” and told reporters he had informed Warsh he “might as well vote with the Board because it’s not gonna matter,” calling the committee “very hostile” and “very political.” He notably spared Warsh personal blame, saying he retains confidence in him.

White House spokesman Kush Desai told Fox News that Trump “absolutely” still believes in Fed independence — a notable tension alongside the administration’s separate, still-unescalated threat to halt trade with surplus countries if the Fed does not cut rates. A White House economic adviser separately told CNBC ahead of Wednesday’s decision that a hike would be a “mistake.” Governor Christopher Waller, a dovish outlier before the vote, had flagged improving inflation data and said he was willing to “give disinflation a chance” — a position that did not carry Wednesday’s unanimous 12-0 vote.

Why it matters Markets are, so far, pricing continued Fed independence — yields eased and stocks rallied broadly Thursday — even as the political pressure campaign escalates rhetorically without yet deepening structurally.

Massie and Khanna Say They Have the Votes to Force a Second Epstein Files Vote

Four Republicans and every Democrat have committed to a discharge petition seeking legal standing to sue over the first transparency law’s enforcement — but the House won’t reconvene to vote until after the midterms.

Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Ro Khanna (D-CA) announced Thursday they have commitments for all 218 signatures needed to force a floor vote on the “Epstein Files Transparency Act II,” which would give state attorneys general, Epstein’s victims and members of Congress standing to sue the Justice Department in court over noncompliance with the original Transparency Act’s disclosure mandate. Four Republicans have committed — Massie, Ralph Norman (SC), Nancy Mace (SC) and Brian Fitzpatrick (PA) — joined by every Democrat, with two absent members reportedly set to sign when the House returns.

This is the same pair’s second successful discharge petition on the subject; the original Transparency Act passed the House 427-1 in November 2025. Because the House remains adjourned until after the November midterms, no vote can occur until members return to Washington.

Early Voting for the 2026 Midterms Opens Today in Three States

Virginia, South Dakota and Minnesota begin mailing and accepting ballots six weeks before Election Day, in a cycle that will decide control of both chambers of Congress.

Friday, September 18 marks the start of ballot mailing and in-person absentee voting in Virginia, South Dakota and Minnesota, the first states to open voting for the November 3, 2026 midterms. In Minnesota, the entire state legislature, every statewide office, all of the state’s US House seats and one US Senate seat are on the ballot; full in-person early voting there opens October 16. Republicans currently hold narrow majorities in both the House and Senate.

An Internal DHS Document Raises Questions About a Noncitizen-Voting Effort Tied to the Midterms

NPR reports an email paired a list of counties offering non-English ballots with a DOJ prosecution checklist; DHS says it is not targeting polling places.

Single source NPR reports it obtained an internal DHS email, dated last December and released through a Democratic National Committee public-records lawsuit, in which a DHS border-policy official sent a 78-page list of counties and states that offer non-English-language election materials or ballots to Rob Law, DHS’s undersecretary for strategy, policy and plans, and to Heather Honey, who leads the administration’s noncitizen-voting effort. Attached to the email was a DOJ “criminal checklist” outlining requirements for prosecuting voter-related charges. Democrats quoted by NPR said they fear the pairing could intimidate or entangle eligible voters, particularly in non-English-speaking communities, ahead of the midterms.

DHS told NPR on the record that it “is not planning operations targeting polling locations.” NPR’s story does not report any concrete enforcement action tied to the list, and this report has not independently verified the email beyond NPR’s own account of it.

What’s next No follow-up action has been reported; this report will note any confirmed operational use of the list, or any further DHS or DNC statement, in a future edition.

Canada’s Carney Embraces the EU’s “Associate Member” Offer, Without Naming Trump

Addressing the European Parliament a day after Trump called the idea a possible “hostile act,” Carney proposed deeper integration on trade, defense and critical minerals — and got a debate, not yet a vote, from Ottawa.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney formally addressed the European Parliament in Strasbourg on Thursday, welcoming the EU’s proposed associate-membership offer — first floated by Commission President von der Leyen in her State of the Union address — and calling Canada and Europe “stronger together” and “not fair-weather allies.” Without naming Trump, he criticized tariffs being “weaponized,” and proposed deeper integration on trade, defense, critical minerals, AI, energy, space, research and financial services. He said Canada’s Parliament will get “debates, plural,” and an eventual vote, neither yet scheduled.

The speech directly follows Thursday’s earlier report that Trump called the EU proposal a possible “hostile act” and threatened additional tariffs on Europe if it proceeds — an escalation this report first noted Wednesday.

A King Hosts an AI Safety Summit and a Tech CEO Calls for Nationalizing the Labs, the Same Week

Palantir’s Alex Karp wants leading AI developers nationalized and made legally liable; King Charles convened OpenAI, Anthropic, Nvidia and Google DeepMind executives at his Scottish estate to discuss “existential dangers.”

Palantir CEO Alex Karp told CNBC Thursday that leading AI labs may need to be nationalized, arguing developers should face civil and criminal liability, and suggesting some labs might actually welcome nationalization as a way to dodge lawsuits from clients whose proprietary data ends up in their training sets. The same day, King Charles III convened a closed-door AI safety summit at Dumfries House in Scotland — roughly 30 attendees including OpenAI, Anthropic, Nvidia and Google DeepMind executives, UK AI minister Kanishka Narayan and a Vatican AI adviser. Charles warned of “existential dangers” if AI fell “into the wrong hands”; no binding agreement resulted.

Both land in a week already shaped by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s “pace the frontier” call for slower capability gains — publicly backed by Sam Altman and Elon Musk — and Meta’s Zuckerberg breaking with it Tuesday, arguing safety should stay company-by-company rather than coordinated. Separately, none of the five China-based firms Anthropic has accused of mass-distilling Claude — Alibaba, Moonshot AI, DeepSeek, Xiaomi and Zhipu — has responded; some secondary outlets report the count as seven, a discrepancy this report has not resolved (Zhipu has rebranded as Z.ai, which may account for some of the difference). Anthropic also has not publicly responded to Microsoft AI chief Mustafa Suleyman’s critique of its Claude “constitution,” reported Wednesday.

Why it matters Within one week, a head of state, a major AI-infrastructure CEO and two of the industry’s biggest labs have all publicly signaled genuine anxiety about frontier AI’s trajectory, with no consensus on whether the answer is nationalization, self-regulation or a negotiated slowdown.

CNBC (Karp interview, primary) · Fortune · Yahoo · Decrypt · TheJournal.ie.

Two Actively Exploited Zero-Days Need Patching Today, One With a Same-Day Federal Deadline

A Chrome sandbox-escape bug is Google’s sixth zero-day of the year; Microsoft’s record-setting Patch Tuesday fixed two more flaws already being used in attacks.

Google’s Chrome V8 zero-day, CVE-2026-85046 — the browser’s sixth of 2026, a type-confusion bug that can let malicious HTML escape the sandbox — was patched September 3, and CISA’s federal civilian patch deadline for it falls today. Microsoft’s September Patch Tuesday fixed roughly 966 to 974 CVEs, the largest Patch Tuesday on record according to SecurityWeek, including two already-exploited zero-days now in CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog: CVE-2026-81963, the Windows Update Stack component’s first-ever exploited zero-day, and CVE-2026-85880, a Windows ALPC heap overflow.

Both land within 48 hours of the still-unresolved, maximum-severity Cisco ISE flaw (CVE-2026-76460, CVSS 10.0) already tracked in this report — discovered via an actual victim’s support ticket, with no available workaround, and a federal deadline that falls tomorrow, Saturday.

DRC’s Ebola Outbreak Passes 7,400 Cases, With a Split Regional Picture

Cases keep climbing overall even as the epicenter shows its first real signs of easing — a pattern WHO calls “encouraging” but “far from over.”

WHO and ECDC data through September 15 puts the outbreak at 7,404 confirmed cases and 3,577 deaths, up 59 cases and 32 deaths from the prior update. Ituri province, the epicenter, accounts for 5,751 cases and 2,622 deaths across 28 of its 36 health zones; North Kivu has 1,323 cases and 821 deaths across 16 of 34 zones, with WHO confirming weekly case counts there have roughly doubled, from about 100 to 200 a week — consistent with the acceleration this report flagged there previously. Roughly 930 patients are currently hospitalized in isolation, with 87.4% of known contacts under follow-up. It remains the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record, caused by Bundibugyo ebolavirus, a strain outside standard Zaire-strain vaccine coverage.
