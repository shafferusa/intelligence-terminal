The Line That Grew Three Times Faster Than Apple’s Business Did

Tuesday, September 8, 2026 · 27 min read

Apple’s net income rose almost three times as fast as its revenue last fiscal year, and finding exactly which of the income statement’s half-dozen subtractions produced that gap — while gross profit, operating income, and the account that finally connects back to the balance sheet each tell their own more honest story — is this lesson’s whole subject.

Accounting

Day 17 of 150 · Accounting 4 of 18 · Foundations

A profit that grew three times faster than the sales that produced it

Apple’s fiscal 2025 annual report, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission for the year ended September 27, 2025, contains two numbers that, side by side, look like they cannot both be describing the same company in the same twelve months. Revenue — everything customers paid Apple for iPhones, Macs, services, and everything else it sells — came in at $416.161 billion, up 6.4 percent from the $391.035 billion the company reported a year earlier. Net income, the number printed at the very bottom of the statement, the one headline writers reach for first, came in at $112.010 billion, up 19.5 percent from $93.736 billion. A company whose sales grew a little better than six percent somehow turned that into a bottom line that grew almost twenty — three times the rate, on the same underlying business, in the same twelve months.

That gap is exactly the kind of number a careful reader should refuse to wave past. Either Apple had an extraordinary year of operating leverage — selling meaningfully more efficiently, squeezing costs, some genuine structural improvement in how the actual business runs — or something else, unrelated to how many iPhones moved off shelves, is doing most of the work. The income statement is the one document built to answer that question honestly, because unlike a single number on a press release, it does not report revenue and net income and nothing in between; it reports every stop along the road from one to the other, and each stop is itself a real, meaningful number that answers a narrower, more specific question than “how did the company do.”

Run the same comparison one line above the bottom — operating income, the subtotal that measures only the business Apple actually runs, before taxes, before anything unrelated to selling products and services — and the picture changes completely. Operating income grew from $123.216 billion to $133.050 billion: up 8.0 percent, close to revenue’s 6.4 percent, the unremarkable shape of a large, mature company having a solid, ordinary year. The extraordinary number lives somewhere between that line and the bottom one.

Finding exactly where, in exact dollars, with a name and a date attached, is where this lesson is headed — but it takes the whole document to get there honestly. This lesson builds the income statement the same way lesson twelve built the balance sheet: the plain-English shape first, then the notation, then a small fictional business worked by hand, then Apple’s own real numbers, twice — once to see what a single year looks like, and once to see what changed between two years and why. By the end, the mystery in the paragraph above has an exact answer, in dollars, and this lesson has also kept a promise lesson twelve made and left open: showing, account by account, how this statement connects back to the balance sheet on either side of it.

A waterfall, not a single number

Lesson twelve of this track called the balance sheet a photograph: a statement of exactly what a company owned, owed, and had left over for its owners, frozen at one specific instant — the close of business on one particular day. It promised that today’s lesson would build the document that plays the part of the movie: the income statement, sometimes called the profit and loss statement or, in a company’s own filings, the statement of operations. Where a balance sheet cannot be asked about any stretch of time at all, an income statement is entirely about a stretch of time — a quarter, a fiscal year — and it exists to answer one question about that stretch: of everything that flowed into the business and everything that flowed back out, what was left over.

The plain-English shape of an income statement is a waterfall, and the reason accountants build it as a sequence of subtractions rather than a single net figure is that each subtraction along the way answers a specific, separately useful question. Start at the top with revenue: everything the business earned from customers during the period, for goods delivered or services performed. Subtract the direct cost of producing or delivering exactly what was sold — parts, manufacturing labor, shipping — and the remainder is gross profit: what is left once you have covered the literal cost of making the thing you sold, before a single other bill gets paid. Subtract everything else it costs to run the business — engineers who are not building any one specific unit sold this quarter, salespeople, executives, marketing, the corporate offices — and the remainder is operating income: what the core business, running exactly as designed, actually produced. Adjust for items that have nothing to do with running that core business — interest earned on cash in the bank, interest paid on debt, gains or losses on investments — and the remainder is pretax income. Subtract what the government takes, and what is left is net income: the number that belongs, in full, to the company’s owners.

One more piece of plain-English machinery matters before any notation appears, and it is easy to misread if nobody says it out loud: an income statement almost never reports cash moving in the door and cash moving out. It reports revenue when it is earned — typically when a product ships or a service is delivered to the customer, whether or not the customer has actually paid yet — and it reports expenses in the same period as the revenue they helped produce, whether or not the bill for them has actually been paid yet. A retailer that sells a laptop on store credit in September books that revenue in September, not whenever the customer’s payment clears; a company that pays an annual insurance premium in January spreads that cost evenly across the twelve months the coverage actually protects, rather than dumping the whole bill into January’s statement. Accountants call this accrual accounting, and the rule that tells a company exactly when a sale counts as earned — formally codified in the United States as Accounting Standards Codification Topic 606, adopted industry-wide in 2018 — is one of the more consequential pieces of plumbing in modern financial reporting, because a company that recognized revenue whenever it felt like it could make a mediocre quarter look like a great one. The practical consequence for a reader is simple and worth holding onto for the rest of this lesson: net income is not the same thing as cash in the bank, and a company can report a genuine, real profit in a period while its actual cash balance goes the other direction, or the reverse. That reconciliation belongs to a different statement entirely — the cash flow statement, day twenty-two of this track — and today’s lesson deliberately sets it aside to focus on what the income statement itself is built to show.

Five subtractions, five questions

Put the waterfall from above into notation. Every quantity below is measured over the same period — the same fiscal quarter or fiscal year — never at a single instant the way every quantity on a balance sheet was.

Rev − COGS = GP
GP − OpEx = OI
OI + NOI = PTI
PTI − Tax = NI
NI ÷ S = EPS

Rev
revenue — total amount earned from customers during the period, recognized under accrual accounting rather than when cash actually changes hands
COGS
cost of goods (and services) sold — the direct cost of producing or delivering exactly the goods and services sold during the period: parts, manufacturing labor, shipping, and comparable direct service-delivery costs; excludes costs that do not vary with what was actually sold
GP
gross profit — Rev minus COGS; what is left after covering only the direct cost of the goods and services actually sold
OpEx
operating expenses — the cost of running the business beyond direct production, most commonly split into research and development (R&D) and selling, general & administrative (SG&A)
OI
operating income — GP minus OpEx; what the company’s core, ordinary business produced during the period, before anything unrelated to running it
NOI
non-operating income and expense — items unrelated to the core business: net interest income or expense, investment gains or losses, and similar items; can be positive or negative, so it is added, not always subtracted
PTI
pretax income — OI plus NOI; income before the government takes its share
Tax
income tax expense — the period’s provision for income taxes, at the company’s effective tax rate, which is rarely identical to any single statutory rate
NI
net income — PTI minus Tax; what belongs, in full, to the company’s owners for the period; also called “the bottom line” or “earnings”
S
weighted-average diluted shares outstanding — the share count used to divide net income into a per-share figure, adjusted upward from the basic share count to include the effect of stock options, restricted stock, and similar instruments that could convert into shares
EPS
diluted earnings per share — net income allocated across each diluted share; the single most-quoted number derived from an income statement

Two things about this chain are worth flagging before working through actual numbers. First, every subtotal in the middle is a real, independently useful number, not just a scratchpad step on the way to net income — gross profit tells a reader about pricing power and production efficiency; operating income tells a reader about the core business, stripped of financing and tax decisions; net income tells a reader what actually, finally, belongs to the owners once every other claim on the business’s output has been settled. Second, unlike the balance sheet’s accounting equation from lesson twelve, which had to balance exactly by construction, nothing about this chain must come out to a tidy number — COGS could exceed revenue (gross profit negative), non-operating items could swing pretax income either direction, and a company can post a positive operating income and still report a net loss, or the reverse, if the items below operating income are large enough. The chain is a sequence of honest subtractions, not an identity that always resolves in the owners’ favor.

The specific shape of this waterfall is not simply a convention textbooks settled on independently; for any company that sells stock to the American public, it is close to a legal requirement. The SEC’s Regulation S-X, the rule set governing the form and content of financial statements in every 10-K and 10-Q, prescribes which major line items an income statement must separately disclose — revenue, cost of revenue, and taxes on income among them — which is why an income statement from Apple, a regional bank, and a small industrial manufacturer all share this same basic multi-step skeleton even though the businesses have almost nothing else in common. A company retains real discretion in how it labels and further subdivides its operating expenses — some break out marketing separately from general and administrative costs, others fold everything together — but it cannot simply report one number, “profit,” and stop there.

A deliberate choice hidden inside “operating expenses”

One line inside operating expenses deserves a closer look before moving to worked examples, because it is a genuine accounting policy decision, not just a category label, and it changes how a company’s own investment in its future shows up on this statement. Research and development spending — Apple’s $34.550 billion in fiscal 2025, roughly 8.3 percent of revenue — is, under U.S. GAAP (specifically the guidance in Accounting Standards Codification Topic 730), expensed immediately, in full, in the period the money is spent. It is not treated the way a factory, a delivery fleet, or an office building is treated: those get capitalized — recorded as an asset on the balance sheet and spread out, as depreciation, across the years the company expects to use them — on the theory that a factory keeps producing value for a decade, so its cost should be matched against the years of revenue it helps produce, not dumped entirely into the one year it was built.

R&D gets none of that treatment, on the theory that the future benefit of a research dollar — will this particular chip design or this line of experimental software actually ship a product, and if it does, for how many years will it sell — is too uncertain to justify treating it as a measurable, depreciable asset the way a building is. So the entire $34.550 billion Apple spent designing chips, software, and products that in many cases will not generate a dollar of revenue for another two or three years all lands on this one year’s income statement, dragging this year’s operating income down by the full amount, with none of it deferred to the future years when the resulting products actually sell.

The practical consequence is that immediate R&D expensing makes a heavily research-driven company’s current-year profitability look structurally worse, dollar for dollar, than an otherwise identical company that instead spent that same cash building a factory — even though both companies made a real investment aimed at future revenue, and even though from a cash perspective the two decisions cost exactly the same amount this year either way. It is one of the clearest examples in financial accounting of a rule that is defensible, consistently applied, genuinely conservative — and still capable of making two companies with economically similar decisions look meaningfully different on the page, for reasons that have nothing to do with which company is actually better run. A reader comparing operating margins across a software company, a pharmaceutical company, and a heavy-industrial manufacturer, without accounting for how differently each one’s biggest investment gets treated on this one line, is comparing three numbers that are not, in the way that matters, measuring the same thing.

There is a genuine exception, and it is worth knowing precisely because it shows the line is not arbitrary. Once a software project has passed a specific point called technological feasibility — roughly, the point at which a working model exists and management has committed to finishing and marketing it — U.S. GAAP (Accounting Standards Codification Topic 985-20, and its companion rule for software built for internal use, Topic 350-40) does allow some further software-development costs to be capitalized rather than expensed immediately, and then amortized over the product’s useful life, much like the factory in the paragraph above. In practice, most large technology companies, Apple among them, still expense the overwhelming majority of software development anyway, because in a modern development process built around continuous iteration and rolling releases, there is rarely a clean, identifiable moment separating “proving the idea works” from “building the shipping product” — and by the time a court could point to technological feasibility having been reached, there is very little remaining development cost left to capitalize. The exception exists on the books; it rarely moves the needle on a real technology company’s income statement, which is itself a small lesson in how an accounting rule’s theoretical scope and its practical effect can diverge.

Building one from a business you’ve already seen

Worked example 1 · the coffee cart’s first quarter

Lesson twelve built a balance sheet for a friend’s coffee cart business from seven account balances at the end of its first three months, and found $13,800 of equity at that instant — without ever learning how much of that was the owner’s original investment and how much was profit the cart had actually earned, because, as that lesson noted, nobody had kept a running count of the difference. The cart’s income statement for that same first quarter closes that gap. Suppose the records for the quarter show: revenue of $28,000 from drinks sold; $9,000 spent on the beans, milk, cups, and lids actually consumed making those drinks; $6,000 in wages to a part-time helper; $2,400 in rent for the spot the cart occupies; $900 in insurance and small supplies; and roughly $270 in interest on the $18,000 equipment loan, accrued over the quarter.

  1. Gross profit: Rev − COGS = $28,000 − $9,000 = $19,000. The $9,000 is only the beans, milk, cups and lids — the ingredients directly consumed making what was sold — not the helper’s wages or the rent, which do not vary directly with how many drinks were made.
  2. Operating expenses: $6,000 (wages) + $2,400 (rent) + $900 (insurance/supplies) = $9,300. Operating income: GP − OpEx = $19,000 − $9,300 = $9,700.
  3. Non-operating: the $270 of loan interest is unrelated to running the actual coffee business — it is the cost of how the equipment was financed, not the cost of making coffee — so it sits below operating income. Pretax income: OI + NOI = $9,700 − $270 = $9,430.
  4. Tax: the cart is an unincorporated sole proprietorship, not a separately taxed corporation, so there is no entity-level income tax line on its own income statement at all — the quarter’s profit passes straight through and is taxed on the owner’s personal return, a different document entirely. Net income for the quarter: $9,430, identical to pretax income, precisely because this particular business has no tax line to subtract.
  5. Now return to lesson twelve’s $13,800 of quarter-end equity. If $9,430 of it is this quarter’s net income, and the owner made no additional draws from the business during the quarter, the remainder — $13,800 − $9,430 = $4,370 — is what the owner must have originally put into the business, a figure the balance sheet alone could never separate out on its own. The balance sheet said what the cart was worth at one instant; the income statement, read alongside it, says how much of that was actually earned during the period versus contributed up front.

Reading Apple’s own income statement

Worked example 2 · Apple, fiscal year ended September 27, 2025

Apple’s own 10-K, filed with the SEC, reports the following for fiscal 2025 (dollars in millions): revenue $416,161; cost of sales $220,960; research and development expense $34,550; selling, general and administrative expense $27,601; non-operating expense, net $321; income tax expense $20,719. Build the full waterfall and compute the margin at every stop — each margin simply being that subtotal divided by revenue, expressed as a percentage.

  1. Gross profit: $416,161M − $220,960M = $195,201M. Gross margin: $195,201M ÷ $416,161M ≈ 46.9% — for every dollar of sales, about 47 cents is left after covering only the direct cost of the hardware and services sold.
  2. Operating expenses: R&D $34,550M + SG&A $27,601M = $62,151M. Operating income: $195,201M − $62,151M = $133,050M. Operating margin: $133,050M ÷ $416,161M ≈ 32.0%.
  3. Pretax income: $133,050M − $321M (net non-operating expense) = $132,729M. Apple’s enormous cash and investment portfolio earns real interest, but the company also carries tens of billions in bonds outstanding, and in fiscal 2025 the two roughly offset, leaving a small net expense rather than the net income a purely cash-rich, debt-free company would show here.
  4. Income tax expense of $20,719M brings net income to $132,729M − $20,719M = $112,010M. Net margin: $112,010M ÷ $416,161M ≈ 26.9%. Effective tax rate: $20,719M ÷ $132,729M ≈ 15.6%.
  5. Per share: Apple reports diluted earnings per share of $7.46 on a weighted-average diluted share count of roughly 15.005 billion shares for the year — net income divided across every share, including the dilutive effect of employee stock awards, that could plausibly claim a piece of it.

Every margin computed above tells a different, specific story from the others: a 46.9% gross margin says Apple keeps roughly 47 cents of every sales dollar after covering only what it costs to make and deliver the product; a 32.0% operating margin says running the whole company — engineering, sales, administration, everything — costs another 15 points, leaving 32 cents of pure operating profit; a 26.9% net margin says taxes and financing costs take a further five points, leaving 27 cents that actually belongs to shareholders. None of those four numbers is “the” profit margin; each answers a narrower question than the one before it.

Where it shows up: the line that actually moved

Worked example 3 · why net income outran the business

Return to the puzzle this lesson opened with: net income up 19.5% against revenue up only 6.4% and operating income up 8.0%. Lay fiscal 2025 next to fiscal 2024, line by line (dollars in millions): revenue $416,161 vs. $391,035; operating income $133,050 vs. $123,216; pretax income $132,729 vs. $123,485; income tax expense $20,719 vs. $29,749; net income $112,010 vs. $93,736.

  1. Revenue growth: ($416,161 − $391,035) ÷ $391,035 ≈ 6.4%. Operating income growth: ($133,050 − $123,216) ÷ $123,216 ≈ 8.0%. Pretax income growth: ($132,729 − $123,485) ÷ $123,485 ≈ 7.5%. Every line down through pretax income moved in a tight, unremarkable band — a company growing steadily, not exploding.
  2. Then the tax line: fiscal 2024’s income tax expense of $29,749M against pretax income of $123,485M is an effective rate of $29,749M ÷ $123,485M ≈ 24.1% — unusually high for Apple. Fiscal 2025’s $20,719M against $132,729M is 15.6%, back to something close to Apple’s ordinary range. A tax rate that fell by roughly eight and a half percentage points, on a base of well over $100 billion in pretax income, is exactly the kind of thing that can make net income growth wildly outrun everything above it.
  3. The reason for fiscal 2024’s unusually high rate is a matter of public record, not speculation: on September 10, 2024, the Court of Justice of the European Union reinstated the European Commission’s 2016 finding that Ireland had granted Apple unlawful state aid through two tax rulings issued in 1991 and 2007, and confirmed that Apple owed Ireland roughly €13 billion in back taxes covering 2003 through 2014 — money Apple had already been holding in an escrow account for years while the case worked through Europe’s courts. Apple’s own fiscal 2024 10-K reports the consequence directly: a one-time net income tax charge of $10.2 billion in its fiscal fourth quarter, made up of $15.8 billion payable to Ireland via release of the escrowed funds, partly offset by a $4.8 billion U.S. foreign tax credit and an $823 million decrease in unrecognized tax benefits.
  4. Strip that one-time charge back out of fiscal 2024’s tax expense to see what the year would have looked like without it: $29,749M − $10,200M = $19,549M, against the same $123,485M of pretax income, an effective rate of $19,549M ÷ $123,485M ≈ 15.8% — almost exactly fiscal 2025’s 15.6%. Once the decade-old European tax dispute is set aside, Apple’s effective tax rate barely moved between the two years at all.

One more timing wrinkle is worth naming, because it is a clean real-world instance of the accrual-versus-cash gap the plain-English section raised. The escrowed funds behind this charge had been set aside years earlier, following the European Commission’s original 2016 order, precisely so the money would already be waiting whenever the litigation finally concluded. In cash terms, that money had substantially left Apple’s own control long before fiscal 2024; the $10.2 billion income-statement charge is the accounting recognition of a decade-old dispute reaching its conclusion, not a fiscal-2024 cash outflow in the ordinary sense. A reader who wants to know exactly what happened to Apple’s actual cash in fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2025 has to read the cash flow statement, day twenty-two of this track, not the income statement alone — this one tax charge would look different on each of the three financial statements, for reasons this lesson has now built the vocabulary to state precisely.

That is the whole answer to the puzzle this lesson opened with. Apple’s actual business — the part measured by revenue and operating income — grew at an ordinary, single-digit pace in fiscal 2025, in line with the year before. Net income grew nearly three times faster almost entirely because fiscal 2024’s net income had been artificially depressed by a one-time, decades-old tax dispute finally reaching its final bill, not because fiscal 2025’s underlying operations meaningfully accelerated. A reader who only ever looks at the bottom line would have concluded something dramatic happened to Apple’s business in fiscal 2025. A reader who reads the whole waterfall finds the real story three lines higher — and it happened a year earlier, in a courtroom in Luxembourg, not in a product lineup.

The general check hiding inside this specific example is worth keeping: whenever a market report says a company’s profit rose or fell by some percentage, compare that figure to how much revenue moved, and, if it is available, how much operating income moved. When the three move together, the story is genuinely about the business. When net income diverges sharply from the two lines above it, the real story usually lives in whichever specific item — a tax charge, a legal settlement, a currency swing, a one-time gain — created the gap, and that item is almost always disclosed by name somewhere in the same filing.

The mistake almost everyone makes

The single most common misreading of an income statement is treating net income — “the bottom line” — as if it were the one clean verdict on how a business performed during a period, when it is actually the subtotal most exposed to items that have nothing to do with how the underlying business performed. Worked example 3 is not an unusual case; it is the ordinary case. Currency swings, gains or losses on selling an investment, a legal settlement, a restructuring charge, a change in a deferred tax valuation, a one-time tax dispute three decades in the making — all of it lives below operating income, and any single one of these items, in a large enough company, can swing net income by double-digit percentages in a single year without a single customer buying a single additional product.

This is precisely why professional equity analysts, credit-rating agencies, and companies themselves routinely emphasize operating income, and further-adjusted variants of it, ahead of net income when describing how a business actually performed — not because net income is fake or unimportant (it is real, it is audited, and as the next section shows, it is the exact number that ultimately lands in the balance sheet’s equity account), but because operating income is deliberately built to exclude the kind of noise that made fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2025 look so different at the bottom of the page and so similar three lines higher. A headline that reports “profit up 19 percent” without saying which subtotal moved, and why, can be completely accurate on the arithmetic and still leave a reader with exactly the wrong impression of what happened inside the business that year.

The corrective is not to distrust net income, or to decide operating income is the “real” number and net income the fake one — both figures are real, both are correctly calculated, and both answer genuine questions. The corrective is the same one lesson twelve reached for the balance sheet: read each subtotal for the specific, narrower question it was built to answer, rather than skipping straight to whichever number a headline chose to quote.

Where the $112 billion actually goes

Lesson twelve ended by promising that today’s lesson would show, account by account, how the income statement connects back to the balance sheet on either side of it. That connection is not a metaphor — it is a specific, traceable line item: net income flows directly into a balance-sheet equity account called retained earnings, the running total of every dollar of profit a company has ever kept rather than paid out to shareholders, since the day it was founded.

Worked example 4 · the retained-earnings bridge

Apple reported retained earnings of −$19.154 billion at the end of fiscal 2024 and −$14.264 billion at the end of fiscal 2025 — both negative, a point worth pausing on before the arithmetic, because a negative retained-earnings balance sounds alarming and, for Apple specifically, is not: lesson twelve’s worked example 3 already showed why, tracing how years of returning more cash to shareholders through buybacks and dividends than the company earned in profit pushed total equity down even in profitable years. Retained earnings going negative is the same phenomenon, isolated to one specific equity account.

  1. Start from beginning retained earnings, fiscal 2024’s ending balance: −$19.154B.
  2. Add fiscal 2025’s net income, the exact figure worked example 2 built line by line: +$112.010B. Subtotal: −$19.154B + $112.010B = $92.856B.
  3. Subtract dividends paid during the year, $15.421B, which reduce retained earnings directly — a distribution of already-earned profit to shareholders, never an expense on the income statement itself. That is a real distinction worth holding next to interest, from worked example 2’s non-operating line: interest paid to a bondholder is the cost of borrowed money and reduces net income on the way down; a dividend paid to a shareholder is a distribution of profit already counted, and reduces equity directly, bypassing the income statement entirely. Subtotal: $92.856B − $15.421B = $77.435B.
  4. Apple’s actual reported ending retained earnings for fiscal 2025 was −$14.264B — $91.699B lower than the $77.435B step 3 produced. Apple spent $90.711B in cash on share repurchases during fiscal 2025, and because Apple retires the shares it buys back immediately rather than holding them in a treasury account, the cost of those repurchases is charged against retained earnings once the separate common-stock account is exhausted — accounting for essentially all of the remaining gap, with the roughly $1 billion of residual difference explained by ordinary timing: repurchases executed but not yet cash-settled at fiscal year-end, and routine share-based tax-withholding activity.
  5. Cross-check the whole bridge against Apple’s total stockholders’ equity, which lesson twelve’s worked example 2 already established at $73.733B for fiscal 2025, up from $56.950B a year earlier — a change of $16.783B. Retained earnings itself moved by −$14.264B − (−$19.154B) = +$4.890B over the year. The common-stock-and-paid-in-capital account, which rose mainly from new shares issued through employee compensation programs, moved from $83.276B to $93.568B, +$10.292B. A third, smaller account, accumulated other comprehensive income — which tracks unrealized swings in the value of Apple’s investment portfolio and hedges — moved from −$7.172B to −$5.571B, +$1.601B. Add all three: $4.890B + $10.292B + $1.601B = $16.783B, matching the actual change in total equity to the dollar. The account-by-account bridge closes completely.

Net income, the number worked example 2 spent five steps building up from $416.161 billion of revenue, is not an abstraction that evaporates once the income statement is finished. It is a specific dollar figure that lands, the same fiscal year, inside a specific line of the balance sheet — exactly the account-by-account connection lesson twelve left open.

The callback

Day fourteen of this curriculum’s Economics track built two curves — supply and demand — found the price and quantity where they cross, and spent its second half on a single sharp result, proved with the product rule: revenue, defined as price multiplied by quantity, is not maximized by charging the highest price a market will bear. It is maximized exactly at the point where demand’s elasticity equals one — the unit-elastic point — because past that point, every further price increase loses more in quantity sold than it gains in price per unit, and total revenue actually starts falling even as the price on the sticker keeps rising.

That result is not a separate idea from today’s lesson wearing different clothes — it is the literal definition of the number sitting at the very top of everything built above. Apple’s $416.161 billion of fiscal 2025 revenue is, product by product and unit by unit, the sum of thousands of individual instances of price multiplied by quantity: an iPhone sold at whatever price Apple set and however many units the market absorbed at that price, a subscription sold at its price and however many customers signed up, repeated across every product and every region Apple sells in. Accounting does not create that number or decide what it should be; it only measures and reports whatever price and quantity the market — supply meeting demand, again and again, product by product — already produced. Every subtraction this lesson built on top of that figure, all the way down to the $112.010 billion of net income and the $91.699 billion that left retained earnings in worked example 4, starts from a number economics determined first and accounting only recorded.

It also explains something worth sitting with about Apple’s pricing choices specifically: day fourteen’s unit-elasticity result is exactly why a company facing real competition — and Apple, in every product category it sells, does — cannot simply raise prices indefinitely to grow this lesson’s top line. Past some point, for a product with real substitutes, higher prices cost more in lost buyers than they gain in revenue per buyer, and the $416.161 billion at the top of this whole waterfall would itself start shrinking. Every subtotal below it — gross profit, operating income, net income, the dollar that eventually reaches retained earnings — inherits whatever happens to that first number, which is exactly why the two tracks, economics and accounting, are not really two separate subjects when it comes to a company’s income statement. One decides what the top line is; the other is simply the honest, careful bookkeeping of everything that happens to it on the way down the page.

Where this leaves you
  • You can build the full income statement waterfall — revenue, cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, operating income, non-operating items, pretax income, tax, net income, and earnings per share — and state, in one sentence each, the specific question every subtotal answers.
  • You can build one from raw numbers, as worked example 1 did for the coffee cart, and use it together with a balance sheet, as lesson twelve’s did, to recover information neither statement reveals alone.
  • You can read a real company’s income statement, as worked examples 2 and 3 did with Apple’s own fiscal 2025 and fiscal 2024 10-Ks, compute real margins at every stop, and explain — with sourced, dollar-for-dollar figures — why net income can grow at three times the rate of the business that produced it, for reasons that trace to a single line and a specific event, not to anything mysterious.
  • You know net income is not the whole verdict on a period, and you know exactly how it connects, account by account, to the balance sheet’s retained-earnings line — the specific promise lesson twelve made and this lesson has now kept.
  • You know where this is going: net income is not cash, a point this lesson raised and deliberately left open. Day twenty-two of this track builds the third statement, cash flow — the document that finally shows where the cash itself goes, and how a company can report a real profit while its bank balance moves the other way entirely.
