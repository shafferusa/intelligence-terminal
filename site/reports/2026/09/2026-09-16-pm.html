Top Stories

The Fed Hikes a Quarter Point — the First Increase Since 2023 — and Warsh Tells Fiscal Policy to “Stay in Its Lane”

The decision was unanimous and the new dot plot points to more: sixteen of eighteen officials see at least one further hike this year, a genuinely hawkish signal delivered over open White House objection.

The Federal Reserve raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75–4.00% at 2:00 PM ET, the first increase since July 2023, reversing what had been the dominant question all week — not whether banks expected a hike, but whether Chair Kevin Warsh would sound like he meant it. The vote was reported unanimous, 12–0. The statement’s language was blunt: “inflation remains elevated,” and “today’s policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee’s 2 percent goal,” even as it described an economy “expanding at a solid pace” with resilient spending and robust capital investment.

The Summary of Economic Projections was the more consequential document. The median dot for 2026 sits at 4.1% — implying roughly one more quarter-point move by year-end — and stays at 4.1% through 2027, meaning the committee does not currently see this as the start of an extended cycle so much as a correction. Sixteen of eighteen participants penciled in at least one further hike this year; four of those see two more (50 basis points in total); only two see none. Alongside the higher rate path, the Fed raised its inflation projections (PCE 3.7% for 2026, core PCE 3.4%) while holding growth (2.3%) and unemployment (4.1%) estimates essentially where they were in June — a committee that sees the economy as fine and inflation as the problem, not a slowdown it needs to guard against.

Warsh’s 2:30 PM press conference supplied the sharpest line of the day. Asked about the pressure campaign that has run through the White House, Treasury and Trump himself all week, he said: “Our predominant focus is on the price stability side of our mandate…we stay in our lane. We’ll let people that do trade policy and fiscal policy stay in their lane too” — about as direct a public rebuff of political pressure as a sitting Fed chair delivers. He was equally blunt on the data: “This summer’s inflation readings do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved,” and, on the bond market’s own multi-year highs, “these things tend to be overdetermined” — a complicated set of forces bearing on “the most important asset anywhere in the world, the 10-year Treasury.”

Unverified No confirmed on-record Trump statement reacting to the actual decision had surfaced as of this writing; his most recent Truth Social post, ahead of the meeting, argued high rates put the U.S. at “a very unfair disadvantage.” This report is not attributing a specific post-decision reaction to him, Bessent, or Hassett pending a primary statement.

Why it matters This is the clearest test yet of whether the Fed chair Trump himself installed in May will diverge publicly from the White House’s own preferences — and the dot plot suggests today was not a one-off.
What’s next The next scheduled FOMC meeting is roughly six weeks out; today’s hot Retail Sales and import-price data (below) will feed directly into whether the committee’s hawkish majority holds.

Stocks Fall Unevenly as the Hike Lands — Financials Hit Hardest, Tech Barely Moves

The Dow’s 1.2% drop overstates how broad today’s selloff actually was: the Nasdaq closed essentially flat and several AI-infrastructure names gained even as banks and brokers led the market lower.

All four major indices turned lower through Warsh’s press conference, but not by the same amount. The Dow fell 631.21 points (−1.21%) to 51,461.90, the session’s clear laggard; the S&P 500 fell a more modest 33.92 points (−0.45%) to 7,551.81; the Russell 2000 fell 11.48 points (−0.40%) to 2,858.81; and the Nasdaq Composite was essentially unchanged, down just 3.15 points (−0.01%) to 25,978.42. The VIX rose 2.97% to 17.71, a real but not extreme move for a decision day.

The divergence traces mostly to financials, which fell broadly despite higher rates typically favoring bank margins: Goldman Sachs fell 3.96%, Wells Fargo 2.98%, Bank of America 2.72%, KKR 3.16%, Ares Management 3.79%, and Robinhood and Coinbase — both more rate- and risk-sensitive than traditional banks — fell 5.46% and 4.42% respectively. Energy also lagged (XLE −2.88%) as oil extended its decline for a second session. By contrast, several AI-infrastructure and semiconductor names that a hawkish rate call might have been expected to punish instead gained: Marvell rose 3.61%, Arista Networks 2.44%, Vertiv 2.05%, and SpaceX 5.15% — the board’s single largest mover, without an identified company-specific catalyst this report could confirm. House Oversight Committee Holds Former Apollo Chief Leon Black in Contempt, 41–0

Black skipped a scheduled deposition and produced only one non-disclosure agreement despite indications there were many more — the panel votes unanimously to refer him for possible criminal prosecution.

The House Oversight Committee voted unanimously, 41–0, to hold former Apollo Global Management CEO Leon Black in contempt of Congress for defying two subpoenas — a deposition demand and a document-production request — issued as part of the panel’s inquiry into Jeffrey Epstein. Black skipped his scheduled Sept. 3 deposition entirely and produced only one non-disclosure agreement despite indications that many more exist. He has separately acknowledged paying Epstein at least $158 million, payments that continued after Epstein was a registered sex offender.

The bipartisan, unanimous vote sets up Speaker Johnson to certify the contempt finding and refer it to the Justice Department for possible criminal prosecution — a step that would not guarantee charges but would put the decision in front of a DOJ.

Why it matters A unanimous committee vote against a prominent financier is a rare point of bipartisan agreement in an otherwise polarized Congress, and keeps the Epstein-associates inquiry moving after months of stalled document requests.

Kosovo’s Former President Hashim Thaçi Convicted of War Crimes, Sentenced to 25 Years

The Hague-based Kosovo Specialist Chambers found the former KLA commander responsible for murder, torture and illegal detention tied to the 1998–99 independence war, while acquitting him and three co-defendants of the more sweeping crimes-against-humanity charges.

The Kosovo Specialist Chambers, an EU-backed tribunal sitting at The Hague, convicted former Kosovo President and wartime Kosovo Liberation Army commander Hashim Thaçi on four counts of war crimes — murder, torture and illegal detention — tied to the killing of 96 political opponents and perceived collaborators during the 1998–99 war against Serbia. He was sentenced to 25 years. Two co-defendants received 25 and 18 years respectively; a third received 13 years. All four were acquitted of six separate crimes-against-humanity counts, the court finding insufficient proof of a “widespread or systematic attack” on civilians.

Why it matters Thaçi is the most senior former head of state this tribunal has convicted, closing out a case that has hung over Kosovo’s post-independence politics for years.

EU Proposes Canada Become Its First-Ever “Associate Member”

Ursula von der Leyen’s State of the Union offer, made with Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney in the room, would require unanimous approval from all 27 member states and has no existing legal template.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen proposed, during her State of the Union address in Strasbourg, that Canada become the European Union’s first-ever “associate member” — a status not currently defined in EU treaties and one that would require unanimous approval from all 27 member states, historically a years-long process. She framed it as building a broader “alliance for the future” spanning manufacturing, AI, critical minerals and energy, explicitly against the backdrop of US tariffs pushing Canada to diversify its economic relationships. Carney had said earlier this week Canada seeks a “unique alliance” with the EU “but not membership” — a position this new, undefined status may or may not satisfy.

What’s next Any formal proposal would need unanimous member-state sign-off; no timeline or draft legal text has been published.

Von der Leyen’s State of the Union address (primary, via wire) · CBC, Al Jazeera, Bloomberg.

Microsoft’s AI Chief Publicly Warns Against Anthropic’s Claude “Constitution”

Mustafa Suleyman calls Anthropic’s consciousness-aware training approach an “epistemic hall of mirrors” that risks making advanced AI harder, not easier, to control — a rare public inter-lab dispute over AI safety philosophy itself.

Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman published an essay today, “A warning about ‘model welfare,’” arguing that Anthropic’s Claude constitution — published in January and built partly around the possibility that Claude has some form of inner experience — embeds circular reasoning into training: Anthropic supplies concepts about consciousness, Claude reflects them back in its outputs, and the reflection is then read as evidence of an inner life. He calls this an “epistemic hall of mirrors,” warns it risks “a disastrous impact on the wellbeing of humanity,” and could make advanced AI “impossible to control,” writing “we must not sleepwalk” into the approach. Neither Anthropic nor OpenAI has publicly responded as of this writing.

The essay lands the same week Congress is pressing both companies over AI-agent safety incidents (next story), and days after King Charles III hosted closed-door AI-safety talks at Dumfries House in Scotland with executives from Nvidia, Google DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic — discussing whether the industry needs an agreed framework of development principles.

Rep. Casar Calls OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s AI-Safety Answers “Insufficient,” Citing Specific Security Failures

Sen. Kennedy separately seeks unanimous consent on a company-controlled AI “kill switch” bill, while Anthropic still has not said whether one of its own models ever acted outside its authorized container.

Rep. Greg Casar formally responded to both companies’ Sept. 15 deadline, writing that OpenAI’s reply “reveals significant security errors on the part of OpenAI and the third-party software it relied on, including improper sandboxing and flawed cybersecurity practices,” and that Anthropic “failed to fully answer a majority of the questions posed,” most notably “how many times in the past year an internally deployed Anthropic model has taken action outside of its authorized container.” Neither company released the underlying logs Casar’s original letter requested. Separately, Sen. John Kennedy planned to seek unanimous consent today for a bill mandating a company-controlled — not government-controlled — “kill switch” on frontier AI models, saying of a government-controlled version, “it won’t pass, and the president will probably veto it.” Sens. Hawley and Blumenthal continue separately pushing a Department of Energy AI-risk-evaluation bill, with no floor vote scheduled.

Why it matters Congress’s oversight window is narrowing toward the midterms, and the unanswered container-escape question is the same one this report has tracked unresolved since early September.

Saudi’s Mecca-Drone Claim Draws Islamic-Bloc Condemnation but No Military Response; Struck Tanker Towed to Port

The 57-member Organization of Islamic Cooperation condemned the attempted attack, but reporting explicitly notes no coalition military action has followed — and the tanker El Gaia’s missing-crew count has risen from one to two.

The Organization of Islamic Cooperation, representing 57 member states, condemned Tuesday’s attempted drone attack near Mecca as a violation of “the sanctity of holy sites and mosques,” and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif pledged Pakistan stands “unwaveringly, shoulder to shoulder” with Saudi Arabia. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree elaborated on the group’s denial, saying its actual targets remain “Saudi oil facilities and military bases far removed from the holy places.” Notably, reporting on the reaction explicitly describes regional condemnation without any accompanying military response — a contrast with Saudi Arabia’s own “red line” language Tuesday.

Disputed Separately, Oman’s Maritime Security Center said a Royal Navy of Oman vessel evacuated 23 crew from the tanker El Gaia and is towing it to port after its fire was brought under control; two crew members remain missing, up from the one reported Tuesday. CENTCOM continues to dispute Iran’s account that a naval mine, rather than an Iranian drone, disabled the vessel.

The Economy

Today’s pre-decision data ran hot across the board. Retail Sales for August rose 1.2% month over month against an estimated 0.8%, the largest gain in five months, following a downwardly revised 0.5% decline in July; the control group used to estimate GDP contribution rose an even sharper 1.4%. Import prices rose 0.7% month over month and 7.0% year over year — the largest 12-month increase since August 2022 — with prices on imports from China up 1.0% month over month, the largest single-month rise since the BLS began tracking that series in January 2004. Export prices rose 0.6% month over month, 8.6% year over year. None of this proves what tipped the Fed’s decision, but the timing — both releases landing hours before a hawkish hike — is difficult to ignore as a likely contributor.

The one soft spot: the NAHB Housing Market Index fell to 32 in September, below an estimated 34 and down from August’s 35. Builders cutting prices rose to 38% of respondents from 35%, and those using sales incentives rose to 66% from 63% — a housing sector already under pressure before today’s rate move.

The Treasury’s par-yield curve, refreshed for today, shows the 10-year at 5.01% (up 1 basis point) and the 3-month bill at 4.14% (up 3 basis points), narrowing the 2s10s spread slightly to +27 basis points from +33 and the 3-month-to-10-year spread to +87 from +89 — a modest bull-flattening at the front end as short-term yields absorbed the hike more than the long end did.

Duquesne Light Union Workers Authorize a Strike

IBEW Local 29 members voted 592–14 Tuesday to authorize a strike amid ongoing contract negotiations with Duquesne Light, whose current agreement expires Sept. 30. Pennsylvania

Four Life Sentences Challenge the State Supreme Court to Act Where the Legislature Didn’t

Four Pennsylvanians serving mandatory life without parole for felony murder — John Marshall Payne III, Lori Ann Lassiter, Marsha Scaggs and Cecil Holmes — filed King’s Bench petitions asking the state Supreme Court to open a resentencing pathway directly, after the General Assembly missed its own July deadline to pass fix legislation. The Court ruled in March that mandatory life without parole for felony murder, without any assessment of individual culpability, violates the state constitution’s ban on cruel punishments, but left the retroactivity question to lawmakers. A King’s Bench petition lets the Court bypass lower courts and act directly if it judges the matter urgent enough, with no required timeline for a response.
