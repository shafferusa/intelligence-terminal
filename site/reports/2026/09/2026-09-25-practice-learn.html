The Number That Broke a Cult

A 2,500-year-old Greek brotherhood believed the whole universe could be measured out in whole numbers and their ratios. Then one of their own found a length that couldn’t be, and mathematics got its most important tool: proof.

Day 1 of 300 · Mathematics 1 of 20 · Numbers, infinity, and the birth of proof

The Big Idea

Numbers didn’t arrive all at once. They grew, in stages, each stage built to solve a problem the one before it couldn’t handle. Counting things gives you the natural numbers: one sheep, two sheep, ten sheep. Comparing quantities as ratios — half a loaf, three parts flour to one part water — gives you fractions. For most of human history, most people assumed that was the whole story: every quantity that could exist was either a whole number or a ratio of two whole numbers.

It isn’t. There are lengths — the diagonal of an ordinary square is one of them — that cannot be written as any ratio of whole numbers, no matter how large you let the numbers get. Not “nobody has found the right fraction yet.” Not “the fraction is just very complicated.” It has been proven, with total certainty, that no such fraction exists anywhere, ever. Numbers like that are called irrational, and discovering the first one broke the worldview of the people who found it so badly that, as the story is told, they tried to keep it secret.

That story is where this lesson starts, because it is also the story of where mathematical proof comes from — the method that lets a mathematician say a claim is true forever, not just true in every case checked so far. That single idea is the spine every later lesson in this subject rests on.

How It Works

Start with the number system built in stages, because the stages matter.

The natural numbers — 1, 2, 3, and onward — are what you get from counting. Every culture that has ever counted anything has them. They are old enough that no one person invented them.

Extend them with zero and with negative numbers and you get the integers: a way to represent “none” and “owed,” not just “some.” This step came later than you might expect, and not from the Greeks. Indian mathematicians were working with negative numbers as debts by the 7th century CE; European mathematicians were still calling them “absurd” more than a thousand years after that. A useful idea does not always spread on a tidy schedule.

Extend again with fractions — any integer divided by any nonzero integer — and you get the rational numbers: 1/2, 3/4, 22/7, and every integer itself (since 5 is just 5/1). Rational numbers are enough to measure almost anything you’d ever need to measure by hand, and ancient Egyptian, Babylonian, Chinese and Greek mathematics all used them fluently, thousands of years before anyone wrote the word “rational.”

That was where the Pythagoreans — a religious and mathematical brotherhood founded around the philosopher Pythagoras in the Greek colonies of southern Italy in the 6th century BCE — believed the story ended. Their central doctrine, roughly, was “all is number”: that every relationship in the universe, from musical harmony to the shape of a triangle, was secretly a ratio of whole numbers. It wasn’t a casual guess. They had genuine evidence for it — they had discovered, for instance, that two plucked strings sound harmonious together exactly when the ratio of their lengths is a simple one, like 2:1 or 3:2. The universe really did seem to run on whole-number ratios.

Then someone in the school — later tradition names a member called Hippasus of Metapontum, though the details of the story are semi-legendary and historians are not fully confident of them — looked at the simplest shape imaginable: a square with sides of length 1. Its diagonal, by the Pythagorean theorem itself (a theorem this same school is named for), has length equal to the square root of 2. The question was innocent enough: what fraction is that?

The answer, it turns out, is that no fraction is that. Not a fraction anyone had missed — no fraction, period, could ever equal it, and this can be shown with total certainty using nothing but logic (the full argument is in the Equation / Model section below). A length existed, sitting inside the simplest shape in geometry, that their entire worldview said could not exist. Later legend holds that the discovery was suppressed, and that Hippasus was drowned at sea for revealing it — a dramatic story that most historians treat as embellishment rather than verified fact, but the underlying mathematical shock was real regardless of how the Pythagorean brotherhood actually reacted to it.

The fix took a while, and it wasn’t quite the one you might expect. The Greeks did not respond by inventing “irrational numbers” in anything like the modern sense — that would wait roughly two thousand years, until 19th-century mathematicians like Richard Dedekind gave the real numbers a fully rigorous definition, a story for a much later lesson once this subject reaches limits. Their actual solution, developed about a century after the crisis by the mathematician Eudoxus of Cnidus and preserved in Book V of Euclid’s Elements, was a theory of proportion for geometric magnitudes: instead of insisting every length correspond to a number, Eudoxus showed how to compare any two lengths — whether or not they shared a common measure — by comparing how they behaved under multiplication, sidestepping the need to name the diagonal’s length as a number at all. It was a genuinely elegant patch, and it held Greek geometry together for roughly two thousand years.

Only much later did mathematicians fold these magnitudes fully into an enlarged number system. Numbers that are not ratios of integers — like the square root of 2, or the more famous constant pi — are called irrational numbers. Put the rationals and the irrationals together and you get the real numbers: every point on the number line, with no gaps left anywhere. That is the number system essentially all of mathematics, science and engineering runs on today.

The second thing this story hands you is more important than the number itself: mathematical proof. A proof is a chain of logical steps, starting from statements everyone already accepts, that forces a conclusion to be true with no possible exception — not “true in every case I’ve checked,” which is how most of science and everyday reasoning works, but true in every case there could ever be, checked or not. That distinction is why the Pythagoreans couldn’t just dismiss the diagonal of a square as an unlucky measurement error. A proof doesn’t care how carefully you measure. It doesn’t admit exceptions later. Every equation you’ll meet in this subject over the next nineteen lessons sits on top of proofs built exactly this way.

Key Concepts

Natural numbers
The counting numbers: 1, 2, 3, and onward. The oldest and most universal part of the number system.
Integers
The natural numbers extended with zero and the negative numbers, so quantities like “owed” and “below zero” can be represented.
Rational numbers
Any number that can be written as one integer divided by another nonzero integer — a ratio, or fraction, such as 3/4 or −7/2.
Irrational numbers
Real numbers that cannot be written as any ratio of two integers, however large — the square root of 2 and pi are the two most famous examples.
Real numbers
The rationals and irrationals together: every point on the number line, with no gaps.
Mathematical proof
A chain of logical steps from accepted starting points that establishes a claim as true in every possible case, permanently — not merely true in every case checked so far.

Example / Application

Here is a place the irrationality of the square root of 2 is sitting quietly in your house right now: the shape of a standard sheet of printer paper.

The international “A-series” paper sizes — A4 is the everyday letter-sized sheet used almost everywhere outside North America, A3 the next size up, and so on — are all built to the same aspect ratio on purpose: the long side is the short side times the square root of 2, to roughly 1.41421 to 1. That specific ratio has one property no other ratio has: fold an A-series sheet exactly in half along its long side, and the two halves you get are each a smaller sheet with the exact same aspect ratio as the one you started with — A4 folds into two A5 sheets, A3 into two A4 sheets, and so on, forever, each one a perfect scaled-down copy of the last.

That self-repeating property is a direct consequence of the square root of 2 being what it is: if a rectangle has sides in ratio x to 1, folding it in half along the long side produces a new rectangle with sides in ratio 1 to x/2 — and those two ratios are equal only when x squared equals 2. Paper manufacturers in the early 20th century picked that ratio precisely so that a single master sheet could be halved, again and again, into every standard size without ever changing shape or wasting a scrap of paper to trimming. It is a genuinely irrational number, doing genuinely useful work, on a shelf in an office-supply store.

There is also a second, more hands-on way to see the same fact — one you can check yourself with nothing but long division. Every rational number’s decimal expansion either stops outright (1/4 = 0.25) or eventually falls into a block that repeats forever (1/3 = 0.333…, 1/7 = 0.142857142857…). The square root of 2, by contrast, begins 1.41421356237… and never stops and never repeats, however many digits you compute — it has been calculated to trillions of digits by people looking for a pattern, and there isn’t one, because the proof below guarantees there cannot be. That gives a second, equivalent way to define “irrational”: a decimal that runs forever without ever settling into a repeating pattern.

Equation / Model

Here is the actual argument — the one that turned “we can’t find the right fraction” into “no fraction can ever exist,” and the oldest surviving example of a proof by contradiction: assume the opposite of what you want to show, follow the logic wherever it leads, and if it leads somewhere impossible, the original assumption must have been false.

Assume, for the sake of argument, that the square root of 2 is rational — that it can be written as a fraction a/b, where a and b are whole numbers with no common factor (if they had one, you’d have already simplified it away, the way 4/6 simplifies to 2/3).

a² = 2b²

a squared equals two times b squared.

a
a whole number, the numerator of the assumed fraction a/b equal to the square root of 2
b
a whole number, the denominator of that fraction, sharing no common factor with a

That line comes from squaring both sides of “the square root of 2 equals a/b” and clearing the fraction. Now follow where it leads. The right side, 2b², is 2 times a whole number, so it’s even — which means a² is even too. And a whole number squared is only even if the number itself is even (an odd number times itself always produces an odd number), so a itself must be even. Write a as 2k for some whole number k, and substitute it back in: (2k)² = 2b² works out to 4k² = 2b², which simplifies to b² = 2k². But that is exactly the same shape of statement as before — which means, by the identical argument, b must be even too.

And there is the contradiction: the proof just showed that both a and b are even, sharing a common factor of 2 — directly contradicting the starting assumption that a and b shared no common factor. Something in the chain of reasoning has to be false, and every step after the assumption followed by ordinary logic from the one before it. The only thing left that could be false is the assumption itself: the square root of 2 cannot be written as a/b for any whole numbers a and b. It is irrational. Nothing about better arithmetic, a faster computer, or more patience will ever turn up the fraction, because the argument shows the fraction cannot exist at all.

Why It Matters

Two things are worth carrying forward from today, and they will both come up constantly for the rest of this subject and well beyond it.

The first is the number system itself: naturals, then integers, then rationals, then reals, each one built to hold a quantity the previous system couldn’t represent. Every later lesson in this block — algebra, functions, calculus, probability — quietly assumes you know which of these number systems it’s working in, and why that choice matters. It also matters outside pure mathematics: a computer cannot store an irrational number exactly, only approximate it to some number of digits, which is precisely why computer arithmetic occasionally produces tiny, surprising rounding errors that pure math never would.

The second, bigger thing is proof itself. Almost everything you’ll ever be told with confidence — in the news, in business, in most of science — is a claim backed by evidence: it has held up in every case examined, so far. That is genuinely useful knowledge, but it is provisional; a single new case can overturn it. A mathematical proof is a different kind of claim entirely: once the Pythagorean theorem is proved, or the square root of 2 is shown irrational, no future discovery can ever overturn it, because it was never a claim about the world’s past behavior to begin with — it is a claim about what necessarily follows from the definitions themselves. Recognizing that difference — between “true in everything we’ve seen” and “true, full stop, forever” — is one habit of mind this whole Mathematics block is here to build.

Two threads from today resurface later in this block. The real numbers built here are exactly what Day 9’s formal definition of a limit needs in order to work at all — without a number line with no gaps, calculus cannot get off the ground. And the tension between “true because it can be proved” and the much stranger question of what proof itself can and cannot ever settle returns, with far bigger consequences, on Day 20’s look at the actual limits of mathematics.

Remember These
  • The number system grew in stages — naturals, then integers, then rationals, then reals — each stage added to hold a quantity the last one couldn’t.
  • An irrational number, like the square root of 2, cannot be written as any ratio of two whole numbers — not undiscovered, proven impossible.
  • A mathematical proof shows a claim is true in every possible case, forever, from pure logic — not by checking examples, however many.
  • Proof by contradiction: assume the opposite of what you want to show, follow the logic, and if it leads somewhere impossible, the original claim must be true.
  • Irrational numbers aren’t just an abstraction — the shape of ordinary printer paper is built on one.
Think About It

The Pythagoreans found exactly one irrational number and it broke their worldview. It later turned out that between any two rational numbers, however close together, there are infinitely many irrational numbers — in a precise sense, irrationals vastly outnumber rationals on the number line. Given that, why do you think whole numbers and fractions were discovered first, and irrational numbers only much later and with such difficulty, even though irrationals are actually the overwhelming majority of numbers that exist?
