Weekly Review

Weekly Review — Sep 21–25, 2026

Saturday, September 26, 2026 · 51 min read

The bond market did the Fed’s arguing for it this week — the 10-year hit its highest yield since 2007 and two note auctions sold soft — while stocks rode an AI rally to a modest gain, Xi’s state visit bought a trade truce but no Taiwan answer, and by Saturday morning Trump had reportedly turned down Iran’s offer to reopen Hormuz.

The Brief

If you read nothing else

  • Markets. The S&P 500 rose 1.2% on the week to 7,743 and the Nasdaq 2.1%. The gains were narrow: chips (SMH +5.9%), Meta (+12.9%) and AMD (+12.6%, briefly worth $1 trillion) carried the week. The Russell 2000 fell 0.8% and the equal-weight S&P 0.6%, and utilities, real estate and banks all lost ground.
  • Rates. The 10-year Treasury yield rose 16 basis points to 5.17%, its highest since 2007, and the 30-year closed at 5.49%. Nearly all of the move was in real yields rather than inflation expectations. A hot flash PMI, two Fed officials calling another hike likely, and the weakest 5-year auction since 2018 all landed in the same 48 hours.
  • Since Friday’s close. Iran offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days in exchange for lifting the US blockade and oil sanctions. The Wall Street Journal reports that Trump rejected it and expects to resume bombing Iran after the Nov. 3 midterms. The White House has not confirmed that account. Houthi missiles and drones were fired at Riyadh and Khamis Mushait overnight; all were intercepted.
  • The world. Xi’s three-day state visit extended the US–China trade truce to Jan. 10 and set up an AI-incident channel, but produced no joint statement and no decision on the $14 billion Taiwan arms package. Trump met Zelensky, said Ukraine could win back all its territory, and backed an energy-strike truce that neither side observed.
  • Washington. A federal judge ordered the White House to restore CNN, MS NOW and Politico’s access for 14 days. Friday evening, the Supreme Court blocked Missouri’s GOP-drawn congressional map for a third time and separately let DHS’s citizenship-check database be used on voter rolls.

Today’s News

Iran offers to reopen Hormuz in seven days; Trump is reported to have said no, and to be planning strikes after the midterms

Tehran’s foreign minister made the offer publicly in New York Friday night. Within hours the Wall Street Journal, citing unnamed US officials, reported that Trump had rejected it and expects to resume bombing Iran once the Nov. 3 election is over.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told reporters in New York on Friday night that Tehran had sent a proposal through mediators; press reports say it was handed to envoy Steve Witkoff on the UN sidelines. Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz and return to nuclear talks within seven days. In exchange, the US would lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waive oil sanctions and observe a ceasefire that includes Lebanon. The timetable Iran published has the first steps taking four to five days, the strait reopening on day six and talks on a final deal starting on day seven.

Unnamed officials, unconfirmed The Wall Street Journal reported, citing US officials it did not name, that Trump turned the offer down. By that account he is skeptical Iran will meet his demands and expects to resume bombing after the midterms, with the scale of any strikes undecided and his position still open to change. Bloomberg, CNBC, CBS, France 24 and Al Jazeera all carried the report, but every version traces to the Journal’s sourcing. The White House has not commented. Trump’s only public word Saturday morning was a Truth Social post: “IRAN CAN NOT HAVE A NUCLEAR WEAPON!!!” Iranian semi-official media say President Pezeshkian has left New York for Tehran.

If the Journal’s account is right, it answers the question hanging over the paper for two weeks: which way Trump’s “big decision” on Iran breaks. The answer would be force, deferred past an election.

Why it matters Hormuz carries about a fifth of the world’s oil trade and has been effectively closed to routine shipping for weeks. On Sept. 13 about eight tankers passed through it, against a normal count near 85 a day. An offer to reopen it that is rejected, together with a strike plan timed after the election, points to a closed strait and elevated oil for at least five more weeks.
What’s next Any on-record White House response, and whether Iran withdraws or amends the offer. Oil eased on deal hopes this week, with front-month WTI down 7.9% to $92.41. The Sunday-evening futures open is the market’s first chance to price the reported rejection.

Houthi missiles and drones fired at Riyadh and Khamis Mushait overnight, hours after Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani military chiefs met

All four were intercepted, the coalition says. The Gulf Cooperation Council called it a “grave escalation.”

The Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted two ballistic missiles aimed at Khamis Mushait, the southwestern city that hosts a major Saudi air base, and two drones heading for the Riyadh area early Saturday. No damage or casualties have been reported. Coalition spokesman Maj. Gen. Turki al-Malki confirmed the interception of a missile aimed at Khamis Mushait (AP). The launches came hours after the military chiefs of Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan met in Riyadh on Friday under the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement signed Aug. 7. The three were Gen. Fayyadh Al-Ruwaili, Gen. Selçuk Bayraktaroğlu and Field Marshal Asim Munir, and they agreed to deepen intelligence and operational cooperation. Al Jazeera describes Pakistan and Turkey as edging closer to joining the war against the Houthis; none of the three governments has said that.

Seven killed as Russia and Ukraine trade strikes overnight; Ukraine sets a refinery near the Black Sea ablaze

The energy-strike truce Trump and Zelensky discussed on Tuesday is still not in force, and Washington has not set a date for the Abu Dhabi technical talks it proposed.

Russian strikes killed four people overnight: two women in the rubble of their home in Zaporizhzhia and two people in Sumy. Supreme Court blocks Missouri’s GOP-drawn map for a third time, and lets states use DHS’s citizenship database on voter rolls

Two election-law orders on Friday, one late in the evening. Missouri votes on its 2022 lines on Nov. 3.

In an unsigned order Friday evening with no noted dissents, the Court blocked an Eighth Circuit directive to switch Missouri to its 2025 congressional map, which was expected to net Republicans one House seat. “At this late date, ordering reversion to the 2025 map in the midst of an ongoing election would usher in electoral chaos,” the Court wrote; ballots have already gone out. This ends, for this cycle, the fight that brought Secretary of State Denny Hoskins before the Missouri Supreme Court on contempt earlier this month.

Earlier Friday the Court voted 6–3 to stay a June district-court ruling, so that DHS’s overhauled SAVE system can be used by states to check voter rolls against citizenship records. SAVE is now linked to Social Security data. Justice Jackson dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Kagan. Votebeat, which covers election administration, expects limited practical effect in November given how close the election is.

Supreme Court orders (primary) · Washington Post · Roll Call · Missouri Independent · NPR · CNN · Votebeat.

Beijing lists eight outcomes from Xi’s visit, including a $30 billion tariff cut Washington has not spelled out

Xi was back in Beijing Saturday afternoon. China’s account of the summit goes further than the White House’s.

China’s state media published an “eight-point consensus” from the Sept. 23–25 visit. It lists:

  • reciprocal tariff reductions on roughly $30 billion of “noncritical” goods, agricultural and energy products among them;
  • both leaders attending APEC and the G20;
  • no transit fees on international waterways (an implicit reference to Hormuz);
  • a call for Iran to honour its pledge not to develop nuclear weapons;
  • a memorandum on military crisis communication;
  • cooperation on recovering the remains of US service members;
  • progress on counternarcotics;
  • a new bilateral channel for AI incidents, with a first dialogue in November.

The two outcomes Washington has confirmed on the record are the extension of the trade truce to Jan. 10, 2027 (by Treasury Secretary Bessent) and the AI channel. South Korea’s Seoul Economic Daily and AP both note gaps between the Chinese list and the White House version. None of the published outcomes mentions the Taiwan arms package.

The Week’s Ten Stories

1 · The long bond breaks higher: the 10-year at a 19-year high, the 30-year at 5.49%

A week after the Fed’s first hike since 2023, the market pushed yields up further on its own, and the move was in real rates.

How it started. Calmly: the 10-year opened the week at 4.96%, and Tuesday’s 2-year auction went off cleanly.

How it developed. Wednesday broke it. The flash PMI showed the fastest private-sector growth in five years, Fed Governor Michael Barr said further hikes were “likely to be needed,” and the 5-year auction drew the weakest demand since 2018. Its share taken by indirect bidders, the category that includes foreign central banks, was the lowest since March 2020. The 10-year jumped 15 basis points in a day. Thursday, New York Fed President John Williams called another hike likely, and the 7-year sold at the highest yield since 1993. The 10-year closed Thursday at 5.18%.

How it ended. Friday the curve twisted: short yields fell, while the 20- and 30-year closed at new multi-decade highs. On the week the 10-year rose 16 basis points to 5.17%. The 10-year real yield rose 17 to 2.85% while inflation expectations barely moved. Five bond funds on this paper’s watchlist touched 52-week lows.

Why it mattered. Higher real yields raise the discount rate on every long-lived asset, from growth stocks to houses. This week they rose without any rise in expected inflation, which says investors want more pay for lending long, not that they fear inflation more.

What was misunderstood. “One more hike priced in” does not explain a long end making new highs on a day short rates fell. Term premium, the extra yield demanded for lending longer, fits better. That is a Market narrative reading, not a confirmed cause.

Unresolved. Whether foreign demand at auctions keeps fading, and whether next week’s inflation and jobs data (below) confirm the Fed’s hawkish dots or undercut them.

2 · Xi in Washington: a trade truce extended, an AI hotline opened, the Taiwan question left open

The first Chinese state visit in eleven years was heavy on ceremony and light on concrete outcomes. Beijing threatened to walk away over Taiwan beforehand and did not.

How it started. Bessent and Vice Premier He Lifeng’s Sunday talks produced a US proposal for an AI-incident channel. Beijing warned the $14 billion Taiwan arms package, authorised by Congress but unsigned, could derail the visit.

How it developed. Trump greeted Xi on the tarmac Wednesday. Overnight the truce was extended from Nov. 10 to Jan. 10, 2027, which Bessent confirmed on the record. Thursday brought the bilateral meeting and a state dinner. Xinhua said Xi pressed Trump to “oppose Taiwan independence,” wording stronger than the standard US line.

How it ended. No joint statement. A senior official said Trump will decide on the arms package “in a fairly short time”; four Republican senators urged him to proceed.

Why it mattered. The truce extension removes a November tariff cliff that would have landed the week after the midterms. The AI channel is a first between the two governments.

What was misunderstood. Pre-summit reports that Beijing would cancel over Taiwan were disputed by a senior US official and never materialised. Several outlets called the missing joint statement a failure, but Chinese state visits often end without one.

Unresolved. The arms package. China-exposed and chip stocks remain sensitive to that decision.

Treasury Secretary Bessent (on record) · Xinhua · China Ministry of Foreign Affairs · Reuters · South China Morning Post · CNN · Washington Times.

3 · Iran: strikes called off, “deal or annihilate” at the UN, a back channel opened, and an offer reportedly refused

The week began with munitions loaded for Yemen and ended with a reported plan to bomb Iran after the midterms. In between came the first direct-adjacent US–Iran contact in weeks.

How it started. Over last weekend Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman called Trump twice urging US strikes on the Houthis. Trump cut short Camp David, then called the strikes off, with bombs reportedly already loaded on fighter jets (Axios). US officials met Houthi representatives in Muscat. A Houthi official claimed the group agreed to stop targeting US shipping; neither Washington nor Riyadh has confirmed that.

How it developed. Tuesday Trump told the UN he would reach a deal with Iran or “annihilate” it; hours later Kushner and Witkoff met Iranian officials indirectly. A tanker entering Hormuz had been hit by an unclaimed projectile Monday, injuring two crew.

How it ended. Iran made its seven-day Hormuz offer Friday night. The Journal reports it was rejected, and that bombing will resume after November (Today’s News). That report is not confirmed.

What was misunderstood. Much of Monday’s coverage read the cancelled Yemen strikes as de-escalation. They were a deferral: the underlying Saudi requests, the Houthi advance and the closed strait were all unchanged.

Unresolved. Everything that matters: whether the reported post-election timetable is real, whether Iran’s offer survives its reported rejection, and who fired on the tankers.

Axios · CNN · NPR · UKMTO (primary, tanker incidents) · Al Jazeera · Wall Street Journal via Bloomberg and CNBC · White House (UN address, primary).

4 · Yemen: the Houthis fight for the high ground as Riyadh keeps coming under fire

The war the paper has tracked since August kept widening on the ground. G7 pressure and a new three-way military pact aimed at Riyadh’s enemies changed nothing yet.

How it started. Last Saturday’s missile on Riyadh, the first since the war widened, set fire to an Aramco fuel depot, and debris killed a Yemeni resident.

How it developed. Five Yemeni military sources told Reuters the Houthis were pushing for the Kahboub mountains, the heights between their new Red Sea coast and the government-held south. The G7 demanded on Tuesday that the group “immediately cease all military actions.” By Wednesday and Thursday, government forces claimed to have taken Mount Qarfan in Taiz and repelled a Houthi push in the Kahboub range (Al Jazeera). Neither side’s territorial claims are independently verified.

How it ended. With another salvo at Riyadh and Khamis Mushait overnight (Today’s News). At least 674 people have been killed and more than 158,000 displaced; UNHCR warns displacement could pass 230,000.

Unresolved. Whether the Houthis hold the Bab el-Mandeb coast, and whether Pakistan and Turkey join the fighting.

Saudi-led coalition and Houthi statements (primary, each disputed by the other) · Reuters · G7 statement (primary) · Al Jazeera · UNHCR · AP.

5 · Trump meets Zelensky and changes his line on Ukraine’s borders; the war carries on around the diplomacy

Three different meeting formats were floated in four days. None has a date, and the energy-strike pause Zelensky said he would accept never took hold.

How it developed. Trump met Zelensky in New York on Tuesday, then posted that Ukraine, with European support, could “fight and win all of Ukraine back in its original form.” That reverses his earlier position that a settlement would follow current lines. The two sides agreed to pursue a reciprocal halt on strikes against energy infrastructure. The Kremlin dismissed the territorial claim; spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Russia was “a real bear,” not “a paper bear.” Zelensky floated a three-way summit, possibly at the G20 in Miami; by Friday Washington had proposed technical talks in Abu Dhabi instead.

How it ended. Both sides struck energy targets every night, including Saturday’s refinery hit in Krasnodar. The EU extended its Russia sanctions through 2029 but dropped two oligarchs from the list, which Kyiv called “shameful.”

What was misunderstood. Trump’s territorial remark was read in some coverage as a policy shift. No change in US aid, sanctions or negotiating terms has followed it.

6 · The AI trade has its biggest week in a month: AMD worth $1 trillion, Meta up 13%, then a warning on AI spending

Two Wall Street notes framed the week. One said Meta’s Muse agent could be the biggest consumer AI app since ChatGPT; the other asked whether any of the hyperscalers’ spending pays for itself.

How it started. Monday a Meta-led chip rally sent the Nasdaq to a record close, and AMD briefly topped $1 trillion in market value, the fourth chipmaker to do so. Amazon had begun blocking Meta’s Muse shopping agent from its store over the weekend.

How it developed. Wednesday’s rate shock knocked the Nasdaq down 1.1%. On Thursday JPMorgan raised its Meta target to $920, calling Muse potentially the most widely used consumer AI app since ChatGPT.

How it ended. Friday a Goldman Sachs note estimated that the five biggest US hyperscalers will spend about $800 billion on AI capital projects this year and about $1.2 trillion next. It put the annual AI revenue needed just to break even at roughly $300 billion. Meta fell 3.3%, also weighed by a reported security flaw in Muse. Microsoft rose 3.7% on the day and 4.5% on the week.

Why it mattered. The AI trade made up most of the week’s index gain. SMH rose 5.9%, while the equal-weight S&P fell.

Unresolved. Micron’s earnings on Sept. 30 are the next hard data point on AI demand.

7 · Three newsrooms take the White House press ban to court, and win the first round

Judge Timothy Kelly, who ruled for CNN’s Jim Acosta on similar facts in 2018, restored CNN, MS NOW and Politico’s access for 14 days.

How it started. The three outlets sued Monday. The TV networks suspended pool coverage in solidarity, and the White House launched a 24/7 “Trump TV” stream.

How it developed. The Justice Department argued access is “a privilege, not a right,” citing national security.

How it ended. Kelly’s order, issued early Thursday, found the ban likely violated due process. Access broke down at the gate Thursday morning, then was restored.

Unresolved. The order lasts 14 days, and a preliminary-injunction fight and an appeal are likely.

8 · Costco beats, and the one-time tariff refund is not one-time

Fiscal fourth-quarter EPS of $6.75 beat estimates even without the refund. Analysts still cut their targets, and the stock rose anyway.

Costco reported $6.75 a share for its 16-week fiscal fourth quarter against a consensus near $6.52. Comparable sales rose 9.4% and membership fees 7.3%. The print included a non-recurring $0.15-a-share benefit from IEEPA tariff refunds. The company said more refunds will show up in coming quarters: it has received about a third of what it expects. Excluding the refund, EPS of about $6.60 still beat.

Beneath the beat, gross margin fell 11 basis points year over year, and on a core-on-core basis 32 basis points, per call summaries. Seven of eight analyst price-target changes Friday were cuts. The shares rose 2.9% Friday and 3.1% on the week.

Unresolved. How much of next year’s earnings growth is refunds rather than shoppers.

Costco 8-K (primary, SEC EDGAR) · Benzinga · Motley Fool · Yahoo Finance.

9 · Russia’s wartime Duma vote returns a record majority amid fraud allegations

The official count gives United Russia about 58% of the party-list vote, on pace for roughly 355 of 450 seats. Final results are due Oct. 5.

The first vote since the full-scale war began, and the first to include the four annexed Ukrainian regions, gave United Russia its largest majority, beating 2016’s 343 seats, on official turnout of 56.66%. An analysis by Novaya Gazeta Europe, relayed by Meduza, puts United Russia’s real vote nearer 34%. Meduza also published leaked documents describing vote-altering tools in the state’s “Vybory 2.0” system. Neither claim has been independently corroborated.

Russian Central Election Commission (primary, government-reported) · Meduza and Novaya Gazeta Europe (single investigative chain) · Moscow Times · Time.

10 · Paramount settles with the states on Warner Bros. Discovery, but its own filing says the timing is not yet certain

Monday’s settlement with 12 state attorneys general sent Warner shares up 10.8%. Paramount’s own filing on Friday was more guarded than the week’s headlines.

The settlement, announced Monday, ended the state antitrust challenge to Paramount Skydance’s roughly $111 billion takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery. That would put CNN under the same owner as CBS News, in the same week CNN was suing the White House. Midweek reports had the deal closing within two weeks. But an 8-K Paramount filed Friday says the merger “is subject to further closing conditions” and that its timing “is not yet certain.” The filing also sets up a move of Paramount’s listing to the NYSE around Oct. 6 and about 470 million warrants for shareholders, contingent on closing. Warner shares ended the week at $30.86, up 11%, well short of what a done deal would imply.

What Changed in the World

The price of money for the long haul went up, and not because of inflation. Two weeks ago, the question was whether the Fed would hike. This week the bond market answered a different one. Real 10-year yields near 2.85% and a 30-year near 5.5% are levels not seen since before the 2008 crisis. They came with soft auction demand from foreign buyers and flat inflation expectations. That shifts what matters for the next few months from the Fed’s dots to who buys US debt, and at what price.

The Iran war now appears to be on a timetable. If the Journal’s account holds, the “big decision” is force, deferred past the election. That makes Hormuz a five-week problem at minimum and ties oil, defense and shipping to a political calendar. Until this weekend it was an open question with no date.

US–China relations stabilised without either side conceding anything. A truce extension, a crisis-communications channel and an AI hotline lower the odds of an accident. On the dispute that could still break the relationship, Taiwan, neither side gave ground.

Much of this week’s diplomacy produced statements rather than decisions. An energy truce that was never observed, a Ukraine summit with three possible venues and no date, a G7 demand the Houthis ignored, a Chinese summit readout Washington has not matched. Against that, four decisions this week had force: the Fed’s officials, the bond auctions, a federal judge and the Supreme Court.

The Week in Markets

The indices. The S&P 500 rose 1.2% to 7,743.41, its first winning week in three and within about 1% of its August record. The Nasdaq Composite gained 2.1% to 27,068.72, and the Dow 0.3% to 51,828.62. The Russell 2000 fell 0.8% to 2,837.55. The week’s gain came almost entirely from Monday (S&P +1.5%, a record Nasdaq close). Three losing sessions followed as yields climbed, including Wednesday’s −0.75% on the rate shock, before Friday’s +0.5% on better-than-feared data. AP noted cooler oil prices as some relief. Likely contributor: the AI trade on Monday and data on Friday. There is no single confirmed catalyst for the week.

Narrow leadership. The market was split: the cap-weighted S&P rose 1.3% (SPY) while the equal-weighted version (RSP) fell 0.6%. Growth and momentum factors gained 2.5–2.9% while value, dividend and low-volatility funds fell. Whole-market breadth ran positive on Monday, Tuesday and Friday and sharply negative on Wednesday and Thursday. Wednesday was the worst session in weeks, with 1,081 stocks up against 4,430 down.

Sectors. Technology led (XLK +3.5%), with semiconductors (SMH +5.9%, SOXX +7.4%) at the front, followed by communication services (XLC +1.9%, Meta) and health care (XLV +1.4%). Rate-sensitive sectors fell hardest: utilities −3.9%, energy −3.5% as oil eased, real estate −2.3% and financials −1.8%. Regional banks lost 1.7%, hit by a flatter curve early in the week before it steepened again. Utilities’ decline to near a 52-week low, alongside the 30-year yield at a 22-year high, is the clearest cross-asset link of the week: bond-like stocks were priced against higher bond yields.

Best and worst on the watchlists. Winners: Firefly (+16.2%), Rocket Lab (+14.5%), Meta (+12.9%), AMD (+12.6%), Intuitive Machines (+12.1%), Marvell (+7.2%), Palantir (+6.8%), Delta (+6.7%), Micron (+6.5%), Danaher (+6.0%). Losers: Voyager Technologies (−12.6%), Novo Nordisk (−10.3%, after a capital-markets-day strategy that disappointed investors), Chipotle (−6.3%), BWX Technologies (−6.1%), Schwab (−5.9%), Cboe (−5.4%), Airbnb (−5.3%), Blackstone (−5.2%). Defense primes fell 2–5% even as the Gulf war escalated, and large banks slipped 1–4%.

Rates. The Treasury curve bear-steepened: long yields rose more than short ones. The 2-year added 5 basis points to 4.81%, the 5-year 12 to 4.98%, the 10-year 16 to 5.17%, the 20-year 16 to 5.54% and the 30-year 15 to 5.49%. The gap between 2- and 10-year yields widened from 25 to 36 basis points after narrowing to 20 on Monday. The 10-year real yield (TIPS) rose to 2.85% and the 10-year breakeven inflation rate held at 2.34%, so the whole move was in real rates. Wednesday alone accounted for 15 basis points on the 10-year.

Credit, dollar, volatility. High-yield spreads widened 12 basis points to 2.80% (Thursday), the most in weeks but still historically tight; investment-grade spreads widened only 2 basis points to 0.79%. LQD fell 1.4% and HYG 0.9%, both mostly on rates. The dollar index rose 0.7% to 100.97; USD/JPY touched 158.81 Thursday. The VIX closed the week at 14.87, essentially unchanged from 14.81, with no inversion in its term structure all week. The rate shock did not reach equity volatility.

Commodities and crypto. Front-month WTI fell 7.9% to $92.41, most of it Monday (−4.5%), on hopes of a deal to reopen Hormuz. Brent quotes conflict between sources (see the appendix). Natural gas rose 9.8%, mostly on Thursday, on a below-average storage build and cooler forecasts. Gold fell 2.3% to about $4,321, silver 3.5%, while copper rose 1.2%. Higher real yields are a likely contributor to the metals’ fall. Bitcoin rose about 3.4% to $84,000, peaking near $86,600 on Monday.

The Economy & Central Banks

The data ran hot where it mattered.

  • Flash PMIs (Wednesday). S&P Global’s September composite reached 58.4, the fastest growth in more than five years. Manufacturing came in at 57.0 (forecast 53.6), the best since May 2022, and services at 58.7 (forecast 56.0).
  • Durable goods (Friday). August orders were flat against an expected 0.4% decline. Core capital-goods orders, a gauge of business investment, rose 1.6% against 0.5% expected.
  • Jobless claims. Initial claims fell to 197,000 and continuing claims were 1.72 million, a labor market with little slack.
  • New home sales. August sales jumped 6.4% to a 684,000 annual rate, the highest since December, but the median price fell 5.8% from a year earlier and supply stands at 8.5 months. Builders are cutting prices to move homes.
  • The soft spots. The Richmond Fed manufacturing index fell to −2. The Chicago Fed National Activity Index was −0.04. The University of Michigan’s final September sentiment reading was 48.1, near a four-month low, while one-year inflation expectations jumped to 4.6% from 4.0%, the steepest monthly rise since June. Households feel prices far more than the bond market’s inflation gauges do.
  • The external balance. The second-quarter current-account deficit widened 15.7% to $246 billion, 3.0% of GDP, on a larger goods deficit. That is relevant to a week in which foreign demand at Treasury auctions softened.

The Fed: a chorus for one more hike.

  • Barr (Wednesday). Inflation is “not clearly trending toward target in a timely way.” The Fed had been “out of position,” and “in my base case, further policy adjustments are likely to be needed.”
  • Williams (Thursday). It is “likely that another rate hike may be appropriate by the end of the year,” though he stopped short of committing to October.
  • Barkin (Tuesday). Inflation risks “outweigh the risks to maximum employment.”
  • Paulson (Philadelphia, Thursday). “Some modest further tightening may be warranted.”
  • Hammack (Cleveland). Inflation risks are “tilted to the upside.” She tied long yields to growth, debt and the expected rate path.
  • Goolsbee. Getting back to 2% will be “painful.”
  • Jefferson spoke only on the discount window; Warsh said nothing in public.

Fed-funds futures, as reported by several secondary trackers, moved the odds of a 25-basis-point hike at the Oct. 27–28 meeting from about half to roughly 70–78% after Williams spoke. That is an estimate; this paper did not read CME’s own page. The Fed’s balance sheet was steady at $6.75 trillion. Bank reserves fell $84 billion as the Treasury’s cash account at the Fed rose $100 billion, most likely the usual mid-September corporate-tax inflow.

On debt management, Deputy Secretary Francis Brooke told the New York Fed’s Treasury market conference on Tuesday that buybacks have now totalled nearly $500 billion over more than 150 operations, against $32 trillion of marketable debt. Scorecard

Twenty-four forecasts came due this week, including several overdue from August: 8 correct, 13 wrong, 3 partial. As always, a forecast counts as “correct” when what it said would happen did happen, whatever probability it carried. So a low-probability call that did not happen shows up as “wrong” even when the probability was about right. The lessons below separate those cases from genuine misses.

Last Sunday’s outlook: 3 of 5.

  • No US strike on Houthi or Iranian targets through Friday (70–80%): correct, but only just. The Yemen strikes were loaded and then cancelled.
  • Costco beats consensus EPS (55–65%): correct, and the beat survived stripping out the tariff refund.
  • The 10-year closes above 5.00% Friday (55–65%): correct at 5.17%. The data ran hotter than the Sunday base case assumed, so the call was right partly for a different reason.
  • No confirmed Trump–Zelensky meeting at the UN (55–65%): wrong. They met Tuesday. The forecast read too much into the meeting’s absence from a released schedule when both teams were openly arranging it.
  • The Kennedy Center’s sworn report draws further court action (40–50%): wrong. The report extended the closure through Sept. 30 on safety grounds, and Judge Cooper has said nothing since. That is the second week running this paper leaned toward confrontation in a court fight and the court deferred instead.

The scenario matrix. Sunday’s base case (incremental escalation in Yemen without US strikes, no breakthrough at the UN, PMIs near consensus, a modest Costco beat) was right on everything except the data, which came in hot. The bear case’s rates leg played out almost exactly: a hot PMI, the 10-year pushing further above 5%, small caps and real estate lagging. Its credit leg did not: high-yield spreads widened only to 2.80%, well short of the 3.00% confirmation line. Stocks still rose, which neither case described.

Summit and diplomacy calls were well calibrated, but two were badly defined. Three forecasts bundled “no joint statement” into a Trump–Xi disruption forecast. One, at 15–25%, graded correct and another, at 20–35%, partial, on that technicality alone, even though nothing was disrupted. Another lumped US and Saudi restraint together and cannot be cleanly scored. Keep routine outcomes out of disruption forecasts, and keep separate actors separate. Correctly calibrated low-probability misses: Trump signing the Taiwan package before the summit (15–25%) and the Trump–Zelensky meeting producing a binding, dated step (10–20%).

Markets and data calls: right on direction, too timid on magnitude. The forecast that the 7-year auction would sell soft graded correct, but at only 20–30% it gave too little weight to what the 5-year had shown the day before. The forecast that Costco would miss once the one-time refund was excluded (25–35%) graded wrong: margins were softer, but earnings still beat. An August forecast of a September rate cut (40–55%) is graded wrong now that the Fed has hiked. It was never revisited after the hot August inflation prints, and it should have been.

The Gulf file: rhetoric kept outrunning action. Iran published no coordinates for its threatened Hormuz exclusion zone by either of two deadlines. The Iran–Oman shipping corridor was never signed. Saudi Arabia never answered the claimed Mecca drone with a distinct, attributable strike. A Fox News report of an attack on a US-contracted vessel was never confirmed. All five graded wrong, and all five carried low probabilities that were roughly right. Two Gulf calls graded correct: a further attack near Hormuz within a week (it came Sept. 21, and the 30–45% range was too low), and Iran not formally blockading all Gulf oil traffic. That second one was right on the letter but missed the substance, because traffic collapsed anyway without a declared blockade.

Everything else. Correct: no further sanction on Missouri’s secretary of state. Wrong: China naming a new Chang’e 7 launch date (its space agency had effectively ruled out this year before the forecast was written); Rep. Casar escalating through a subpoena threat, hearing or joint statement (he escalated through a bill instead, a day after the deadline). Partial: a Saudi strike on Houthi positions at the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint, which is reported but sourced mainly to the Houthis.

A premise that was wrong. Last week’s editions twice described SOFR’s 23-basis-point jump on Sept. 17 as unexplained, possibly quarter-end funding stress. It was the Fed’s hike, which took effect that day, and the effective fed funds rate moved by the same amount. A forecast still open until Sept. 30, that funding rates stay elevated because of repo pressure, rests on that mistake. It will be graded on its literal terms with the flawed premise noted. The correction is logged.

Risks Entering the Week

A long end that keeps climbing without a catalyst. The 20- and 30-year made new highs Friday on a day with no auction and no Fed speaker. Next week brings August PCE inflation (Wednesday) and the September jobs report (Friday, Oct. 2). If both run hot, a further leg higher is plausible, and futures already price an October hike as more likely than not. The strain would land first on rate-sensitive sectors that are already weak: real estate, utilities, regional banks and small caps.

Oil priced to a political calendar. A reported post-midterm strike plan and a closed Hormuz point to elevated prices, with spikes on any tanker incident. Front-month WTI fell 7.9% this week on hopes of a Hormuz deal; those hopes are exactly what the weekend report undercuts. A confirmed rejection of Iran’s offer, or any US strike before November, is the trigger to watch.

Quarter-end. Tuesday, Sept. 30 closes the quarter. Watch funding markets, index rebalancing and window dressing, as funds tidy portfolios before reporting. It is also the day Duquesne Light’s union contract expires (Local, below) and the day Micron reports.

Crowding in the AI trade. The week’s gains rested on a handful of AI names. Goldman’s capex note already knocked Meta 3.3% in a day. A disappointing Micron outlook would test how much of September’s rally was positioning.

The Taiwan decision. A signature on the $14 billion package “in a fairly short time” would test the truce agreed Thursday. Beijing has called the package a red line.

The World

The General Assembly’s general debate pauses Saturday and concludes Monday. Much of the week’s diplomacy happened on its sidelines, including a Trump–Macron pledge on Hormuz that Paris called a step toward options rather than a plan.

AI & Technology

Anthropic’s pre-IPO week. Akamai announced Thursday evening an $11.6 billion, seven-year agreement to supply Anthropic with distributed cloud computing, via a press release and an 8-K. It is the largest deal in Akamai’s history and could expand to about $20 billion. Anthropic receives a warrant for up to about 5% of Akamai, vesting with its spending. Akamai raised its 2026 capital budget by about $1.7 billion. Its shares, which jumped 12% Monday on early reports, closed the week up 9% at $113.94. Separately, Single source The Information reports that Anthropic has asked shareholders to give its seven co-founders a combined 50.1% of voting power on most matters, with no extra economic stake. The control would last while at least three of the seven keep a minimum holding, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust would still pick most of the board. Press reports put the listing in late October or November at a valuation near $2 trillion. Anthropic has not confirmed either, and this week’s EDGAR sweep found no public filing.

Chips. Alibaba unveiled the Zhenwu V900, which it calls China’s most powerful AI chip: about three times its predecessor, scalable to 500,000-chip clusters, with mass production in early 2027. It landed two days before Xi did, and was widely read as a statement of Chinese self-sufficiency. The US–China AI-incident channel agreed at the summit is covered above. Its terms have not been published.

Agents collide. By Friday, The Information reported a bug-bounty-disclosed flaw in Meta’s Muse that could expose a user’s cloud workspace if they processed a malicious link and approved it. Meta rated it moderate and is adding clearer warnings. Amazon’s block of Muse from its store remains in place.

Regulation. The Sanders–Casar Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act would create a Cabinet-level Department of AI, ban “superintelligent” systems, pause advanced development pending safety rules, and threaten up to 20 years in prison. With the House in recess it is a marker, not a vote. New York City’s council has summoned OpenAI and Anthropic to testify Oct. 5. Mustafa Suleyman’s Sept. 16 critique of how Anthropic trains Claude has drawn no public reply.

Security. CISA’s deadline for agencies to patch the actively exploited F5 BIG-IP flaw (CVE-2026-94127, rated 9.8 of 10) passed Friday with more than 14,700 exposed systems still visible in the latest scan. Of the four AI coding tools hit by last week’s “Plugin4Shell” flaw, Claude Code and Codex are patched; GitHub Copilot is not, and Google retired Gemini CLI instead of fixing it.

Science

Ebola in Congo: better in one province, worse in another. The WHO’s latest count (Sept. 23) is 7,890 confirmed cases and 3,799 deaths, a fatality rate near 48%, across seven provinces. Ituri, the original epicentre, is down about 26% over 21 days, but North Kivu is up 73%, and the virus reached two new health zones this week, on the borders with the Central African Republic and South Sudan. This is the Bundibugyo strain, for which the licensed vaccines and treatments (designed for the Zaire strain) are not approved. Doctors Without Borders began a vaccine-effectiveness study among 20,000 health workers last weekend.

Measles: the first US death in the national count. The CDC confirmed its first measles-related death of 2026 on Wednesday, without naming the state. That sits awkwardly beside Pennsylvania’s own count of four deaths (Local, below). The CDC’s national tally was 3,471 cases as of Sept. 17, the most since measles was declared eliminated in 2000.

Research. A study in Geosphere, based on 3,100 years of seafloor sediment cores, found paired landslide layers suggesting that great Cascadia earthquakes, including the one in 1700, were followed within minutes to hours by ruptures on the northern San Andreas fault. If confirmed, that would mean the Pacific Northwest and Northern California face a linked hazard rather than two separate ones.

Space

Starship’s first orbital flight waits on a licence. Flight 14 is set for Monday, Sept. 28 at 8:15 AM ET from Starbase. It is the debut of the V3 vehicle, carries the first Starlink V3 satellites, and is planned as the first full orbital flight. Booster 21 and Ship 41 are stacked, environmental approvals are done and flight restrictions are posted through Oct. 7. The FAA has not yet issued the launch licence itself; SpaceX expects it before Monday.

SpaceX as a stock. The first post-IPO lockup expired Thursday, freeing about 328 million shares (roughly $49 billion). A day earlier a filing showed President Gwynne Shotwell proposing to sell 342,170 shares under a pre-arranged plan, and the stock fell more than 4%. The unlock itself passed without a dislocation. SPCX ended the week down 2.6% at $148.68, with further unlocks on Oct. 9 and Oct. 24.

NASA picks a new telescope. PRIMA, a far-infrared observatory with its cost capped at $1.2 billion, is the first mission in NASA’s new Probe class, targeting a 2033 launch to study how stars, planets and galaxies form. Albania became the 73rd signatory of the Artemis Accords.

Launches and space stocks. China flew a Long March 8A with its 26th batch of Guowang broadband satellites and a Long March 6 with a Yaogan-40 group. Space stocks rallied despite SpaceX’s slip: Firefly +16.2%, Rocket Lab +14.5%, Intuitive Machines +12.1%. Voyager Technologies (−12.6%, no identified catalyst) was the exception.

Undercovered. Real yields. Most coverage said “yields hit a 2007 high.” Few noted that nearly all the move was in real rates, with inflation expectations flat.
