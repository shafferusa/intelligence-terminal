Why Doubling Your Speed Quadruples Your Stopping Distance

Thursday, September 10, 2026 · 26 min read

Every form of motion, height and heat turns out to be priced in the same currency — energy — and today’s exchange rate is not the one intuition expects: work is the transaction, power is how fast the money moves, and the same theorem that explains why a car going twice as fast needs four times the road to stop is quietly the same one setting the electric bill for every AI data center this paper has been covering all year.

Physics

Day 19 of 150 · Physics 4 of 26 · Foundations

A number driver’s ed never quite explains

Every learner driver eventually gets some version of the same lecture, delivered in slightly different words depending on who is teaching: leave more room when you go faster, because stopping takes longer. It is sound advice, and almost everyone quietly attaches a rough rule of thumb to it — something like doubling your speed roughly doubles how much room you need to stop. That rule survives a lifetime of ordinary, uneventful driving without ever being tested, which is exactly the problem, because it is wrong by a factor that matters enormously the one time it actually gets tested.

Ask almost anyone directly — if you are going twice as fast, how much extra pavement do you need to come to a full stop? — and the overwhelming majority answer some version of “twice as much,” treating braking distance as though it scales the same simple way distance-in-five-seconds scales with speed. It feels obviously right. Once reaction time is set aside and the question is narrowed to the stretch of road the brakes themselves actually need once they are applied, it is off by a factor of two, in exactly the direction that matters: not twice the room, but four times it.

The reason has almost nothing to do with brakes, tires, or cars specifically, and everything to do with a quantity that gets thrown around loosely in everyday speech — “I don’t have the energy,” “that took a lot of energy” — but that physics defines with a precision most people have never actually been taught. That quantity is today’s whole lesson: energy, the single ledger every physical process gets written into, and the two operations, work and power, that move numbers in and out of it.

The universal accounting currency

Strip away the units and the formulas for a moment and energy is a remarkably simple idea: it is the capacity to make something happen — to move something, heat something, light something, lift something. A moving car, a cup of hot coffee, a stretched bowstring, a full gas tank, a charged phone battery, a boulder balanced on a cliff edge — every one of these looks completely different, but underneath, each is storing or carrying the same single quantity, just parked in a different account: motion-energy, heat-energy, elastic energy, chemical energy, height-energy, electrical energy. Physics’ most useful trick, discovered piece by piece across the nineteenth century, is to treat every one of those accounts as interchangeable line items in the same ledger, measured in the same unit, and to insist on a strict rule: the total never appears from nowhere and never vanishes into nothing. It only ever moves from one account to another. That is the “universal accounting currency” this lesson is built around, and in a more careful, general form it is the first law of thermodynamics — a rule this curriculum will meet formally when it reaches heat and thermodynamics later in the physics track.

Work is the name for the specific transaction that moves energy from one account to another. Push a box across a floor and your muscles’ chemical-energy account drains while the box’s motion-energy account fills. Drop an apple and its height-energy account (formally, gravitational potential energy) drains while its motion-energy account fills, at exactly the same rate. Slam the brakes and the car’s motion-energy account drains while the brake pads’ and tires’ heat-energy accounts fill. In every case, work is a force acting through a distance, and it is the mechanism — the only mechanism — by which a specific number of energy units gets moved from one place to another. It is not a feeling, and it is not effort, a distinction the misconception section of this lesson will come back to directly.

Power is a completely separate idea: the speed of the transaction, not its size. Two ways exist to move the same thousand dollars — wire it instantly, or hand over a dollar a day for close to three years — and both move an identical total while doing so at wildly different rates. Power is the rate at which work gets done, the rate at which energy actually moves. Here is where the opening puzzle resolves, in plain words before a single equation appears: a tire’s grip on the road sets a strict ceiling on how many energy units friction can pull out of the car’s motion-energy account for every meter of skid — it cannot work any harder than that ceiling allows, no matter how fast the car was going when the brakes were applied. So the total bill that has to be paid before the car stops — and that bill happens to grow with the square of the car’s speed, for reasons the formalism below will derive properly — is what determines how many meters of road get used, not the speed of the payment itself. Double the car’s speed and its motion-energy account holds four times the balance it held before, while the rate at which friction can drain that account, meter for meter, has not changed at all. Four times the balance, same withdrawal rate per meter, means four times the distance to empty the account. That is the whole mechanism behind the number driver’s ed never quite explains.

One consequence of treating energy as a single, universal ledger is worth stating plainly before the formulas arrive, because it quietly rules out an idea people keep reinventing: a machine that runs forever, producing useful work with nothing put in to pay for it. Every real mechanism — an engine, a muscle, a falling weight turning a wheel — loses some fraction of the energy passing through it to friction, air resistance, or electrical resistance, all of which convert useful, organized motion-energy into disorganized heat that scatters into the surroundings and is, in practice, gone for good as far as doing further useful work is concerned. That one-way leak is why every real engine needs a continuous input to keep running, and it is also why “perpetual motion machine” patents, submitted by the hundreds over the centuries, have a perfect record of not working: not because inventors keep making the same fixable mistake, but because the accounting itself forbids it. A ledger that only ever loses money to friction’s fee can never balance on its own.

Work: a force paid out over a distance

For a force that stays constant while it acts, work has a clean, direct formula: multiply the force by the distance the object moves, and by the cosine of the angle between the two. That cosine term is doing real work in the formula (no pun intended, though the pun is unavoidable): it isolates the component of the force that actually lies along the direction of motion. A force pushing straight along the direction of travel does its full, undiscounted amount of work. A force at some angle to the motion does proportionally less. A force acting exactly perpendicular to the motion — cos 90° = 0 — does no work at all, however large it is. This is not a minor footnote: it is the reason the centripetal force this curriculum derived four lessons ago, which always points sideways toward the center of a circular path and never along the direction of travel, does zero work on a satellite or a spinning coin no matter how long it acts. A force can be large, real, and continuously present, and still transfer not one joule of energy, if it never points even partly the way the object is actually moving.

W = F d cosθ

W
work done by the force, in joules (J)
F
the magnitude of the constant force, in newtons
d
the magnitude of the object’s displacement while the force acts, in meters
θ
the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector

The unit itself, the joule, is named for a nineteenth-century English brewer who did physics as an evening avocation. James Prescott Joule managed his family’s brewery in Salford while running, largely in its cellar, one of the most stubbornly precise experimental campaigns of the era: an insulated, baffled can of water, a paddle wheel mounted inside it, and a pair of falling weights on pulleys turning that paddle wheel through the water. As the weights fell a known distance under a known force — a measured, calculable amount of mechanical work — the paddle’s drag through the water converted that work into heat, and Joule measured the resulting temperature rise with a thermometer precise enough to read hundredths of a degree. He first described the apparatus in 1845, refined it through a careful campaign in 1849, and published the results in 1850 as “On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat” — the paper that earned him election to the Royal Society. His measurement converged on a figure of about 4.15 joules for every calorie of heat produced; the calorie was later fixed, by international convention rather than by any new measurement, at exactly 4.184 joules, so the small gap between Joule’s number and today’s is mostly a matter of definitional tidying, not experimental error on his part.

Joule was not working in a vacuum. Half a century earlier, in 1798, the Bavarian army’s minister of war and part-time physicist Benjamin Thompson — better known by his title, Count Rumford — noticed something that the reigning theory of heat could not explain while watching cannon barrels being bored at a Munich arsenal: the boring tool, dulled on purpose so it would cut almost nothing, still made the brass barrel scalding hot, indefinitely, for as long as horses kept turning the boring machine. The theory of the day held that heat was a weightless, indestructible fluid called “caloric,” squeezed out of the metal as it was cut — but a dull tool cutting essentially no metal at all should have had almost none of that fluid to release, and yet the heat kept coming, apparently without limit, for as long as mechanical work kept being put in. Rumford published the observation the same year, arguing that heat looked less like a substance being released and more like motion itself, transferred from the turning mechanism into the metal. He did not have the tools to put a precise number on the connection between the two, and it took Joule’s paddle wheel another half-century to supply one, but Rumford’s cannon is the moment the idea that heat and mechanical work might be the same currency first became impossible to ignore.

Joule himself never saw his name attached to a unit: the physicist and engineer Wilhelm Siemens first proposed “joule” as a unit of heat in 1882, and it was formally adopted at the second International Electrical Congress on the last day of August 1889 — six weeks before Joule died. It became part of the SI system proper in 1948, when the ninth General Conference on Weights and Measures adopted it as the unit of energy, work, and heat, a status carried forward unchanged into the modern SI. A joule, in the units this lesson has already been using, is one newton of force acting over one meter — kg·m²/s² underneath the hood, the same unit whether the energy in question is motion, height, heat, or (as later physics lessons will show) light and electricity too.

From force and distance to a theorem about motion

Real forces are not always the tidy, constant kind the formula above assumes. Friction stays roughly constant across a skid, but plenty of forces — a spring pushing back harder the more it is compressed, gravity itself weakening very slightly with altitude — change continuously as the object moves. Work done by a changing force cannot be found by one multiplication; it has to be accumulated, a tiny slice of force-times-distance at a time, over the whole path. That is exactly the accumulation problem yesterday’s lesson solved, in a completely different setting, for a car’s odometer: turning a rate (there, velocity) into a total (there, distance traveled) by summing infinitely many infinitesimally thin slices, and showing, via the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, that doing so is the precise mathematical reverse of differentiation. Work is the identical operation, run on a different pair of quantities: force is the rate being accumulated, distance is what it is accumulated over, and the total is called work rather than distance.

W = ∫ F dx      (from xi to xf)

∫ … dx
the definite integral over the path, exactly as day eighteen defined it: the limit of a sum of ever-thinner slices of F multiplied by a tiny step dx, added up across the whole path from the starting position xi to the ending position xf
F
the force along the direction of motion, which may itself change from point to point along the path

Push this a step further and something genuinely useful falls out. Newton’s second law, from day ten of this curriculum, says the net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration, F = ma, and acceleration is itself the derivative of velocity with respect to time, a = dv/dt. And velocity, from the very first physics lesson this curriculum taught, is itself the rate at which position changes, so a tiny step in position, dx, equals v dt — velocity times a tiny step in time. Substitute both of those facts into the work integral above:

F dx = (m dv/dt)(v dt) = m v dv

The dt cancels cleanly, top and bottom, leaving an integral entirely in terms of velocity: W = ∫ m v dv, taken from the object’s initial speed vi to its final speed vf. And m v dv is precisely the kind of expression day eighteen’s reversed power rule was built to handle — the antiderivative of v is v²/2 — so the integral evaluates directly to a clean, closed formula:

Wnet = ΔKE = ½mvf2 − ½mvi2

Wnet
the total (net) work done on the object by every force acting on it, added together
ΔKE
the change in the object’s kinetic energy — final minus initial
m
the object’s mass, in kilograms
vi, vf
the object’s speed at the start and end of the interval in question

This is the work-energy theorem, and it is not a special case — it holds for any net force, constant or changing, friction included, exactly because it was derived directly from Newton’s second law and nothing else. The quantity ½mv² that appears on the right is kinetic energy, the formal name for the motion-energy account this lesson has been referring to informally: the energy an object carries by virtue of moving, proportional to its mass and, critically, to the square of its speed. That squared relationship is the entire mathematical content behind the opening puzzle — not a coincidence, not an approximation, but the direct, inevitable consequence of integrating v dv rather than just v.

Potential energy, and a currency that doesn’t care about the path

Some forces have a special property worth naming carefully. Lift a book from the floor to a shelf and the work gravity does on it — negative work, since gravity points down while the book moves up — depends only on the book’s starting height and ending height, never on the route it took to get there. Carry it straight up, or walk it in a slow spiral around the room on the way to the shelf, and gravity’s tally is identical either way, because gravity only cares about the net change in height, not the path traveled. A force with this property is called conservative, and it lets physics define a second kind of energy account, potential energy: a balance that depends only on an object’s position or configuration, not on its history. Friction is the standard example of the opposite kind, a non-conservative force: the heat it generates depends entirely on how far the surfaces actually slid against each other, so a longer, winding path genuinely costs more than a short direct one, in a way gravity’s toll never does.

Near Earth’s surface, gravitational potential energy takes an especially simple form:

PE = m g h

PE
gravitational potential energy, in joules
m
mass, in kilograms
g
the standard acceleration due to gravity near Earth’s surface, 9.8 m/s² (the internationally defined value is 9.80665 m/s², fixed by agreement in 1901, not separately re-measured)
h
height above whatever reference level is chosen as zero — the ground, a tabletop, sea level, whatever is convenient for the problem at hand

This formula is an approximation, good only for heights that are small compared to the size of the planet doing the pulling — a limitation the curriculum’s day-fifteen lesson on orbits brushed up against without naming it, since gravity technically weakens with distance and mgh silently assumes it does not. For anything from a dropped book to a jet airliner’s cruising altitude, the approximation is excellent; the exact form waits for a later physics lesson.

Combine the work-energy theorem with the definition of a conservative force’s work as the negative change in potential energy (Wconservative = −ΔPE, which is essentially what “path-independent” means in equation form), and something clean falls out. The net work is the sum of the conservative and non-conservative pieces: Wnet = Wconservative + Wnc = −ΔPE + Wnc. Set that equal to ΔKE, the work-energy theorem’s left-hand side, and rearrange:

ΔKE + ΔPE = Wnc      (KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf when Wnc = 0)

Wnc
work done by every non-conservative force present — friction, air resistance, an engine, a push from a hand
KEi + PEi, KEf + PEf
the object’s total mechanical energy — kinetic plus potential — at the start and end of the interval

When no non-conservative force does any work — no friction, no air resistance, no engine — the sum of kinetic and potential energy stays perfectly constant throughout the motion, even as the two individually trade off against each other continuously. This is conservation of mechanical energy, and it is the single most useful shortcut in introductory mechanics: it lets a problem be solved by comparing only the start and the end, without ever tracking what happened in between.

Power: the speed of the transaction

Formally, average power is simply work divided by the time it took:

P = W / t      P = F v  (constant force, along the direction of motion)

P
power, in watts (W); one watt is one joule per second
W
work done, in joules
t
the time interval over which that work was done, in seconds
F, v
for a constant force acting along the direction of travel, power simplifies to force times speed — itself just P = dW/dt, the instantaneous version, worked out with the same derivative machinery day eleven of this curriculum built for velocity itself

The watt is named for the Scottish engineer James Watt, and the story behind its far older cousin — horsepower — is worth telling in full, because it is a genuinely useful illustration of what power actually measures. In the early 1780s, Watt needed a way to sell his improved steam engines to customers who were still running horse-powered mills, breweries among them, and his existing royalty scheme (a cut of the coal a mine saved by switching from an older engine) meant nothing to a customer who had never burned coal at all. So he measured a mill horse directly: watching one turn a 24-foot mill wheel roughly 144 times an hour, and estimating the force it pushed with at around 180 pounds, he worked out that the horse was doing something on the order of 32,500 foot-pounds of work every minute — and rounded the figure up to a clean 33,000 foot-pounds per minute, which he published as the definition of one horsepower, around 1782. That number survives essentially unchanged today: 33,000 foot-pounds per minute converts to exactly 745.7 watts, the modern (mechanical) horsepower still stamped on car engines and lawnmowers alike.

It is a useful habit, when a number like 745.7 watts shows up, to hold it against a familiar reference: a resting human body, just running its basal metabolism with no exercise at all, gives off heat at a rate not far from 100 watts — close enough to an old-style incandescent bulb’s power draw that the coincidence gets used, only half-jokingly, to explain why a room full of people warms up noticeably faster than the light fixtures in it would suggest.

The figure is sometimes retold as a piece of marketing exaggeration — Watt supposedly padding the number so his engines would look more impressive by comparison — but the historical record does not support that story, and if anything points the opposite way. A horse can sustain something close to one full horsepower for a few hours at a stretch, but averaged across a full working day it manages closer to three-quarters of that, and can only reach several times Watt’s figure in short, hard bursts. Watt’s 33,000 foot-pounds per minute looks less like an inflated boast and more like a fair estimate of a horse working about as hard as a horse reasonably can — which is precisely why buying one of his steam engines, capable of running at that output hour after hour without ever tiring, was such an easy sell.

Where it shows up

This paper has spent the past several editions tracking a running argument about artificial intelligence that rarely gets stated in physical terms: whether OpenAI’s claimed Millennium Prize solution holds up, whether Congress is satisfied with Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s safety responses, why an Anthropic researcher resigned publicly warning that neither lab is moving responsibly — all of it sits on top of hardware that has to be built, cooled, and above all, powered, and the power side of that ledger is where this lesson’s distinction between energy and power stops being an abstraction and starts being the actual bottleneck reported on in the business pages.

Worked example · what an AI data center actually asks of the power grid

Nvidia’s current top-of-line data-center system, the GB200 NVL72, packs 72 of its AI chips into a single liquid-cooled rack. Industry integrator specifications put the whole rack’s power draw at roughly 120 kilowatts — a typical, order-of-magnitude figure that varies somewhat by installation, not a fixed constant. Compare that to a healthy, untrained adult’s sustainable power output, roughly 100 watts maintained for an hour or more (a fit cyclist can sustain two to four times that; brief maximal bursts can reach several hundred watts more still — 100 watts is the conservative, continuous baseline used here).

  1. One rack’s continuous draw against one person’s continuous output: 120,000 W ÷ 100 W = 1,200. A single rack of chips asks the power grid for roughly as much continuous power as twelve hundred people pedaling generators, all day, every day it runs.
  2. Scale up to a full campus. OpenAI, Oracle, and SoftBank’s Stargate data-center site in Abilene, Texas — widely reported through 2025 and 2026, with build-out figures still shifting as construction proceeds — has a stated target of roughly 1.2 gigawatts of capacity (1.2 × 109 W), against a few hundred megawatts already built and running as of this reporting.
  3. At the target figure, that campus alone represents 1.2 × 109 W ÷ 120,000 W per rack ≈ 10,000 racks’ worth of continuous draw — and 1.2 × 109 W ÷ 100 W ≈ 12 million people’s worth of continuous human power output, roughly the population of a large U.S. metro region, pedaling without rest, forever, just to match one data center’s electric bill.
  4. Converted into the unit this lesson just derived the history of: 1.2 × 109 W ÷ 745.7 W/hp ≈ 1.61 million horsepower — a single AI data-center campus, on paper, on the order of a million and a half of James Watt’s mill horses, running continuously, all pointed at training and running AI models rather than turning a mill wheel.

The reason these figures matter to the industry — and the reason grid capacity, not total electricity, keeps showing up as the actual constraint on how fast new AI data centers can come online — is exactly the friction-ceiling logic from the opening puzzle, transplanted onto a power line instead of a tire. Enough time and enough coal, gas, or uranium can eventually generate almost any total amount of energy. A transmission line, a transformer, or a substation cannot be made to deliver energy faster than its rated capacity, no matter how much total energy is sitting upstream waiting to flow through it. That is precisely why new AI campuses so often get built next to existing power plants, natural-gas fields, or their own dedicated generation rather than simply plugging into the nearest substation: the binding constraint is never really “is there enough energy in the world,” it is “can it be delivered at the rate this many racks demand, right now, continuously” — the same distinction between total energy and its rate of delivery that separates work from power in every equation above.

Worked example · a straight drop, no friction

Conservation of mechanical energy makes some problems almost embarrassingly short. Take an object released from rest at the top of a 60-meter drop — roughly the height of a twenty-story building, and in the range of a serious drop on a modern steel amusement-park coaster, treated here as an idealized, frictionless, illustrative case rather than a measurement of any specific ride. How fast is it moving at the bottom?

  1. With no friction or air resistance doing any work, mechanical energy is conserved: KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf.
  2. At the top, the object is at rest, so KEi = 0, and PEi = mgh. At the bottom, take h = 0, so PEf = 0, and all the energy has become kinetic: KEf = ½mv2.
  3. Setting the two sides equal: mgh = ½mv2. The mass cancels — the fourth time this curriculum has watched a mass cancel cleanly out of a motion problem, and for the same underlying reason each time: both sides of the equation scale with mass in exactly the same way.
  4. Solving for v: v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 60) = √1,176 ≈ 34.3 m/s — about 76.7 mph, or 123.5 km/h.
  5. A real coaster falls a little short of this frictionless ideal: rolling friction in the wheels and drag from the air quietly draw down the energy budget before the train reaches the bottom, which is exactly why engineers build in extra drop height, or an assisted launch, to cover the difference rather than relying on gravity alone to hit a target speed.
Worked example · the highway stop, in full

Return to the opening puzzle with actual numbers. A car brakes hard on dry pavement, tires gripping at a peak coefficient of friction μ = 0.7 — a commonly cited figure for a hard, ABS-assisted stop on dry asphalt, illustrative rather than a measurement of any specific car or tire. Using g = 9.8 m/s², compare the braking distance from 30 mph to the braking distance from 60 mph, once the brakes are already applied.

  1. Friction is the only horizontal force acting once the brakes lock (or, with ABS, hover at the edge of locking), and it opposes motion, doing negative work over the stopping distance d: Wfriction = −μmg · d.
  2. The work-energy theorem sets this equal to the change in kinetic energy. The car ends at rest, so KEf = 0: −μmg · d = 0 − ½mv2.
  3. Mass cancels again, leaving d = v2 / (2μg) — distance proportional to the square of the speed, exactly as the opening puzzle promised.
  4. At 30 mph (13.41 m/s): d = 13.412 / (2 × 0.7 × 9.8) = 179.9 / 13.72 ≈ 13.1 m, about 43 feet.
  5. At 60 mph (26.82 m/s): d = 26.822 / 13.72 = 719.4 / 13.72 ≈ 52.4 m, about 172 feet — almost exactly four times the 30 mph figure, not twice it, confirming the square-law relationship directly.
  6. One honest caveat: reaction distance (how far the car travels before the driver even touches the brake) is not part of this calculation, and it scales linearly with speed, not quadratically — so total stopping distance, reaction plus braking combined, grows a little less dramatically than a clean factor of four. At higher speeds the braking portion dominates the total, which is exactly why the square-law effect is the one that matters most in the range where it counts.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Picture a waiter holding a loaded tray at shoulder height, arm trembling after a few minutes, or a weightlifter holding a loaded bar motionless overhead in the middle of a lift. Both would say, correctly in everyday language, that they are working hard. By the strict physics definition just derived, both are doing exactly zero mechanical work on the object they are holding. If the tray or the bar does not move — zero displacement — then W = Fd cosθ is zero no matter how large F is, since d itself is zero. Carry that same tray at a perfectly constant height across a room and the same zero shows up a different way: the supporting force is vertical, the displacement is horizontal, the angle between them is 90°, and cos 90° = 0 makes the work vanish again, even though the waiter is now visibly moving and still just as tired.

Nothing about that conclusion is a trick or a technicality; it is exactly what the formula says, and the apparent paradox dissolves the moment the question “work on what?” gets asked properly. Physics’ definition of work tracks only the external mechanical energy transferred to the object being held or carried — and if that object’s kinetic and potential energy never change, no mechanical work was done on it, full stop. The waiter’s fatigue is a real, separate, internal biological process: individual muscle fibers cannot hold tension indefinitely without fatiguing and briefly relaxing, so the nervous system continuously recruits fresh fibers to take over, and every one of those contractions burns real chemical energy (drawn from the same ATP-based currency that powers essentially all animal movement), converting nearly all of it directly into metabolic heat inside the muscle rather than into any external mechanical work on the tray. The waiter is doing a great deal of biochemical work, in the loose sense, and burning real calories doing it — while doing zero mechanical work, in the strict sense, on the thing being held. Both statements are true. They are simply answers to two different questions, and everyday language, which has only one word for both, conflates them constantly.

A smaller, closely related slip is worth naming in the same breath: work that comes out negative is not “no work,” it is energy moving in the opposite direction, out of an account rather than into one. Braking does strongly negative work on a car — a very real, very large transaction, just one that drains the motion-energy account instead of filling it.

The callback

Yesterday’s lesson built a general machine for turning a rate into a total: an odometer, quietly running the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus every time it adds up a speedometer’s reading over the seconds of a drive, proving along the way that accumulation and differentiation are exact inverses of one another. Today’s entire formalism is that identical machine, pointed at a different pair of quantities. Instead of accumulating velocity over time to get distance, this lesson accumulated force over distance to get work — and it produced its single cleanest result, the work-energy theorem, for exactly the reason day eighteen would recognize immediately: a well-chosen substitution turned “add up force over distance” into “add up mv over velocity,” an integral that day eighteen’s reversed power rule dispatches in one line. Nothing new was invented today. The odometer’s trick simply got pointed at a different rate, and a theorem about energy fell out where a theorem about distance fell out yesterday.

Where this leaves you
  • You can state and derive the work-energy theorem from Newton’s second law and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and you can explain, in plain language, why kinetic energy runs on the square of speed rather than speed itself.
  • You can compute a braking distance, a fall from height, or the power a moving force delivers, and you know when mechanical energy is conserved and when non-conservative forces like friction take a cut along the way.
  • You can tell the difference between total energy and the rate at which it moves — and you have seen that same rate-ceiling logic explain both a car’s stopping distance and why AI data centers get built next to power plants rather than ordinary substations.
  • You know exactly why holding something heavy and motionless does zero physics work even though it is exhausting, and why that is not a contradiction once “work on what?” gets asked properly.
  • What this sets up: momentum and collisions, later this same week in the physics track, introduce a second ledger that behaves differently from energy’s — one that never leaks the way kinetic energy can leak into heat and sound during a crash, even when energy itself very visibly does.
