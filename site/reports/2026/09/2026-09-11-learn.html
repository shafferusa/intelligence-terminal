Why Water Is Cheap and Diamonds Are Not

Friday, September 11, 2026 · 26 min read

Adam Smith noticed that water is priceless yet nearly free while diamonds are nearly useless yet dear, and could not explain why — a trio of economists working independently in the early 1870s found the fix hiding in a single idea, that value lives at the margin rather than in the whole, and today’s lesson follows that idea from a shopper’s coffee-and-pastry budget through a firm’s shutdown decision to this week’s own $103 oil headlines and the reason a factory should never let a sunk cost decide its next move.

Economics

Day 20 of 150 · Economics 3 of 20 · Foundations

The paradox that waited a hundred years

Picture two glasses on a table. One holds ordinary tap water — the single substance every cell in your body needs to keep functioning, arguably the most useful thing that exists. The other holds nothing but a half-carat diamond, a compressed lump of carbon whose main talents are bending light attractively and scratching glass. Offer almost anyone the choice between owning one or the other, free and clear, and the diamond wins by a landslide, even though a person deprived of the water would be dead within a week and a person deprived of the diamond would be exactly as healthy as before. Something in that answer ought to feel wrong. For about a hundred years, the best economists alive did not have a rigorous way to explain why it isn’t.

Adam Smith named the puzzle plainly in 1776, in the book that founded economics as a discipline: “Nothing is more useful than water: but it will purchase scarce any thing… A diamond, on the contrary, has scarce any use-value; but a very great quantity of other goods may frequently be had in exchange for it.” Smith noticed the contradiction, gave it a name that stuck — the diamond–water paradox — and then, by his own admission, could not resolve it. He spent the rest of The Wealth of Nations building a theory of value anchored mostly in labor: a thing is worth what it costs, in effort, to produce. That theory explains a great deal. It does not explain why water, essential to life and often nearly free to produce, sells for less than a stone whose only function is decoration.

The theory that Smith reached for, and the labor-cost theories that followed it through David Ricardo and Karl Marx, all shared one quiet assumption: that value is a property of the thing itself, fixed by how much went into making it, the same for everyone, all the time. Water is essential, therefore water should be precious. Diamonds are frivolous, therefore diamonds should be cheap. The theory kept producing that prediction and the market kept refusing to honor it, for a century, because the assumption underneath the theory was wrong in a specific and fixable way — and finding the fix took until three economists, working in three different countries, arrived at almost the same answer within three years of each other, without having read one another’s work.

Value lives at the edge, not in the whole

The fix, once seen, looks almost too simple to have taken a century: nobody ever chooses between all the water in the world and all the diamonds in the world. Every real choice is about one more unit, weighed against the units you already have. You already have, in effect, unlimited access to water — a tap, a well, a river, a rainstorm — so the next glass of water adds almost nothing to your life that the glass before it hadn’t already supplied. You have zero diamonds, so the next diamond would be the first thing of its kind you own, and it adds a great deal. Total usefulness and next-unit usefulness are entirely different numbers, and price tracks the second one, not the first.

This is marginal thinking, and “marginal” here is not doing its everyday-English job of meaning “small” or “unimportant.” It means, specifically and only, the next one — the next unit consumed, the next unit produced, the next dollar spent, the next hour worked. A margin is an edge, and marginal thinking is the discipline of always asking what happens at the edge of a decision rather than averaging over the whole of it. Three economists reached for exactly that idea, independently, in the early 1870s: William Stanley Jevons in England, publishing The Theory of Political Economy in 1871; Carl Menger in Austria, publishing his Principles of Economics the same year; and Léon Walras in Switzerland, publishing his Elements of Pure Economics in 1874. None of the three had read the others’ work before publishing his own, and each arrived at the idea from a different direction. Jevons, in Manchester, came to it from utilitarian philosophy, treating economics as an offshoot of Jeremy Bentham’s felicific calculus — the notion that pleasure and pain could, in principle, be measured and maximized like any other quantity. Menger, in Vienna, arrived from a more institutional tradition and founded what became the Austrian School on the back of his 1871 book, emphasizing that value is a judgment an individual makes about a good’s usefulness to their own particular purposes rather than a property built into the good itself. Walras, in Lausanne, was the most mathematically ambitious of the three, using marginal utility as one building block inside a far larger project: a complete system of equations describing how every market in an economy could, in principle, settle into a mutually consistent set of prices all at once — general equilibrium theory, a research program still running today. Historians of economic thought call the resulting shift, from a cost-of-production theory of value to a next-unit theory of value, the Marginal Revolution, and it is one of the few moments in the history of any field where the same correct idea occurred to multiple people who could not have copied one another — three different starting points converging on an identical answer within three years, which is usually good evidence that an idea was correct and simply waiting for the right question to be asked of it.

The same next-unit logic runs both sides of a market, and today’s lesson teaches it on both sides in turn. A consumer decides how much of something to buy by asking what the next unit is worth to them, compared to what they already have and what else that money could buy instead. A producer — a firm — decides how much to make by asking what the next unit costs to produce, compared to what it can be sold for. Neither decision is made by looking at totals. A firm that has already spent a fortune building a factory does not ask “is this factory, on average, worth what we paid for it” when deciding whether to run one more shift; it asks whether that one more shift, by itself, brings in more than it costs. A shopper standing at a coffee stand does not ask “is coffee, in general, worth having” before buying a second cup; they ask whether this cup, the one they’re about to buy, is worth what it costs given that they already have the first one in hand. Get comfortable with that reflex — always the next unit, never the whole stock — and the rest of today’s lesson is mostly bookkeeping.

The same edge-of-the-decision logic explains why a restaurant owner deciding whether to squeeze one more table into a busy Friday night thinks in almost exactly the terms a diner uses deciding whether to order dessert. A restaurant running at half capacity can seat one more table for almost nothing extra; a restaurant already spilling into the parking lot pays a much higher marginal cost — slower service, frustrated existing customers, a kitchen pushed past its comfortable pace — to seat that identical one more table. Neither the owner nor the diner needs to know the restaurant’s total revenue for the year, or the diner’s lifetime consumption of dessert, to make a good decision. Both need to know only what the very next unit, right now, given everything already committed, is actually worth.

The consumer’s arithmetic

Economists give the satisfaction a person gets from consuming something a deliberately abstract name: utility. It is not money, and the early marginalists were careful (Jevons more than Menger) to treat it as a genuine, if unmeasurable, quantity of felt satisfaction, typically counted in an imaginary unit economists call “utils” purely for teaching purposes — nobody claims utils are real or comparable between two different people. What matters for today’s lesson is not the unit but the shape of the curve: as a person consumes more and more of a single good in a given stretch of time, each additional unit typically adds less extra satisfaction than the one before it. Economists call this the law of diminishing marginal utility, and nearly everyone has felt it directly: the first slice of pizza at a party is wonderful, the fourth is fine, the eighth is a mistake you regret.

MUx = ΔTUx / Δx

MUx
the marginal utility of good x — the extra total utility gained from consuming one more unit of x, holding consumption of everything else fixed
ΔTUx
the change in total utility caused by that one extra unit
Δx
the change in the quantity of x consumed — one additional unit, in the examples this lesson uses

One honest caveat belongs here before going further: this lesson treats utility as though it were a measurable quantity with real numbers attached to it, the way Jevons originally proposed. Later economists — chiefly Vilfredo Pareto around the turn of the twentieth century, and more rigorously John Hicks and R. G. D. Allen in the 1930s — showed that almost everything consumer theory needs can be derived from a weaker assumption: that a person can only rank baskets of goods as better, worse, or equally good, never actually measure by how much better. That refinement is called ordinal utility, and it is more defensible philosophically, but it needs indifference curves and a heavier mathematical apparatus to state cleanly. The cardinal, utils-counting version used here gets every conclusion this lesson actually needs exactly right — the equimarginal principle survives the switch to ordinal utility completely intact — while staying close enough to plain arithmetic to work through by hand, which is why it is still how the idea gets taught first.

Written with continuous quantities and a smooth utility function U(x), MUx is simply the derivative dU/dx — exactly the object day eleven of this curriculum defined for the first time, now pointed at satisfaction instead of distance or velocity. Diminishing marginal utility is the statement that this derivative itself keeps shrinking as x grows: MUx is a decreasing function of x, or equivalently d²U/dx² < 0. Nothing about that shape is a law of physics — it is an empirical regularity about how people actually value things, strong enough to build a theory on, but not something that could be derived from first principles the way Newton’s laws can.

A consumer with a fixed amount of money to spend and more than one good to choose between faces a real constraint, and the marginal framework turns that constraint into a rule. Suppose a dollar spent on good x buys MUx/Px worth of extra satisfaction — the marginal utility per dollar, dividing what the next unit is worth by what it costs. If that ratio is higher for x than for y, the next dollar is better spent on x; spend it there, and because of diminishing marginal utility, x’s ratio falls a little with each unit bought, while y’s ratio stays wherever it was. A rational shopper, spending one dollar at a time, keeps chasing whichever good currently offers the best return per dollar — which means that at whatever point the money runs out, the marginal utility per dollar of every good actually being bought has been driven to the same level. Economists call this the equimarginal principle, and it is the entire content of consumer theory compressed into one sentence: spend your next dollar wherever it buys the most, and keep doing that until the money is gone.

MUx / Px = MUy / Py      subject to  Pxx + Pyy = I

MUx, MUy
the marginal utility of the last unit purchased of each good
Px, Py
the price per unit of each good
Pxx + Pyy = I
the budget constraint — total spending on both goods equals income I, exactly, with none left unspent and none borrowed

This is a constrained optimization problem in exactly the sense day sixteen of this curriculum used that phrase: maximize one quantity (total utility) subject to a limit on another (the budget). The equimarginal condition is what that day’s reasoning produces when it is pointed at a consumer’s shopping list, and the callback at the end of this lesson makes that connection explicit rather than leaving it implied.

Worked example · rationing a twelve-dollar coffee-and-pastry budget

A coffee shop regular has exactly $12 to spend this morning, coffee costs $2 a cup, and a pastry costs $3 each. Diminishing marginal utility means each successive cup or pastry is worth less to her than the one before it; suppose her honestly-reported marginal utilities, in utils, for successive units are:

Coffee (P = $2): 20, 16, 12, 8, 4      Pastry (P = $3): 27, 21, 15, 9, 3

  1. Convert every unit into marginal utility per dollar by dividing each figure by its price. Coffee: 10, 8, 6, 4, 2. Pastry: 9, 7, 5, 3, 1.
  2. Rank every unit across both goods by that per-dollar figure, and buy in descending order until the $12 runs out: coffee’s 1st unit (10, running total $2), pastry’s 1st unit (9, total $5), coffee’s 2nd unit (8, total $7), pastry’s 2nd unit (7, total $10), coffee’s 3rd unit (6, total $12). The budget is now exactly exhausted.
  3. The resulting basket is 3 coffees and 2 pastries, costing 3 × $2 + 2 × $3 = $6 + $6 = $12 — every cent accounted for. Total utility gained: coffee contributes 20 + 16 + 12 = 48 utils; pastry contributes 27 + 21 = 48 utils; the basket delivers 96 utils in total.
  4. Notice that the marginal utility per dollar is not perfectly equal at the stopping point — the last coffee bought sits at 6, the last pastry at 7 — because coffee and pastries can only be bought in whole units. The equimarginal principle holds exactly only when quantities can vary continuously; with lumpy, indivisible goods, the best a shopper can do is get as close to equal as the available units allow, spending down to the last dollar without ever spending on a unit that would have paid off less than one she skipped.
  5. Compare that outcome to an equally affordable but poorly chosen basket: four pastries and no coffee, also exactly $12. Total utility there is only 27 + 21 + 15 + 9 = 72 utils — a full 24 utils worse than the equimarginal basket, for the identical $12, because that basket kept buying pastries well past the point where coffee was the better next dollar.

The firm’s arithmetic

A firm runs the identical logic, translated into costs instead of satisfaction. The relevant question when deciding how much to produce is never “what did the whole operation cost, on average, per unit” — it is “what would one more unit cost, right now, given everything already in place.” Economists call that figure marginal cost, and in the short run, when a firm has already built its factory, signed its leases and can’t change any of that quickly, marginal cost typically rises as output rises — the mirror image of a consumer’s diminishing marginal utility. A fixed factory can only be pushed so hard: workers start tripping over each other, machines get run past their efficient pace, and each additional unit of output squeezes a little more expensively out of a workforce and equipment base that isn’t growing to match.

MC = ΔTC / Δq

MC
marginal cost — the extra total cost incurred by producing one more unit of output
ΔTC
the change in total cost caused by that one extra unit, including every input the firm has to pay for — labor, materials, power, anything variable with output
Δq
the change in quantity produced — one additional unit, in the examples this lesson uses

With a smooth, continuous cost function TC(q), marginal cost is dTC/dq — the same derivative operation as marginal utility, now run on a cost curve instead of a satisfaction curve. A firm selling into a competitive market, where it is small enough that its own output can’t move the price, receives the market price P for every unit it sells; each additional unit sold therefore brings in exactly P in extra revenue, so marginal revenue MR = P, a constant that does not depend on how much the firm has already produced. Profit is simply revenue minus cost, π(q) = P·q − TC(q), and finding the quantity that maximizes it is, once again, exactly the calculus problem day sixteen taught: take the derivative of π with respect to q, set it to zero, and solve.

dπ/dq = P − dTC/dq = 0     ⇒     MR = MC

π(q)
profit as a function of quantity produced, π(q) = P·q − TC(q)
MR
marginal revenue — the extra revenue from selling one more unit; equal to price P for a firm that cannot influence the market price on its own
MC
marginal cost, as defined above — here written as the derivative dTC/dq for a smoothly varying cost function

The rule that falls out — keep producing exactly as long as the next unit brings in at least as much revenue as it costs, and stop the moment it doesn’t — is the producer-side twin of the equimarginal principle. A consumer keeps spending on whichever good pays off best per dollar; a firm keeps producing whichever unit still pays off at all, and stops precisely where the next one wouldn’t.

One more piece completes the short-run picture. The MR = MC rule finds the best quantity to produce if producing at all is worthwhile — but it says nothing about whether the firm should be operating this period in the first place. Fixed costs, as the misconception section below explains in full, are already spent and unavoidable in the short run whether the firm makes one unit or a thousand; the only costs a firm can actually escape by shutting down entirely are its variable costs, the ones that scale with output. That gives a second rule sitting underneath the first:

Shut down if P < AVC(q)      where AVC(q) = VC(q) / q

AVC(q)
average variable cost at quantity q — total variable cost divided by quantity produced; excludes fixed costs entirely
VC(q)
total variable cost at quantity q — the portion of total cost that disappears entirely if the firm produces nothing at all
P
the market price, unchanged from its role earlier in this section

The reasoning is the sunk-cost logic this lesson keeps returning to, applied one level up. If price at least covers the variable cost of production, every unit sold recovers its own variable cost and contributes something — however small — toward the fixed cost that has to be paid regardless of whether the firm produces at all. Operating at a loss can still be the profit-maximizing choice, in the narrow sense of the smallest available loss, precisely because the fixed cost is gone either way, and the alternative — shutting down — guarantees losing every bit of it with nothing coming back in return. If price falls below average variable cost, though, each unit sold loses money even before touching the fixed cost at all, and shutting down, which caps the loss at the fixed cost alone, beats staying open and piling variable losses on top of it.

Worked example · the unit that decides whether to run one more shift

A small manufacturer sells into a competitive market at a fixed price of $50 per unit and has already committed $40 in fixed costs this period — a lease payment and equipment that has to be paid regardless of how much gets made. Its short-run marginal cost rises with each additional unit, as the fixed factory gets pushed harder: the cost of the 1st unit is $30, the 2nd $32, the 3rd $36, the 4th $42, the 5th $50, the 6th $60, the 7th $72.

  1. Build total cost by adding fixed cost to the running sum of marginal costs: TC(0) = $40 (fixed cost alone, nothing produced); TC(1) = $70; TC(2) = $102; TC(3) = $138; TC(4) = $180; TC(5) = $230; TC(6) = $290; TC(7) = $362.
  2. Compare each unit’s marginal cost to the $50 marginal revenue. Units 1 through 4 all cost less than $50 to make, so each one adds to profit. The 5th unit costs exactly $50 — a wash, adding zero extra profit, but not a loss either. The 6th unit costs $60, more than it sells for; making it would actively destroy profit, and the 7th, at $72, is worse still.
  3. Compute profit directly at each quantity, π(q) = 50q − TC(q), to confirm: π(3) = $150 − $138 = $12. π(4) = $200 − $180 = $20. π(5) = $250 − $230 = $20. π(6) = $300 − $290 = $10.
  4. Profit rises from q = 1 through q = 4, holds exactly flat from q = 4 to q = 5 (because the 5th unit’s marginal cost exactly equals marginal revenue — zero marginal profit on that one unit), and falls from q = 5 onward. The firm should produce 5 units — the largest quantity at which no unit made a loss — for a maximum profit of $20.
  5. Notice what never entered the calculation: the $40 fixed cost. It shows up once, identically, in every single row of total cost, and it cancels out of every marginal-cost comparison and every profit difference between adjacent quantities. It absolutely affects whether the firm is profitable overall — profit is $20, not $60, because $40 had to be covered first — but it plays no role whatsoever in deciding how many units to make. That fact is the entire subject of this lesson’s misconception section below.

Apply the shutdown rule to the same manufacturer as a final check. At its profit-maximizing quantity of 5 units, total cost was $230, of which $40 was fixed, leaving $190 in variable cost — an average variable cost of $190 ÷ 5 = $38 per unit. The $50 market price clears that $38 easily, so shutting down was never a live question for this firm this period; producing was solidly worth doing, not merely the best of two bad options. The shutdown rule only starts to bind when price falls close to, or below, that $38 average-variable-cost figure — a very different number from the $362 ÷ 7 ≈ $52 average total cost per unit at full output, which folds in the sunk $40 and is therefore, per the reasoning above, the wrong number to be checking price against at all.

Where it shows up

This paper’s market editions have spent the past several days tracking oil climb past $100 and then past $103 a barrel, as Iran’s threats against shipping through the Strait of Hormuz deepened (2026-09-10 pm). Producer theory is not a side note to that story; it is the reason the story has two very different casts of characters reacting to the identical price on the identical morning.

The break-even question “is $103 a profitable price to sell oil at” does not have one answer, because different producers face wildly different marginal costs of getting the next barrel out of the ground. Saudi Arabia’s giant, decades-old onshore fields, Ghawar chief among them, lift oil at an estimated marginal cost on the order of $3 to $5 a barrel — the wells are old, the geology is unusually forgiving, and the infrastructure has been paid for many times over. A shale operator in the Permian Basin, by contrast, drilling a new well into rock that has to be fractured to release the oil at all, needs roughly $56 to $66 a barrel just to break even on that new well, according to the Dallas Fed’s regular survey of the industry. At $103 a barrel, both producers are comfortably above their marginal cost and both are motivated to pump more — but the Saudi producer is earning something close to $100 of margin on every additional barrel, while the shale producer is earning closer to $40 to $47, a genuinely different incentive even though both firms read the same headline price.

One careful distinction is worth making explicitly, because glossing over it is exactly the kind of imprecision this lesson is trying to train out of you: Saudi Arabia’s lifting cost — the marginal cost of pulling one more barrel out of an existing well — is not the same number as its fiscal break-even price, the oil price the Saudi government needs, across its entire budget, to avoid running a deficit, which the IMF has put above $90 a barrel in recent years. Those are two entirely different calculations answering two entirely different questions. The lifting cost is the marginal-cost figure this lesson has been building toward, and it is what actually governs the short-run decision of whether to open the valve on one more barrel. The fiscal break-even price is a statement about an entire national budget, built from taxes, subsidies and spending commitments that have nothing to do with what it costs, at the margin, to extract oil. A firm’s producer-theory decision runs on the first number. A finance ministry’s budget runs on the second. Reporting that conflates them — and plenty does — ends up implying Saudi Arabia is losing money below $90 oil, when in producer-theory terms it is earning an enormous margin on every barrel all the way down to single digits.

The consumer side of the identical headline is just as instructive, and gasoline happens to be one of the most thoroughly measured examples of price elasticity — marginal utility’s close cousin — in the entire economics literature. Estimates of how much gasoline consumption falls in the short run, weeks to a few months, when price rises vary considerably by era and method: research using 1975–1980 data found a short-run price elasticity of roughly −0.21 to −0.34, meaning a 10% price increase cut consumption by roughly 2 to 3.4%, while a widely cited NBER study using 2001–2006 data found the response had shrunk to just −0.034 to −0.077, about a tenth as large; more recent work using state-level fuel-tax variation has argued the true figure sits closer to −0.37, nearer the older estimates. Economists genuinely disagree on the precise number and on why it may have shifted, but virtually every reputable estimate agrees on the underlying shape: in the short run, gasoline demand barely responds to price at all, in either direction. That is marginal utility working exactly as this lesson described it. A commuter’s next gallon of gas is worth roughly what it was worth yesterday, because the trip to work still has to happen whether oil is $80 or $103 a barrel — the marginal utility of that gallon stays high and doesn’t fall much just because the price did. Only over the longer run, as people trade in vehicles, move closer to work, or shift to transit, does the marginal utility of the next gallon actually start to fall the way this lesson’s coffee-and-pastry example assumed it would within a single morning.

The mistake almost everyone makes

The worked example above buried a small, deliberate trap in step five: the $40 fixed cost never once entered the decision of how many units to produce, even though it was very real money the firm had genuinely spent. Ask most people, including most people running small businesses, whether a project that has already absorbed a large investment should be more likely to continue, and a great many will say yes — we’ve already put so much into this, it would be wasteful to stop now. That instinct has a name, and it is wrong.

It is called the sunk cost fallacy: treating money, time or effort that has already been spent and cannot be recovered as though it were still a live input to a forward-looking decision. A cost is sunk the instant it becomes unrecoverable regardless of what happens next — the $40 in the worked example was owed whether the firm made zero units or a thousand, so it could not possibly change which quantity, going forward, maximized profit. The only costs that belong in a marginal decision are the ones that change depending on what you choose next: exactly the marginal costs this lesson has been computing all along. A rational decision-maker, at every moment, should ask only “given where I stand right now, what does the next unit cost, and what is it worth” — and should never let the size of what has already been spent tip that comparison one way or the other.

The clearest illustration of the fallacy in the wild carries its own nickname. Anglo-French governments kept funding the Concorde supersonic airliner well past the point where its own internal cost projections showed it would never turn a profit, reportedly in part because canceling a project that had already consumed so much public money felt politically unthinkable. The biologist Richard Dawkins and his student Tamsin Carlisle named the pattern the “Concorde fallacy” in a 1976 paper in Nature, arguing that the identical mistake shows up throughout the animal kingdom — an animal that has already invested heavily in a fight or a mate is more likely to keep investing, independent of its actual odds of winning going forward. The psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer gave the human version its full empirical grounding almost a decade later, in a 1985 paper called “The Psychology of Sunk Cost,” running experiments that showed people reliably throwing good money after bad specifically because they had already spent some, holding every forward-looking fact about the decision constant. The fallacy shows up at every scale from a factory deciding whether to keep an unprofitable product line alive because of what the tooling cost, down to sitting through a bad movie because the ticket is already paid for — the ticket money is gone either way, and the only real choice left is whether the next two hours are worth more spent watching or spent doing something else.

A related idea is easy to conflate with sunk cost, and worth separating cleanly. Opportunity cost — what a decision-maker gives up by choosing one option over the next-best alternative — belongs in every marginal decision; sunk cost never does. The manufacturer in this lesson’s worked example should absolutely weigh what its workers and machines could otherwise be doing before committing them to a fifth unit; that comparison is a live, forward-looking opportunity cost, exactly the kind of thing marginal analysis is built to handle. What it should never weigh is the $40 already spent on the lease, because no choice available to the firm today can get that money back or make it any larger. The fallacy is not caring about the future too little — it is letting a number from the past masquerade as though it still belonged to the future.

The callback

Day sixteen of this curriculum taught optimization: given a function, find the input that makes it as large (or as small) as possible, by taking a derivative, setting it to zero, and solving. That lesson used a fence enclosing the largest possible area and a delivery route minimizing total distance as its worked examples — problems that look, on the surface, like nothing in today’s lesson about coffee, pastries and a manufacturer’s shift schedule. They are the identical machine. A consumer maximizing utility subject to a budget and a firm maximizing profit subject to a cost structure are both, formally, optimization problems of exactly the kind day sixteen solved: take the derivative of the thing being maximized, set it to zero, and read off the condition that falls out. Point that same derivative-equals-zero machinery at a consumer’s utility function and it produces the equimarginal principle, MUx/Px = MUy/Py. Point it at a firm’s profit function and it produces MR = MC. Neither result required a single new mathematical idea beyond what day sixteen already built; economics, in both halves of today’s lesson, is calculus wearing a different name tag.

Where this leaves you
  • You can state the difference between total utility and marginal utility, explain why the diamond–water paradox stumped economists for a century, and say in one sentence what the Marginal Revolution of the 1870s actually changed.
  • You can apply the equimarginal principle to allocate a fixed budget across two goods, and the MR = MC rule to find a competitive firm’s profit-maximizing output, including working through the arithmetic by hand.
  • You can explain why fixed and sunk costs never belong in a marginal decision, name the sunk cost fallacy on sight, and recognize the same mistake whether it shows up in a factory’s product line or a bad night at the movies.
  • You can read a headline like “oil settles above $103” and immediately ask the producer-theory question underneath it — profitable for whom, at what marginal cost — rather than treating one price as though it means the same thing to every seller.
  • What this sets up: later economics lessons build directly on today’s two curves, adding market structure — monopoly, oligopoly, and what changes when a firm can no longer treat price as fixed the way today’s competitive firm could.
