Top Stories

September CPI lands at 8:30 into a bond market already at 5.2%: the Fed’s last look at inflation before it decides whether to hike again

Gasoline should push the headline number up sharply. The core rate is the number the Fed, and the 10-year, will trade on.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the September Consumer Price Index at 8:30, with its real-earnings report alongside (BLS release calendar). Consensus compiled by Newsquawk, CNBC and Dow Jones is for prices to rise 0.6% on the month, up from 0.4% in August. That would lift the annual rate to about 3.6% from 3.4%; some calendars have 3.7%. Core CPI, which excludes food and energy, is expected at 0.2% after 0.3%, with the annual core rate forecast at 2.4% or 2.5%, depending on the survey. One forecaster, Morningstar, has the headline at 0.5%.

This is mostly an oil story. With Brent above $100 for most of the past month on the war in the Gulf, energy is expected to add most of the monthly rise. The Fed raised rates in September to 3.75–4.00%, its first increase since 2023 and the first under Chair Kevin Warsh. Minutes released Oct. 7 said “most participants” saw another increase as likely appropriate by year-end. Then September payrolls rose only 29,000 and unemployment ticked up to 4.2%. Futures-implied odds of a hike on Oct. 28 have fallen to under 20% on the CME’s FedWatch, down from about 70% before the jobs report, Newsquawk reports. The latest official index on FRED is August’s, at 334.131, up 0.40% on the month; core rose 0.29%.

The bond market goes in with yields near their highest in more than two decades. Tuesday’s official curve had the 2-year at 4.79%, the 10-year at 5.21% and the 30-year at 5.56% (Treasury). Most of the 10-year’s level is real yield: the 10-year TIPS yield was 2.91% on Oct. 9 and the 10-year breakeven 2.33% on Oct. 13 (FRED). Markets expect inflation to come back down; what has gone up is the return investors demand for lending long.

Why it matters A 0.6% headline with a 0.2% core is the soft outcome: an oil shock that has not yet spread into other prices. A core of 0.3% or more would revive the hike case and put pressure on the long end. Exposure, not direction: two-year Treasuries and rate-sensitive groups (regional banks KRE, homebuilders, utilities XLU, long bonds TLT); the dollar; gold. The Social Security Administration sets the 2027 cost-of-living adjustment from the third-quarter CPI-W, so the number also sets the raise for about 70 million beneficiaries. Projections run from 3.4% (independent analyst Mary Johnson) to 3.6% (the Senior Citizens League), against 2.8% for 2026.
What’s next The Beige Book at 2:00 today; producer prices, retail sales and jobless claims at 8:30 Thursday; import prices Friday. A preview lists Chair Warsh speaking at 11:30 on Thursday; we have not confirmed that time.

ASML beats on profit, raises its outlook for the third time and warns it may not be able to meet demand in 2027–28

This was the first big read on AI hardware since the memory selloff began. The equipment maker says the build-out is speeding up, not slowing.

The Dutch chip-equipment maker reported third-quarter sales of about €11.7 billion, up about 55% from a year earlier, and net income of about €5.0 billion, with a 57.5% gross margin (company release via GlobeNewswire; Investing.com, Euronews). Analysts surveyed by Investing.com had expected net income of €4.13 billion on sales of €11.63 billion: a clear profit beat, with revenue roughly in line. Yahoo Finance notes that in dollars revenue was slightly below forecasts. The company sold 113 lithography systems, up from 86 in the second quarter. It guided fourth-quarter sales to €14.2–15.2 billion and lifted its 2026 range to €44–45 billion.

Chief executive Christophe Fouquet said order momentum keeps rising each quarter. He said the supply planned for 2027–28 may not fully meet demand, and that ASML is studying how to add capacity beyond the 30% increase it has already announced. ASML shares were up about 1.8–2.7% in Amsterdam in early trade (Reuters via Business Recorder and MarketScreener). The American shares were indicated about 1.5% higher before the open (Benzinga), after closing Tuesday at $1,717.76, down 1.9%. Mixed early read A few outlets described the stock falling on a revenue “miss” and the supply warning, and the AP says Tokyo chip-equipment names turned lower after the release. The early reaction depends on which snapshot you read.

Why it matters The question since Monday has been whether the memory rout means AI spending is slowing. Seoul is down about 13% in six sessions, and Micron fell 3.6% on Tuesday. ASML’s answer is the opposite: customers want more machines than it can make. That does not settle the memory-pricing worry, which is about Chinese competition from CXMT, not demand. Exposure, not direction: ASML, the equipment group (AMAT, LRCX, KLAC, Tokyo Electron), SMH, and TSMC, which reports Thursday.

Bank of America reports a day after a record $1.2 billion money-laundering penalty, with Morgan Stanley and BlackRock close behind

The bank admitted willful Bank Secrecy Act violations. The penalty is expected to come straight out of third-quarter earnings.

On Tuesday the Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network fined Bank of America $1.2 billion. The penalty credits a $750 million fine from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the largest in that agency’s history. A separate $11.6 million sanctions settlement with the Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control brings the total above $1.21 billion (Treasury press release; OCC release NR 2026-89). Treasury calls it the third-largest FinCEN penalty, after Binance ($3.4 billion, 2023) and TD Bank ($1.3 billion, 2024). Regulators say the bank failed to report more than $5 billion of suspicious activity, some of it possibly linked to Chinese money-laundering networks, narcotics and fraud. The OCC first flagged the problems in 2023 and issued a cease-and-desist order in December 2024. The bank admitted willful violations.

The bank said it expects a $1.2 billion charge in the third quarter that is not tax-deductible. CNBC reports the charge will cut earnings by about 17 cents a share. Results are due at about 6:45, with a call at 8:30; they had not been published when this edition went to press. LSEG’s consensus is about $1.10 a share; StreetAccount’s for net interest income is about $16.53 billion. The bank has guided full-year net interest income growth to the top of a 6–8% range, and has said investment-banking fees will be down at least 10% from a year earlier. Analysts will need to separate reported from adjusted figures. Morgan Stanley follows at about 7:30, with consensus estimates between $2.83 and $3.12 a share depending on the survey. BlackRock (about $14.04, Zacks), Progressive and State Street also report this morning.

Why it matters Tuesday showed the market is judging banks on what drives profits, not on the headline beat. Goldman rose on a big beat driven by equities trading; Citigroup fell 5.6% despite beating. Lenders with the most consumer and loan exposure fared worse: Wells Fargo fell 3.1% and the regional-bank fund KRE 0.7%, with no single confirmed catalyst. Bank of America is the largest consumer deposit franchise to report, so its card losses and deposit costs matter more than the one-off fine.

Iranian officials say shipping attacks will intensify before the US election, and a Houthi missile hits an Aramco office in Dhahran

Brent is back above $106. The market is still treating attacks on ships as a cost of shipping, not lost barrels.

Reuters, citing two senior Iranian officials it did not name, reports that Tehran is stepping up attacks on shipping to keep oil prices high before the Nov. 3 US midterms. One official said there would be “complete closure soon.” A US military official confirmed to Reuters that the Revolutionary Guard is now using unarmed spotter boats after US strikes destroyed much of its radar. Reuters counts at least five incidents in 48 hours, now reaching waters off Qatar and the UAE. The US-led Joint Maritime Information Center rates the threat “severe,” its second-highest level. The attacks themselves are confirmed by the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency and several outlets. Iran’s stated intent rests on unnamed officials.

On land, a Houthi missile struck Saudi Aramco’s administrative headquarters building in Dhahran on Tuesday (Bloomberg, The National, Al Jazeera). The Saudi energy ministry reported a “limited fire” and no casualties; Fox News described damage to several floors. Aramco had not commented. It is the first reported Houthi hit on an Aramco site in this round, though it was an office, not an export or processing plant. The Saudi-led coalition intercepted more missiles aimed at Riyadh and struck Houthi targets in Yemen. The president, on Truth Social, blamed Iran and suggested the attacks aim to lift oil prices before the election.

Brent for December delivery was $106.40 at about 6:24, up 0.7% from Tuesday’s $105.69 settlement, and WTI $95.22, up 1.1% (Yahoo Finance, delayed).

Why it matters Every strike so far has hit ships, airports and now an office building, and none a Gulf export terminal. That is why oil trades around $105 and not $130, the IMF’s adverse case. Iranian officials naming a political deadline adds a calendar to the risk. Gasoline is also the biggest single input to today’s CPI.

China’s exports jump 36% in September, the fastest since February, and chip exports more than double

The trade surplus widened to $133.5 billion. Exports to the US rose almost 40% as the tariff truce takes effect.

China’s customs data, released this morning, show exports up 36.3% from a year earlier, against 25.5% expected in a Reuters poll. Imports rose 31%, and the surplus was $133.5 billion, up from $119.1 billion in August; the surplus so far this year is $938 billion (CNBC). Chip exports rose 108.1%. Shipments to the US rose 39.6% to $47.88 billion. Last month Washington and Beijing announced plans to roll back tariffs on $30 billion of each other’s goods. Shanghai stocks still fell 0.9% today, and Hong Kong 0.1%.

Why it matters Two things for markets. First, cheap Chinese goods arriving in volume push against US goods inflation just as energy pushes the other way. Second, a 108% jump in chip exports fits the worry behind the memory selloff: Chinese chipmakers are taking share at the lower end.

Drones sink a cargo ship off Bulgaria; 11 crew missing, and Sofia summons Russia’s ambassador

It is the second deadly attack on Black Sea shipping off NATO coasts in eight days. Nobody has claimed it.

Drones sank the Guinea-Bissau-flagged cargo ship HS Dignity about 31 km east of Krapets, on Bulgaria’s northern coast, on Tuesday. Eleven of its 16 crew are missing, most of them Syrian. A second, Ukrainian-owned ship was hit; its crew were rescued (CNN, AP via CBC, Al Jazeera). Prime Minister Rumen Radev said there was no conclusive evidence of who was responsible but that “the outcome benefits the Russian Federation.” Sofia summoned Russian Ambassador Eleonora Mitrofanova, who said she had no information from Russia’s defence ministry and raised the possibility of a “third-party provocation.” NATO says it will adjust its eastern-flank posture “as needed.” On Oct. 6, an attack off Romania sank one ship and set another alight; Romania reported two dead.

Why it matters Black Sea war-risk insurance and grain freight costs, and a test of how NATO handles attacks it cannot attribute in a member’s waters.

Russia hits Kyiv’s power system again as the “energy ceasefire” goes unconfirmed by Moscow

Zelensky says Washington has threatened to cut intelligence-sharing unless Ukraine stops striking Russian refineries.

Ballistic missiles struck energy infrastructure in Kyiv on Tuesday evening without an air-raid warning, Ukrainska Pravda reports. Single source Al Jazeera reports a further large Russian attack overnight that killed at least six people and cut power in parts of the capital. The toll is preliminary and we have not found a second source.

The president announced an “ENERGY CEASEFIRE” on Oct. 11, saying “both parties have agreed.” Moscow has not confirmed it, and Zelensky says Kyiv learned of it from the media. Asked about the strikes since, the president said “there’s been violations on both sides” (Ukrainska Pravda). Zelensky told NPR the administration had warned it could cut intelligence-sharing unless Ukraine stopped long-range strikes on Russian refineries. He said Ukraine would keep striking until both sides respect a ceasefire.

Why it matters Ukraine’s refinery strikes are one reason diesel is tight worldwide. Pressure on Kyiv to stop them, together with the administration’s Oct. 9 easing of sanctions on Russian diesel exports, would ease fuel prices, which is the point.

An OpenAI engineer is charged with planting a recording device in an executive conference room

OpenAI says it has no sign that model weights, code or customer data were taken.

The FBI arrested Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, a Saudi national employed as an OpenAI engineer, on Oct. 9; the case became public Tuesday. The Justice Department charges him with using or attempting to use a device to intercept oral communications, which carries up to five years in prison. Prosecutors say an encrypted recorder was hidden behind a television in a conference room used for confidential discussions and found around Sept. 30. OpenAI says it fired him and has “no indication” that model weights, source code or customer data were taken (NBC, CBS, Washington Post, Fox Business). He has been charged, not convicted. A detention hearing is set for Oct. 19.

The curve. At Tuesday’s official close the 3-month bill yielded 4.26%, the 2-year 4.79%, the 5-year 5.00%, the 10-year 5.21% and the 30-year 5.56%. The 10-year was down 3 basis points from Friday’s 5.24%. The curve is upward-sloping: 2s10s is 42 basis points, 3-month/10-year 95 and 5s30s 56. It is little changed overnight (Newsquawk). Last week it reached 5.36%, reported as the highest since 2002. With the funds rate at 3.75–4.00%, the 3-month bill at 4.26% is pricing a little more tightening, not cuts.

Dollar, volatility, credit. The dollar index is 102.23, just below Monday’s one-year high. The VIX closed Tuesday at 15.27, with nine-day volatility at 11.99 and three-month at 17.96. That is normal contango, and short-dated protection over CPI is cheap. High-yield spreads were 3.11% on Oct. 12 and investment-grade spreads 0.83% (ICE BofA via FRED): credit is calm, close to its tights, while equities in the most leveraged parts of the market (alternative-asset managers, memory) are being repriced.

Commodities and crypto. Oil up, gold down, copper up: a war premium being rebuilt, not a flight to safety. Bitcoin is range-bound near $83,000.

The most fragile assumption. That the oil shock stays in the headline and out of the core. The tape is pricing a soft 0.2% core, falling odds of an October hike, and a VIX near 15 into the release. What would break it: core at 0.3% or higher, especially in services excluding shelter, or a jump in airfares and transport services that shows fuel costs passing through. That would bring back the hike case at 5.2% ten-year yields, with an S&P 500 that still leans on a handful of AI stocks: only about 32% of stocks with enough history sit above their 50-day trend. What would confirm it: core at 0.2% or below and a Beige Book that describes slowing, not overheating.

The Economy

Inflation. Core has been running at about 0.2–0.3% a month; the level of prices is high, but the monthly rate outside energy is not accelerating. In today’s report, watch shelter (decelerating), core goods (tariffs push up, cheap Chinese imports push down) and transport services, where fuel costs would show first. A New York Fed study reported this morning estimates tariffs raised prices of the consumer goods it studied by about 2.9 percentage points; the White House disputes it.

Labor. September payrolls rose 29,000 against about 85,000 expected. July was revised down to −10,000 and August to 133,000 (BLS via FRED; PAYEMS 159.044 million). Unemployment rose to 4.2% from 4.1%. Average hourly earnings rose 0.1% on the month and 3.0% on the year, a wage rate that does not fuel inflation. Initial jobless claims were 197,000 in the week to Oct. 3 (FRED), still low: hiring has slowed sharply, but layoffs have not risen. Thursday’s claims are expected at 195,000–200,000.

The Fed. The effective funds rate is 3.88% (Fed H.15, Oct. 12). Cleveland’s Beth Hammack argued on Oct. 12 for moving “relatively quickly” to a more restrictive stance (Investing.com); Governor Jefferson and New York’s John Williams have signalled patience. The sub-20% hike odds are market pricing, not guidance. The Beige Book at 2:00 covers late August through September; the last one said activity “increased modestly.”

The Treasury. No coupon auctions this week, only bills: a 17-week bill today and 4- and 8-week bills tomorrow (TreasuryDirect). Last week’s 10-year auction cleared at 5.300% with strong demand, after a weak 20-year reopening at 5.420% in September. The next coupon tests are a 20-year reopening on Oct. 21 and a 5-year TIPS sale on Oct. 22. On policy, Secretary Bessent said on Oct. 6 that he stands by his debt-management approach. Treasury counselor David Zervos said on Oct. 8 that real yields are “really, really high” and could come down. In an Oct. 7 letter, Senate Banking Committee members said the department’s buybacks are “failing,” noting that yields rose on the days of the first two operations. We found no new statement from Bessent this week. SOFR was 3.87% on Oct. 9.

Global central banks. The ECB’s deposit rate is 2.5% after a September increase. President Lagarde says inflation is rising mainly on energy with no second-round effects yet, and chief economist Lane calls the rise in long-term rates a “material tightening” (Newsquawk). The Bank of England’s Bailey says UK firms are concentrating on containing costs. The Bank of Korea, at 3.00% after hikes in July and August, decides Oct. 22; Governor Shin says policy stays restrictive. At the IMF and World Bank meetings in Bangkok, this week’s World Economic Outlook holds global growth at 3.1% for 2026 and puts US growth at 2.3%. It assumes oil averages $96 this year and that Hormuz traffic resumes by March.

Risks & Scenarios

A hot core print (35–45%). Core CPI rounds to 0.3% or higher. Basis: consensus is 0.2%, but August was 0.3%, and fuel costs have had a month to reach airfares and freight. Consequence: hike odds for Oct. 28 move back up, two-year yields rise and the 10-year retests 5.30%. Invalidator: core at 0.2% or below with services excluding shelter soft.

Headline at or above 3.6% (55–70%). Basis: consensus of 3.6–3.7%, with gasoline running well above a year ago. A high headline number with a quiet core is the outcome the market expects, and on its own would not move the Fed.

A hike on Oct. 28 (15–25%). Basis: futures pricing under 20%; the minutes’ hawkish lean; a weak jobs report. Today’s core and Thursday’s PPI decide whether that range moves.

Oil spikes above $110 before Oct. 23 (20–35%). Basis: Brent at $106.40 is less than 4% away; Iranian officials say attacks will step up; the Houthis have now hit an Aramco building. Invalidator: a confirmed Iranian reply to the US Hormuz proposal through Qatari mediators, or a week without a tanker hit.

The 2027 COLA lands between 3.4% and 3.7% (70–85%). Basis: two of the three quarterly CPI-W readings are known, and independent projections cluster at 3.4–3.6%.

The World

Saudi Arabia and the Houthis. Beyond the Dhahran strike, the Saudi-led coalition has kept up air strikes on Houthi positions, and Yemen’s government says its offensive toward Sanaa, under way since Oct. 5, is gaining ground; the Houthis deny it (Al Jazeera, Bloomberg). Unconfirmed toll One compilation puts deaths from the Houthi strikes on three Saudi airports between Oct. 6 and 12 at 20 or more. AP has counted at least 13 in the main Riyadh attack.

Gaza. The year-old ceasefire is fraying. An Israeli strike killed one person in Gaza City on Tuesday, and a woman was killed in al-Rimal early today. Israel says it targeted a Hamas member, accuses Hamas of rearming and is investigating. Al Jazeera reports the Israeli withdrawal line has moved west in Shujaiya, displacing families (Al Jazeera, AFP).

France. Student-led protests entered a third week as the National Assembly opened debate on the 2027 budget, and unions have joined. The justice ministry reports 6,877 arrests since mid-September, mostly of minors. More than 215 students and nearly 800 police have been injured (Euronews, Bloomberg).

Sudan. An air strike on a gold mine near the Egyptian border killed at least 72 miners, a rights monitor says (Al Jazeera). Army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan rejected talks with the Rapid Support Forces last week.

AI & Technology

Google ships Gemini 4 Argon. Google made its new top model generally available on Tuesday, through its API, AI Studio, Vertex, the Gemini app for paying subscribers and GitHub Copilot. It has a context window of one million tokens. Introductory pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens until Nov. 30, then $4 and $20. Google says it scores 77.9% on the DeepSWE coding benchmark, ahead of OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra (74.1%) and Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 (74.2%). Those are Google’s own numbers, not independent tests (Google blog, GitHub changelog). Exposure: Alphabet, and pricing pressure on rival models.

Anthropic’s investor day. Reported, not announced Bloomberg reports Anthropic is hosting institutional investors at its San Francisco headquarters today to present third-quarter financials and an IPO timetable. The report says marketing could begin as early as the week of Nov. 9, with trading before Thanksgiving, and investors cite valuations of $1.8 trillion to $2 trillion. The company has made no statement. It confidentially submitted a draft registration on June 1; no public filing has appeared.

Meta code theft. Attackers used a stolen employee credential to get into some of Meta’s GitHub repositories and took code, plus personal data on about 1,500 people, mostly US employees. Meta says about 99% of what was taken was business data, and much of the personal data was synthetic test data. It says its products and internal systems were not breached (SecurityWeek, Cybernews).

The chip buyers. Microsoft says its Maia 300 inference chip becomes its main AI chip from 2027. Reuters reports possibly more than a million units next year. That is money moving within TSMC’s customer base rather than away from it, and investors appeared to weigh it as Nvidia lagged Meta and Microsoft this week. Nvidia rose 1.0% on Tuesday and AMD 1.9%.

Science

The most distant fast radio burst. A team led by Manisha Caleb of the University of Sydney reports in Science that FRB 20240304B was detected by South Africa’s MeerKAT telescope. The James Webb Space Telescope placed its host galaxy at redshift 2.148, about 3 billion years after the Big Bang, more than doubling the previous distance record. The host is a small, clumpy, star-forming galaxy, which suggests these millisecond radio flashes can appear soon after a galaxy starts making stars. That matters because fast radio bursts are used to weigh the otherwise invisible gas between galaxies (University of Manchester, Sky & Telescope).

A planet born after its star died? Hubble spectra of the white dwarf HS 0209+0832, reported Monday in Nature Astronomy, carry a chemical signature the authors read as debris from a “second-generation” planet formed after the star shed its outer layers (Universe Today). This is one study, and the interpretation is the authors’.

Space

Cargo on its way. SpaceX launched its 35th resupply mission to the space station on Tuesday at about 6:33 a.m. from Cape Canaveral. The Dragon capsule, on its fourth flight, carries about 2,860 kg, including the last pair of roll-out solar arrays and hardware for making artificial retinas in orbit. The booster landed on its eighth flight. Docking is set for about 11:00 a.m. Thursday (SpaceNews, NASASpaceflight).

Artemis and Starship. NASA says the solid boosters for Artemis III are stacked for a tanking test and all four RS-25 engines are installed on the core stage. Artemis III is now a low-Earth-orbit test of the lunar landers, targeted for mid-to-late 2027; the landing has moved to Artemis IV. Starship’s Flight 15 is now no earlier than Nov. 8, slipped from Oct. 19.

Also in the News

Measles. The CDC counts 4,080 confirmed US cases this year through Oct. 8, the most in more than 30 years, up 193 in a week, with 414 hospitalised and five deaths. Pennsylvania has the largest state count, and New York has declared a statewide disaster emergency. The US risks losing its measles-elimination status (Reuters, CBS).

Pennsylvania

Skill games are now illegal across Pennsylvania, with no fix from Harrisburg for months

The state Supreme Court’s stay expired with no legislative deal, so its June ruling that skill games are slot machines now applies, and enforcement begins today. American Legion and VFW posts across the state have already pulled their machines, Fox43 reports. Lawmakers are still divided over how to tax and regulate the games. Rep. Russ Diamond said a fix is “at least six months out” (Fox43, local21). The General Assembly returns Monday, Oct. 19, with data-centre regulation and energy policy also pending before the session ends Nov. 30.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

A 1-gigawatt gas plant and data-centre campus is proposed on an old U.S. Steel landfill in Elizabeth Township

Developer Tim Hendricks is proposing up to 28 data-centre buildings and a 1-gigawatt natural-gas plant on a 549-acre former U.S. Steel landfill on Henderson Road, near the Youghiogheny, PublicSource and the Pennsylvania Capital-Star report from public records. He has not formally filed plans or applied for permits. At a town hall, residents raised water, power, noise and proximity concerns, and an environmental group opposes the project. Accounts differ A trade outlet citing the Times Leader describes a larger plan with zoning permits and a lawsuit; that may be a different project.
