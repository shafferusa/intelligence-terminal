Today’s News

Two more tankers hit near Hormuz overnight, and smoke rises near an Aramco site in Riyadh

These are the first attacks on Gulf energy shipping since the G7 agreed its emergency oil release on Friday. No one has claimed them, and oil markets cannot react until futures reopen Sunday evening.

Early Saturday, Britain’s UK Maritime Trade Operations centre reported that two crude tankers had been struck by “unidentified projectiles” in separate incidents near the Strait of Hormuz.

  • First ship. Outbound through the strait. It caught fire briefly and lost power. The fire was put out and the ship continued its voyage.
  • Second ship. Hit a few hours later about four nautical miles east of Oman. Regional outlets identify it as the Kuwait-flagged Kazimah III, bound for Lomé in Togo, with damage on its port side. India’s embassy says five Indian crew members were moved ashore.

UKMTO reported no casualties. One maritime outlet headlined two injuries, and this edition could not reconcile the two accounts.

Single witness, cause unknown Separately, a witness told Reuters on Saturday morning of a large plume of smoke and fire near an Aramco facility in Riyadh. NASA’s FIRMS satellite fire data shows heat signatures at the site. Saudi authorities have not confirmed an incident and nobody has claimed one. The Houthis have struck Saudi cities and energy sites repeatedly since September, including a fuel depot at Riyadh’s airport on Sept. 20. That history is context, not attribution.

Why it matters Oil’s floor this week was the risk of a strike like these. Brent fell below $100 on Friday morning on the G7 release and recovered every cent by the settle. Attacks on two tankers in one night, and a possible incident at a Saudi energy site, test whether a release of about one day’s world consumption can offset a strait that is still not open to normal traffic.
What’s next OPEC+ ministers meet online on Sunday. Reuters sources expect November targets to be left unchanged. Oil and stock futures reopen Sunday at 6 PM ET, the first chance to price the weekend.

The Week’s Ten Stories

1 · The long end will not come down: the 10-year touches a 2002 high and ends the week at 5.28% on soft data

Every piece of US data this week argued for lower yields. Long yields rose anyway, which suggests the long end is pricing something other than the next Fed meeting.

How it started. On Monday the 2-year jumped 11 basis points, to 4.92%. Governor Cook said inflation was running at “almost double our target,” and the 10-year closed at 5.24%.

How it developed. The rest of the week brought news that should have lowered yields, and the long end rose each time:

  • Tuesday. New York Fed President Williams said there was “no need for urgency,” and the 2-year fell. The 10-year still closed at 5.26%, and the 30-year traded at its highest since 2002.
  • Wednesday. The inflation revision came in lower than expected. The 30-year closed at 5.64%, a 24-year high.
  • Thursday, overnight. A global long-bond selloff took the 10-year to about 5.34%, Britain’s 30-year gilt above 6% and Japan’s 30-year to about 4.2%. Treasuries then reversed, and the 2-year fell 10 basis points for the day.

How it ended. On Friday the jobs report missed badly. The 10-year fell to about 5.16% at 8:30, then rose all day and closed at 5.28%. That was 11 basis points higher on the week and 4 basis points higher on the day. The 30-year finished at 5.63%, up 14 basis points. The 2-year was up only 2, at 4.83%. The gap between the 2- and 10-year yields widened from 36 to 45 basis points.

Why it mattered. Mortgage rates, corporate refinancing and equity valuations are priced off the long end, not off the Fed’s next decision. When rates rise because the Fed is expected to tighten, softer data can bring them back down. A rise driven by term premium, the extra return investors demand for lending long, does not reverse that way.

What was misunderstood. Many accounts linked the rise in yields to the October hike odds. The week disproved that link. Hike odds fell by about 50 points and the 10-year rose anyway. A market narrative points to supply, fiscal worries, oil and a global selloff, but none of these is confirmed as the cause. Inflation expectations barely moved: the 10-year breakeven went from 2.34% to 2.36%.

Unresolved. Whether $119 billion of 3-, 10- and 30-year auctions, from Tuesday to Thursday, clear without another weak result.

2 · Payrolls rise 29,000 and the October hike falls off the table

The labour market has gone from cooling to close to stalled. The Fed’s debate is now about December.

How it started. Futures put about a 70% chance on an October hike on Monday. Tuesday brought two weak readings: Conference Board confidence fell to 81.9, its lowest in more than 12 years, and job openings missed at 7.08 million.

How it developed. Fed officials disagreed on timing:

  • Barr (Tuesday): further hikes are “likely to be needed.”
  • Williams (Tuesday): one more “may be appropriate late this year.”
  • Jefferson (Thursday): worried that higher energy prices could spread into other prices.
  • Logan (Thursday evening): the target range “needs to rise an additional 50 basis points or more.”

Jobless claims stayed near 197,000, close to their lowest in decades.

How it ended. The BLS reported on Friday:

  • September payrolls: +29,000.
  • Revisions: July cut to −10,000 and August to +133,000, removing 60,000 jobs from earlier reports.
  • Three-month average: about 51,000 a month.
  • Unemployment: 4.2%. Participation also rose, to 61.8%.
  • Hourly pay: +0.1% on the month and 3.0% on the year, the slowest annual pace since 2021.

October hike odds fell to about 15–18% (CNBC, Kalshi), and Reuters put them below 15%.

Why it mattered. Pay is rising 3.0% a year, about the same as core inflation, so real wages are flat. That weakens the wage-price argument for more hikes. Oil is still near $100, though, and the inflation argument for one more hike has not gone away.

What was misunderstood. “A soft report means lower rates” held for the front end only. The 2-year fell in the first minutes after the report and closed 5 basis points higher on the day.

Unresolved. Wednesday’s September FOMC minutes will show how many officials wanted more than one more hike. September CPI is due Oct. 14.

3 · Inflation is rewritten lower and growth higher

August itself was ordinary. Most of Wednesday’s “downside surprise” came from revising the past, not from a cooler August.

What happened. On Wednesday BEA published August personal income and outlays together with its annual update of the national accounts:

  • August core PCE: +0.25% on the month, in line with forecasts.
  • Core PCE over the year: 3.0%, against the 3.3–3.4% expected.
  • Revision to July: core inflation for the year to July was cut to 2.98% from 3.34%. FRED’s vintage data show the change.
  • Second-quarter GDP: raised to 2.2% from 1.5%.
  • August consumer: spending rose 0.9% while income rose only 0.2%, and the saving rate is 4.1%.

Why it mattered. The revised history has faster growth and lower inflation than before. That is a better starting point for the Fed and a weaker case for urgency. It does not change the Fed’s current inflation worry, which is oil passing through into other prices.

What was misunderstood. Headlines called it a big inflation miss. The monthly figure matched forecasts; the lower annual rate came from revised earlier months.

Unresolved. This edition has not read BEA’s full revision tables. Reports that the update changed how some prices are measured are unconfirmed.

4 · Iran: an offer rejected, a delegation expelled, oil held above $100

Diplomacy went backwards this week, and the oil price stayed high despite more supply.

How it started. On Sunday Trump said on the record that Iran’s seven-day plan to reopen Hormuz “would not be acceptable.” Brent jumped toward $109 on Monday morning, then settled at $105.28 after reports that Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline was running again, carrying about 3.5 million barrels a day to Red Sea ports.

How it developed.

  • Monday and Tuesday. Qatari mediators met each side separately in New York, with no result. Rubio then ordered Iran’s UN delegation, including Foreign Minister Araghchi, to leave New York, according to Axios. Iran’s mission says the departure had been scheduled. The IRGC said it had seized a US underwater drone in the strait. Iran’s negotiator warned that “no infrastructure will remain safe” without security guarantees.
  • Thursday. The WSJ and the AP reported more US forces heading to the region, Iran said “the ball is in the US court,” and Treasury added sanctions on Iran’s industrial sectors and a shadow-banking network. Brent rose 4.4%.
  • Friday. The G7 agreed through the IEA to release up to 100 million barrels of crude and diesel over four months. Brent briefly fell below $100 and settled at $102.25.

Why it mattered. Brent settled above $100 every day of the week, while US crude fell 1.4%, to $91.11. The extra supply from Saudi pipelines, the US strategic reserve and the IEA release capped the price but did not bring it down. Traders are pricing a strait that stays closed until there is a political settlement.

What was misunderstood. Some reporting presented the release as relief for prices. It equals about one day of world consumption spread over four months, and most of the early volume is diesel for Europe.

Unresolved. Overnight tanker strikes (Today’s News), Sunday’s OPEC+ meeting, and whether the mediation channel through Qatar survives the expulsion.

7 · FICO loses nearly a quarter of its value as Fannie and Freddie open the door to VantageScore

9 · Starship reaches orbit for the first time; Crew-13 makes the fastest US crewed trip to the station

The rocket that NASA’s lunar landings depend on finally flew an orbital mission, though a short one.

What happened.

  • Starship, Monday. Flight 14 reached orbit after one Raptor engine shut down early during ascent and controllers reversed a “no-go” call. It deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites. The planned ten-hour mission was cut to about three hours and ended with a splashdown in the Pacific.
  • Crew-13, Thursday. The crew launched at 11:10 AM and docked 7 hours 55 minutes later, the fastest US crewed trip to the International Space Station. The ISS now holds 11 people.

Why it mattered. Starship’s suborbital phase ran for more than three years, and the lunar-lander work for Artemis depends on orbital flights and refuelling. Shares of SpaceX (SPCX) barely moved on the launch day. They rose 7.4% on Friday on heavy volume, with no single confirmed catalyst.

Unresolved. Why the engine shut down early, and when the next flight will be. Musk has said he is eyeing an attempt to catch the ship by year’s end.

10 · Washington renames AI “Super Intelligence” and signs a voluntary safety pact with the labs

It brings in external audits and board oversight, and it is not binding law.

What happened. On Tuesday Trump and executives from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft, xAI and Amazon signed a “Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities.” It sets out four layers of control:

  • internal controls on cyber, biological and chemical risk;
  • internal verification teams;
  • an independent external auditor;
  • a board-level committee that reviews the audits.

Trump called it “morally binding.” A same-day executive order replaces the term “Artificial Intelligence” with “Super Intelligence” across the executive branch. Micron’s earnings release used the new term the next day.

Why it mattered. It is the administration’s answer to calls for AI regulation: self-policing with audits instead of statute. Federal preemption of state AI laws is still stalled. On Wednesday California signed a package of AI bills, including a first-in-the-nation rule on lawyers’ use of generative AI.

Unresolved. Who the external auditors are and what happens when an audit fails. The text names no penalty.

What Changed in the World

The US labour market stopped adding meaningful numbers of jobs. Three months averaging about 51,000, with July now negative, is the weakest hiring stretch of this expansion. A month ago the Fed was hiking into a hot economy. It is now weighing a hike into a stalled job market with inflation near 3%. That changes the October decision and makes the whole path less certain.

The bond market stopped taking its cues from the Fed. For two weeks the front end and the long end have moved in opposite directions. Fed officials can calm the 2-year. So far nothing calms the 30-year, not even soft data. This was a global move too: gilts above 6% and Japan’s 30-year near 4.2% hit the same overnight peak. The worry has shifted from inflation to who keeps buying long-dated government debt.

The Gulf war now has a calendar. Trump’s own words, “probably right after the midterms,” together with the delegation’s expulsion and a carrier buildup due by November, point to five more weeks of a closed strait and oil near $100. Overnight’s tanker strikes and the reported Saudi plans on Yemen’s coast make the risk run in one direction.

Credit joined the warning. Last week this paper noted that credit lagged the rates move. This week it caught up. Stocks are the one market still calm, and their only support is a narrow chip rally.

Scorecard

Forty-two forecasts came due this week, the largest batch yet: 17 correct, 22 wrong, 3 partial. A forecast counts as correct when what it said would happen did happen, whatever probability it carried. Many of the “wrong” grades below are low-probability calls that did not happen and were reasonably calibrated. The lessons separate those from real misses.

Science

A new kind of Parkinson’s drug. On Monday the FDA approved AbbVie’s tavapadon (brand name Juvmo), a once-daily pill that partially activates two specific dopamine receptors, D1 and D5. In the TEMPO trials it cut daily “off” time, when symptoms return, by 1.9 hours, against 0.9 hours on placebo, on top of levodopa. It is the first drug of its class. Launch is expected this month.

A heart valve that grows with the child. On Thursday the FDA approved the Autus Size-Adjustable Valve for children with congenital pulmonary valve disease. It is the first valve that can be expanded after surgery as the child grows, and the first US-approved valve with synthetic polymer leaflets rather than animal tissue. Each expansion could replace a repeat open-heart operation.

Alzheimer’s tau spreads like a prion. A Nature paper published Wednesday reports that tau filaments from the brains of people with Alzheimer’s and corticobasal degeneration seeded new filaments of the same shapes in normal mice. Prions are misfolded proteins that copy their shape onto healthy ones, and this is direct evidence that tau propagates the same way. That matters for therapies aimed at stopping the spread through the brain. It is one peer-reviewed study in mice.
