The Triangle Ratios That Learned to Repeat Forever

Friday, October 2, 2026 · 15 min read

Moving sine and cosine off a fixed triangle and onto a rotating circle turned two triangle ratios into the default mathematical shape of anything that repeats — sound, tides, and the current in a wall socket.

Mathematics

Day 5 of 300 · Mathematics 5 of 20 · Trigonometry: triangles, circles, and periodic motion

The Big Idea

Trigonometry began as a measuring tool. Ancient surveyors, astronomers and navigators needed a way to connect an angle you can measure — the tilt of a sightline to a star, the slope of a hill — to a distance you cannot measure directly, such as the star’s height above the horizon or the hill’s true elevation. The answer, built piece by piece over more than a thousand years, was a short list of fixed ratios between a right triangle’s sides — sine, cosine and tangent — that depend only on one of the triangle’s angles, never on its size. Know the angle, and you know every ratio; know one side and one ratio, and every other side follows without pacing off the distance yourself.

That would already be a useful trick confined to triangles. What turned trigonometry into something far bigger is a single reframing: instead of asking what a triangle’s side ratios are, place the triangle inside a circle and ask where a point sits as it sweeps around the circle’s edge. Sine and cosine stop being properties of one fixed triangle and become coordinates of a moving point — defined at every angle, not only the acute ones a triangle can hold, and repeating exactly every time the point comes back around to where it started.

That repetition is the real payoff. Anything that cycles on a fixed schedule — a plucked string’s vibration, the alternating current in a wall socket, a tide rising and falling against a harbor wall — turns out to be describable, almost always, with exactly these same two functions. A tool built to measure triangles became the default mathematical language for anything that goes in circles or comes back around.

How It Works

Start with the triangle, because that is genuinely where the subject starts. Take any right triangle and pick one of its two non-right angles; call it θ (theta). Relative to θ, one of the two shorter sides is opposite it (the side that doesn’t touch the angle), and the other is adjacent to it (touching the angle, but not the hypotenuse). The three basic ratios compare these sides: sine of θ is the opposite side divided by the hypotenuse; cosine of θ is the adjacent side divided by the hypotenuse; tangent of θ is the opposite side divided by the adjacent side — which, since sine and cosine already carry the hypotenuse underneath, works out to simply sine divided by cosine. Generations of students have memorized this trio as SOH-CAH-TOA: Sine is Opposite over Hypotenuse, Cosine is Adjacent over Hypotenuse, Tangent is Opposite over Adjacent.

What makes these ratios worth naming at all is yesterday’s idea: similar triangles. Any two right triangles that share the angle θ are similar (matched by the AA test), so their sides scale together — the ratio of opposite to hypotenuse comes out identical no matter how large or small the triangle is. The ratio depends on the angle alone. That is what let Thales read the Great Pyramid’s height off its shadow, and it is what lets a surveyor or an astronomer today turn one measured angle into a distance they could never pace off directly.

The Greek astronomer Hipparchus, working around 140 BCE, is generally credited as the field’s founder: he is believed to have compiled the first table relating angles to chord lengths in a circle, built to support astronomical calculation, and Claudius Ptolemy expanded the same approach roughly three centuries later in the Almagest. The Greek tables worked with whole chords — the straight-line distance across an arc — which is workable but clumsy. The more convenient idea, a half-chord measured against the radius and close to the modern sine, appears around 500 CE in the work of the Indian astronomer Aryabhata, who called it jya, meaning roughly “bowstring” — a fitting image for a chord stretched across an arc.

What happened to that word on its way into English is one of mathematics’ odder accidents. Arabic scholars transliterated jya as jiba, a word with no independent meaning of its own in Arabic, and routinely abbreviated it in manuscripts down to its bare consonants. Centuries later, when 12th-century translators rendered Arabic mathematical texts into Latin, one of them read that abbreviated form not as jiba but as the ordinary Arabic word jaib, meaning a “fold” or “bosom” of a garment — an unrelated word that happened to look identical once the vowels were dropped. He translated it accordingly, using the Latin word for a fold or curve: sinus. English later anglicized that to “sine.” The function you now know as sine is, etymologically, a roughly 900-year-old translator’s mistake — its real name was never about a shape at all.

That history explains the triangle ratios. The circle is what frees them from the triangle entirely. Draw a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin of a graph, and measure an angle θ counterclockwise starting from the positive x-axis. Define cosine of θ as the x-coordinate of the point where that angle meets the circle, and sine of θ as its y-coordinate. For any angle between 0° and 90°, this matches the triangle definition exactly: drop a line straight down from the point to the x-axis and you have built a right triangle with hypotenuse 1 (the radius), so “opposite over hypotenuse” is just the y-coordinate divided by 1, and “adjacent over hypotenuse” is the x-coordinate divided by 1.

But the circle definition does not stop at 90°, because a point can keep moving around a circle long after a right triangle runs out of room for an angle. At 150° the point has swung into the upper-left, with a negative x-coordinate and a positive y-coordinate: cosine has gone negative while sine is still positive. At 270° the point sits at the bottom of the circle: cosine is 0, sine is −1. Keep going past 360° and the point simply laps the circle again, landing exactly where it started. Sine and cosine, defined this way, produce an output for literally every angle, not only the ones a triangle can hold — and because the circle has only one way around, those outputs repeat exactly every full turn. A function whose values repeat in a fixed cycle like this is a periodic function, and the length of that cycle is its period: 360° for sine and cosine, measured the ordinary way.

Tangent deserves a word of its own, because the circle makes its geometric meaning sharper than the ratio definition alone suggests. On the unit circle, tangent of θ is sin θ divided by cos θ — which is literally the slope of the straight line running from the origin out to the point at angle θ, since slope is rise over run, and that point’s rise and run are exactly sin θ and cos θ. That also explains tangent’s one real quirk: at 90° and 270°, cosine is 0, so the ratio divides by zero and tangent is undefined. Geometrically, the line from the origin has gone perfectly vertical at those angles, and a vertical line has no finite slope to report.

A handful of angles are worth being able to picture directly from the triangle itself, because they recur constantly in later work. Cut a square in half along its diagonal and you get a triangle with two 45° angles and two equal legs; if each leg has length 1, the Pythagorean theorem makes the hypotenuse √2, so sine and cosine of 45° are both 1/√2, about 0.707. Cut an equilateral triangle in half instead and you get a 30°-60°-90° triangle whose sides come out in the fixed ratio 1 : √3 : 2 — which gives sine of 30° as exactly 1/2, cosine of 30° as √3/2 (about 0.866), and the same two values swapped for 60°. None of that needs a calculator or a lookup table; it falls straight out of two triangles anyone can draw.

“The ordinary way” is doing some work in that sentence, because 360 is not a number mathematics would choose on its own — it is a legacy of Babylonian astronomers, who divided the circle that way roughly 4,000 years ago, plausibly because 360 sits close to the number of days in a year and divides evenly by a great many small numbers. The unit a circle actually hands you for free is the radian: the angle you get when the arc you sweep out has the same length as the circle’s own radius. Because a full circumference is 2π times the radius, a full turn comes out to exactly 2π radians — about 6.283. A right angle is π/2 radians; a straight line is π radians. The word itself is comparatively recent: the mathematician James Thomson, physicist Lord Kelvin’s brother, coined “radian” for a Queen’s College Belfast exam paper in 1873, even though the underlying idea — measuring an angle by the arc length it produces — had already been used informally by mathematicians for well over a century before that. Radians earn their keep once calculus enters the picture in a few lessons’ time; for now, the fact worth keeping is simpler: an angle has two equally legitimate units, and the circle itself, not human convention, is what defines the radian.

Key Concepts

Sine, cosine, tangent
The three basic trigonometric ratios of a right triangle’s sides relative to one angle θ: sine = opposite/hypotenuse, cosine = adjacent/hypotenuse, tangent = opposite/adjacent (equivalently, sine/cosine). On the unit circle, cosine and sine become the x- and y-coordinates of a point at angle θ.
The unit circle
A circle of radius 1 centered at the origin of a graph, used to define sine and cosine for any angle — not only the acute angles a triangle can hold — as the coordinates of the point that angle points to.
Radians
A unit for measuring angles defined by the circle itself: one radian is the angle that sweeps out an arc equal in length to the radius. A full turn is 2π radians (about 6.283); a straight angle is π radians.
Periodic function
A function whose output values repeat in a fixed cycle as the input increases; the length of that cycle is its period. Sine and cosine repeat every 360° (2π radians).

Example / Application

Periodic motion shows up constantly once you know to look for it, and in nearly every case the shape it traces is a sine wave: a plucked string’s air-pressure vibration, a tide rising and falling against a harbor wall, the length of daylight across a year. The clearest example sitting in every wall of Logan’s house is electrical: the alternating current, or AC, that household wiring delivers.

“Alternating” is a precise description, not a marketing word. The voltage at a US wall outlet does not hold steady at some fixed number the way a battery does; it oscillates along a sine curve, 60 complete cycles every second.

A tuning fork or a single clean musical note is an even purer case. A fork cut to concert pitch A vibrates at 440 cycles per second, and the air-pressure wave it sends out traces, to a very good approximation, a single sine wave at that frequency — which is why that tone, viewed on an oscilloscope, looks exactly like the unit circle’s y-coordinate, just plotted against time instead of angle.

What the wall socket is actually doing. US household power is rated at 120 volts, but that figure is a root-mean-square average, not the voltage’s true peak — it is the steady equivalent voltage that would deliver the same average power as the real, swinging signal. The actual peak voltage is higher, by a factor of the square root of 2:

Vpeak = 120 × √2 ≈ 120 × 1.41 ≈ 169.7 volts.

So the voltage at a US outlet is tracing V(t) = 169.7 × sin(2π × 60 × t), swinging from about +169.7 volts to about −169.7 volts and back, 60 times every second — a period of 1/60 second, roughly 16.7 milliseconds — and crossing exactly 0 volts twice in every cycle, 120 times a second. None of that is visible in ordinary use: 120 crossings a second is far faster than a lamp’s filament can cool or an eye can detect, so the light just looks steadily on. Most of the rest of the world instead standardizes around 230 volts RMS at 50 cycles per second — a different amplitude and a different period traced along exactly the same kind of curve.

Equation / Model

The unit-circle definition is worth writing down exactly, because everything above follows from it directly. For a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin, and an angle θ measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis:

(x, y) = (cos θ, sin θ)

The point x, y equals the point cosine theta, sine theta.

θ
the angle, measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis, in degrees or radians
x
the horizontal coordinate of the point where angle θ meets the unit circle — by definition, cos θ
y
the vertical coordinate of that same point — by definition, sin θ

That definition carries a consequence worth deriving in full, because it is the most-used identity in all of trigonometry and it comes from nowhere more exotic than yesterday’s theorem. Every point on a circle of radius 1 satisfies x² + y² = 1² — that is just the Pythagorean theorem, applied to the right triangle formed by the point, the origin, and the x-axis, with the circle’s radius as the hypotenuse. Since x is cos θ and y is sin θ by definition, substituting gives:

cos² θ + sin² θ = 1

Cosine squared theta plus sine squared theta equals one.

This is the Pythagorean identity, and it holds for every angle θ there is — not because of any new fact about triangles, but because it was already guaranteed the moment sine and cosine were defined as coordinates on a circle of radius 1. It is the reason sine and cosine can never both be large at once: if cosine is close to 1, sine squared has almost nothing left to contribute, so sine must be close to 0, and vice versa. The two functions permanently trade off against each other, in lockstep, because together they can never add up to more than a fixed total.

Why It Matters

The jump this lesson makes — from “ratio of two sides on one fixed triangle” to “coordinates of a point sweeping around a circle, forever repeating” — is why trigonometry never stayed confined to surveying and astronomy. Once sine and cosine are understood as the basic shape of repetition itself, they turn up anywhere something cycles: a sound engineer describes a musical tone as a sum of sine waves at different frequencies; an electrical engineer designs a power grid around the sine wave worked out above; a physicist models visible light as an oscillating electric field tracing the same curve, at frequencies around hundreds of trillions of cycles per second. All of them are using exactly the function this lesson built out of a triangle and a circle.

One of the earliest machines to put that idea to practical use came from James Thomson’s own family. His brother, the physicist Lord Kelvin, built a mechanical tide-predicting machine in the 1870s that combined several separate sine waves — each one representing a distinct astronomical cycle of the sun and moon pulling on a harbor’s water — by turning them through gears and summing the results with pulleys and a wire, printing out a tide curve for years ahead faster than any team of human calculators could compute one by hand. The underlying idea, adding together sine waves of different frequencies to build up a more complicated repeating signal, is today called harmonic or Fourier analysis, and it is the same principle a modern computer uses to store a digital song as a list of sine-wave components rather than a raw pressure recording.

There is a deeper unification still to come. Day 7’s lesson on complex numbers will introduce Euler’s identity, which shows that sine and cosine are not really a separate pair of functions at all — they turn out to be two faces of the exponential function itself, once that function is allowed to take an imaginary input. None of that machinery is needed yet; the unit-circle picture built today is what will make that later identity legible instead of mysterious when it arrives.

For now, the habit worth keeping is simpler: whenever something in the world repeats on a cycle — a wave, a rotation, a season — a sine or cosine function, in radians or degrees, is almost certainly the mathematical model hiding underneath it.

Remember These
  • Sine, cosine and tangent are ratios of a right triangle’s sides relative to one angle — they depend only on the angle, never on the triangle’s size.
  • The unit circle extends sine and cosine to every angle, not only the acute ones a triangle can hold: cos θ and sin θ are simply the x- and y-coordinates of a point at angle θ on a circle of radius 1.
  • A radian is the circle’s own natural unit for angle: the angle that sweeps out an arc equal to the radius. A full turn is 2π radians.
  • Because rotation repeats, sine and cosine are periodic functions — the default mathematical shape of anything that cycles, from sound to AC electricity to tides.
  • cos² θ + sin² θ = 1 for every angle, a direct consequence of the Pythagorean theorem applied to the unit circle.
Think About It

Early in this lesson, sine and cosine were defined by which side of a right triangle you compare to the hypotenuse: opposite for sine, adjacent for cosine. A right triangle’s two non-right angles always add up to 90°. Using just that fact and the triangle definitions — not the unit circle — can you work out why cosine of θ always equals sine of (90° − θ), for any acute angle θ? What does that tell you about how the two functions relate to each other?
