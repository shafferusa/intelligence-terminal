Top Stories

The 10-year yield hits a 2002 high, then a strong auction and the Fed’s minutes calm the long end

Treasury sold $39 billion of 10-year notes at 5.300%, below the pre-auction market level, and the minutes confirmed most officials expect another hike this year. Stocks still finished lower, led down by small caps and industrials.

The morning belonged to the bond selloff. The 10-year yield rose to about 5.365% in the session, its highest since 2002, and the 30-year to about 5.72%, also a 24-year high (CNBC). Oil was firm and the dollar was at a 17-month high. The S&P 500 was down about 0.7% at its low (ts2.tech, a lower-tier source; the size of the intraday drop is not confirmed elsewhere).

The 1:00 PM auction reversed that. Treasury’s results (TreasuryDirect):

  • $39 billion 10-year reopening, sold at a high yield of 5.300%, the highest at a 10-year auction since 2000
  • About 1.7bp below the 5.317% when-issued level just before the sale (investingLive)
  • Bid-to-cover 2.77, above the recent average of about 2.5
  • Indirect bidders, a proxy for foreign and institutional demand, took $31.1 billion, about 80%
  • Direct bidders took $6.6 billion
  • Primary dealers, who absorb what nobody else wants, were left with under $1 billion, about 2.5%

Dealers were left with a very small share. That is as clean as an auction gets: buyers who had stayed out of the morning selloff came in at 5.30%. BMO called it strong, and RTTNews called demand “modestly above average.”

The minutes followed at 2:00 and held no surprises on the main question. Yields were little changed into the close: the 10-year finished at 5.28% (up 1bp), the 2-year at 4.77% (down 2bp) and the 30-year at 5.67% (up 3bp) on Treasury’s official curve.

Stocks did not join the recovery. The S&P 500 fell 0.22% and the Nasdaq 0.22%. The Dow lost 0.66%, and the Russell 2000 fell 1.31%. Market narrative Bloomberg’s live coverage tied the weakness to higher oil lifting yields and stoking fears of more Fed hikes. That fits the morning. It does not explain why small caps and industrials stayed weak after yields eased. Equal weight (RSP −0.81%) trailed the cap-weighted S&P by more than half a point.

Why it matters

The auction answered the most important question in the bond market this week: is there still a buyer for long Treasuries at these yields? Today there was, at 5.30%. That puts a working ceiling under the 10-year, for now. But the yield still closed higher, and nothing in the minutes suggests short rates are done rising.

What’s next

Tomorrow brings the $22 billion 30-year reopening at 1:00 PM. September’s 30-year reopening sold at 5.308%, and the bond now trades about 36bp higher. Weekly jobless claims are at 8:30 AM, expected near 200,000. Governor Waller speaks at 4:30 AM. September CPI on October 14 is the next real test for the October 28 meeting.

Sources: TreasuryDirect auction results (primary); Treasury par yield curve (primary); FRED; CNBC; investingLive; RTTNews; Bloomberg; Yahoo Finance; TheStreet.

Fed minutes: “most participants” expect another hike by year-end, and energy prices are the worry

The September record shows a unanimous committee that now sees inflation risks tilted up and the labor market broadly balanced. It gives no timing signal between October and December.

The minutes of the September 15–16 meeting, at which the Fed raised its target range a quarter-point to 3.75–4.00%, were published at 2:00 PM. That was its first increase since 2023. What the minutes say:

  • The next step. “Most participants assessed that another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate by year end.” Participants also stressed that they approach “each meeting with an open mind.”
  • The balance of risks. “Almost all participants” judged that inflation risks were tilted to the upside, while labor-market risks “had diminished and were now broadly balanced.”
  • Energy. “Many participants” said that the longer energy prices remained elevated, the greater the risk that higher costs in some sectors could spread into broader price pressures. The minutes name geopolitical developments that have pushed up crude and refined-fuel prices. They also name surging AI-related investment.
  • Expectations. “Some participants” worried that after more than five years of inflation above 2%, elevated rates of inflation could begin to affect inflation expectations.

Fed staff estimated August PCE inflation at 3.8% headline and 3.4% core, both year over year.

The minutes do not choose between October 28 and December 9 (AP). Before their release, futures pricing implied a hold in October and a hike in December (Reuters). This morning’s CME estimate put the chance of an October move at about 22%. We have no reliable post-minutes reading of that probability, so we do not give one. There were no scheduled Fed speakers today.

Sources: Federal Reserve, Minutes of the FOMC, September 15–16, 2026 (primary); AP via KSAT; Reuters via Yahoo Finance.

Russia fires about 70 missiles and 130 drones at Ukraine; at least 19 killed

Pryluky in the Chernihiv region was hit hardest, where a five-storey apartment block collapsed. The toll rose through the day and was still being revised at press time.

Overnight into Wednesday, Putin’s 74th birthday, Russia launched one of the largest combined strikes of the war. Ukrainian officials counted about 70 missiles, many of them ballistic, and about 130 drones. Ukraine had warned a day earlier that a large attack was coming.

The death toll rose through the day. AP reported at least 19 dead. France 24 counted at least 21 civilians killed, four of them children, and about 60 wounded. Other outlets carried higher figures late in the day. At least 14 of the dead were in a single five-storey apartment block in Pryluky. Fires broke out in Kyiv. President Zelensky said energy infrastructure was a principal target. Russia’s defence ministry described a “massive strike” on facilities that make and store missiles and drones.

Zelensky said Ukraine struck four targets inside Russia in 24 hours, including oil facilities in the Perm, Samara and Astrakhan regions. That follows Monday night’s drone attack on a Moscow-region fuel depot. Search summaries carried claims that Ukraine has run out of Patriot interceptors. We could not trace them to a named source, so we do not report them as fact.

Sources: AP via the Washington Post and Washington Times; France 24; ABC News; the Globe and Mail. Casualty figures are from Ukrainian officials and were still changing at press time.

Tanker attacks around Hormuz hit their highest weekly count of the Iran war

Maritime security sources counted at least 12 attacks on tankers in the week to October 5. The IMO’s own count was nine, also a record. Brent held just above $101.

Reuters reported that maritime security sources counted at least 12 attacks on oil, LNG and LPG tankers around the Strait of Hormuz between September 28 and October 5. That is the highest weekly number since the war began on February 28. The International Maritime Organization counted nine for the same week, above its previous high of eight in mid-July. Named vessels include the supertankers Cameroon Prosperity and Ghana Prosperity, the gas carriers Vela Gas and Maran Gas Mystras, and the On Peace, on which India says 12 crew were injured.

UKMTO describes the projectiles as “unknown,” and no source we found confirms who fired them. We found no new UKMTO incident dated today.

Brent traded near $101 most of the day; the December contract was $101.15 late in the session on delayed data. WTI was about $89. The EIA said US commercial crude stocks fell 3.2 million barrels last week, against expectations of a build of about 1.7 million. Gasoline stocks rose 0.4 million and distillates were little changed. The IEA’s governing board met informally today about a possible further release of emergency oil and diesel stocks, following the G7’s October 2 agreement to release 100 million barrels over four months (secondary sources only).

Why it matters

The attack rate is still rising, not plateauing. That keeps a floor under crude and, through the Fed’s energy concern, under short-term rate expectations. Iranian statements about a maritime “exclusion zone” and safe-passage talks with Oman appeared in live blogs we could not read, and we do not report them as fact.

Sources: Reuters (via Investing.com, Arab News); IMO; UKMTO; EIA weekly petroleum status report via Reuters/BOE Report; Yahoo Finance (delayed futures).

Unexplained We found no Caterpillar or Deere filing, downgrade or news report to explain the moves, and older articles about past selloffs dominate searches. A sector-wide drop of this size on a day of rising long yields and a stronger dollar fits the usual pattern for cyclical, export-heavy stocks, but that is a pattern, not a confirmed cause. Many of these names, Caterpillar and GE Vernova especially, had large gains this year on data-center power and construction demand. That leaves them exposed to profit-taking.

SpaceX reportedly lines up about $40 billion of debt to buy Nvidia chips; shares fall 2.5%

Bloomberg reported roughly $10 billion of bank loans and $30 billion of investment-grade debt, with Apollo involved. SpaceX has not confirmed it, and about 328 million shares come off lockup on Friday.

Bloomberg reported that SpaceX is arranging about $40 billion of financing to buy Nvidia AI chips. Reuters and the FT were cited as reporting it too, and CNBC described Apollo’s role. The reported split is about $10 billion of bank loans and $30 billion of investment-grade debt. SpaceX had not commented in anything we found, and we found no SEC filing.

Likely contributor The debt report for SpaceX itself. For the rest of the group, small-cap weakness overall (Russell −1.3%) is at least as plausible an explanation. Susquehanna raised its SpaceX price target to $173 from $144.

What’s next

That figure comes from one market-data headline feed, and we found no company or underwriter notice confirming it. A $40 billion debt raise would make SpaceX one of the largest investment-grade borrowers of the AI build-out. That is exactly the kind of supply the Fed’s desk linked to higher term premiums.

The Economy

The Treasury and supply. The 10-year reopening was the second of this week’s three coupon sales: $58 billion of 3-year notes Tuesday, $39 billion of 10-year notes today and $22 billion of 30-year bonds Thursday, all settling October 15. Both sales so far have stopped through their when-issued levels. The 3-year sold at 4.932% with a 2.62 bid-to-cover. A 17-week bill also sold today with a 3.10 bid-to-cover. Tomorrow brings the 30-year, plus $110 billion of 4-week and $105 billion of 8-week bills.

The curve. On Treasury’s official curve, the 1-year fell 4bp to 4.42% and the 2-year 2bp to 4.77%. The 5-year was unchanged at 5.03%. The 10-year rose 1bp to 5.28%, and the 20- and 30-year rose 3bp to 5.71% and 5.67%. The full table is in the appendix.

The front end eased after the minutes and the long end did not. That is a steepening driven by the long end: the market accepts more hikes but wants more yield for holding long bonds. The 3-month to 10-year spread is about 106bp.

Consumer credit. The Fed’s G.19 at 3:00 PM showed consumer credit grew at a 1.9% annual rate in August, seasonally adjusted, roughly $8 billion for the month. Revolving credit, mostly credit cards, shrank at a 4.2% annual rate. Non-revolving credit, mainly auto and student loans, grew. Falling card balances at a time of high rates and a slowing job market look more like consumer caution than consumer strength. One month is not a trend, and we found no consensus figure.

Credit. The ICE BofA high-yield spread tightened to 3.03 percentage points on Tuesday, from 3.12 (FRED, EOD official, one-day lag). It is still very tight by historical standards. HYG slipped 0.1% today and LQD was flat. Credit markets are not echoing the long-end stress.

Energy and inflation. The EIA’s weekly report showed a 3.2 million-barrel crude draw, to 424.1 million barrels. Cushing stocks rose 0.4 million. Brent is near $101 and WTI near $89. Ten-year breakeven inflation was 2.36% on Tuesday, unchanged for three sessions (FRED). Bond investors’ long-run inflation expectations are not moving even as the Fed worries about them.

Global central banks. Bank of Japan Governor Ueda said on Tuesday that the BoJ will keep raising rates and that conditions remain accommodative. Markets price about a 25% chance of a hike at its October 29–30 meeting (FXStreet). The dollar ended near ¥158. India’s central bank concluded its policy meeting today. Reports of the outcome conflict, one outlet reporting a hike and another no change, and we could not reach the RBI’s own statement, so we do not report a decision. We found no ECB or Bank of England remarks today. The ECB’s deposit rate stands at 2.50% after its September hike.

Science

Chemistry Nobel for the mirror-image problem. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Henri B. Kagan, emeritus of Université Paris-Sud (now part of Paris-Saclay), and Kenso Soai of the Tokyo University of Science. Press reports give the citation as “for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis.” We could not read the Academy’s own page to confirm the exact wording.

The prize concerns chirality: many molecules come in left- and right-handed forms that behave differently in the body.

  • Kagan showed that a catalyst that is only partly one-handed can still produce a product far more one-handed than expected, a “non-linear effect.”
  • Soai found a reaction whose product catalyses its own formation. It amplifies a tiny initial imbalance toward almost entirely one mirror form.

Both ideas matter for drugs, flavors and materials. They also bear on an old question: why life uses only one handedness of amino acids and sugars. Kagan was born in 1930. The prize is SEK 12 million, about $1.2 million, shared (CNN, STAT, Royal Society of Chemistry, C&EN). Literature follows tomorrow at 7:00 AM ET.

State Senate passes a bipartisan ban on PFAS in firefighting foam

The Pennsylvania Senate passed a bipartisan bill from Sens. Yaw (R) and Miller (D) on PFAS, the “forever chemicals.” It would:

  • Ban making and selling firefighting foam that contains PFAS from July 2027
  • Ban using it from 2028
  • Require warning labels on firefighter protective gear that contains PFAS

Firefighters’ long exposure to these chemicals is the bill’s central concern. The bill now goes to the House.
