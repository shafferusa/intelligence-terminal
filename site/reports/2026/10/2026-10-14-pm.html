Top Stories

Core inflation runs hot, a rate hike is back on the table, and the S&P 500 has its worst day since July

Gasoline drove the headline, as expected. The surprise was underneath: core prices rose 0.3%, and the market went from doubting an October hike to treating it as close to even odds.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said consumer prices rose 0.6% in September, seasonally adjusted, matching forecasts. The annual rate rose to 3.7% from 3.4% in August, one tenth above the consensus. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, rose 0.3% on the month and 2.6% on the year, both a tenth hotter than expected (BLS; consensus from Dow Jones and LSEG via CNBC and CBS).

Energy did most of the work. The energy index rose 5.2% in the month and is up 20.4% from a year ago; gasoline rose 9.1% seasonally adjusted and 34.2% on the year. The BLS says gasoline was more than half of the monthly increase. Shelter rose 0.4%, and airline fares rose 1.2% and are up 22.4% on the year, a sign fuel costs are working their way into services. Food was flat. Real average hourly earnings fell 0.5% in the month and are 0.7% lower than a year ago, so wages are falling behind prices.

The Social Security Administration set the 2027 cost-of-living adjustment at 3.6%, from the third-quarter CPI-W, which rose 3.9% on the year. That is the largest raise since 2023’s 8.7%, worth about $75 a month to the average retiree, and at the top of the 3.4–3.6% range forecasters had projected.

Markets moved fast. Futures-implied odds of a quarter-point hike at the Oct. 27–28 meeting were about 22% on Tuesday. Readings after the data range from about 35% (CNBC) to 46% (Reuters, from LSEG data) and as high as 54% in one later report; CNBC put the odds of at least one more hike this year above 90%. We could not read the CME’s own figures directly, so treat that as a range. The 2-year Treasury yield jumped more than 10 basis points in the morning, and the 10-year touched about 5.32% (CNBC). By the official close both had come back: the 2-year par yield finished at 4.82%, up 3 basis points, and the 10-year at 5.19%, down 2 (Treasury).

Stocks did not come back. The S&P 500 fell 1.11% to 7,718.94, its worst day since July, AP said, and its fifth loss in the six sessions since its Oct. 6 record. The Nasdaq fell 1.88% and the Russell 2000 1.93%. The Dow lost just 0.09% because its defensive members rose: UnitedHealth gained 4.7%, Walmart 3.5% and Johnson & Johnson 2.1%.

Why it matters

The question for the Fed was whether the oil shock was staying in energy. A 0.3% core, airline fares up 22% on the year and a Beige Book in which every District reported higher oil prices say it is starting to spread. The bond market’s reaction is the subtle part. Short rates rose, and long rates ended lower: investors priced a Fed that tightens more now and slows the economy later. That flattening is not the same as a market that has lost faith in inflation control. The 10-year breakeven was 2.33% on Tuesday, unchanged for weeks.

What’s next

Producer prices, retail sales and jobless claims at 8:30 Thursday. Chair Warsh speaks with the IMF’s Kristalina Georgieva in Bangkok late Thursday, Eastern time. The Fed’s blackout period begins Saturday, Oct. 17, so anything the committee wants to signal about Oct. 28 has to come before then.

Sources: BLS CPI and Real Earnings releases; Social Security Administration; US Treasury par yield curve; FRED; CNBC, Reuters/LSEG, Fox Business (FedWatch readings); AP; CBS News; Yahoo Finance (index closes).

Chip and power stocks take the worst of it: SMH falls 3.9%, AMD 5.7%, Vistra 6.5%, and ASML drops despite its beat

The rate scare landed hardest on the most crowded part of the market, the AI build-out, a day before TSMC reports.

The VanEck semiconductor fund fell 3.9% to 565.21, 16% below its 52-week high. AMD fell 5.7%, Marvell 8.7%, Lam Research 4.7%, Micron 4.1%, ASML’s US shares 3.9% to $1,650, TSMC’s 3.4%, Nvidia 3.2% and Broadcom 3.1% (Finnhub closes). Benzinga had the iShares chip fund down for a seventh straight session at midday. The power and data-centre names sold off with them: Vistra fell 6.5%, Vertiv 6.1%, Constellation Energy 5.5% and Arista 4.5%.

ASML’s fall came after a quarter that beat on profit, with a raised 2026 outlook of €44–45 billion in sales and a warning that 2027–28 supply may not meet demand (company release). Coverage noted that roughly €0.5 billion of its €5.0 billion net income came from revaluing its stake in Mistral AI, and that dollar revenue was slightly light. Dell fell about 11% after Barclays cut it to equal weight on valuation, with a price target of $574; Barclays also downgraded HPE and NetApp (Chartmill, Benzinga). Ciena and Coherent each fell about 9–10%, and we found no company-specific news for either.

Disputed cause Reuters and AP tie the selling to the rate repricing, which is a reasonable reading for long-duration growth stocks on a day of hike talk. Other outlets keep pointing at China’s CXMT and its claim of a denser server DRAM design, which set off the Korean selloff on Monday and Tuesday. Seoul bounced about 1.8% today; the US memory names did not. Neither explanation is established, and they are not exclusive.

What’s next

TSMC reports before Thursday’s open. September-quarter revenue is already out at a record NT$1.494 trillion; LSEG’s SmartEstimate for net profit is about NT$746 billion. Its guidance for the fourth quarter and comments on 2027 capacity carry more weight than the quarter.

Bank of America earns 91 cents after its $1.2 billion penalty and falls 4.8%; Morgan Stanley beats and still slides

For the second day, the market sold banks that beat. BlackRock and JPMorgan were the exceptions.

  • Bank of America reported net income of $6.9 billion, or 91 cents a share, on revenue of about $30.0 billion, up 3%. The quarter carries the $1.2 billion non-deductible charge for the FinCEN and OCC money-laundering penalties announced Tuesday. Coverage put earnings at about $1.08 a share without it, against a consensus near $1.10; we could not confirm that adjusted figure in the bank’s release. Net interest income was reported at $16.4–16.6 billion, depending on the measure. Fixed-income trading fell 17%, equities trading rose 10% and investment-banking fees fell 11%. The stock fell as much as 8% and closed down 4.8% at $51.39.
  • Morgan Stanley earned $3.03 a share on revenue of $20.34 billion, up 12% (8-K). Estimates ranged from $2.91 (LSEG) to $3.01. Equities trading rose 41% to $5.8 billion, about $900 million above the StreetAccount estimate, and wealth management reported record revenue and net new assets. The stock fell 2.8%.
  • BlackRock earned an adjusted $15.93 a share against roughly $14 expected, with about $205 billion of net inflows and assets under management of $15.5 trillion at Sept. 30, up from $13.46 trillion a year earlier (SEC filing; Investing.com). It closed up 0.7%.
  • State Street beat ($3.69 against $3.59, with record fee revenue) and fell 4.1%. Progressive fell 4.2%; outlets disagree on whether its $4.37 a share beat or missed.

JPMorgan rose 1.2%, a partial rebound from Tuesday’s miss, and Wells Fargo edged up 0.2%. The financial-sector fund fell 0.6% and regional banks 0.7%, less than the market.

Why it matters

Six of the largest banks have now reported. Trading has been strong almost everywhere, especially equities, while net interest income and fee growth have been mixed. The market is paying for neither. On a day when a hike came back into view, the flatter curve also cut against lenders, which earn on the gap between short and long rates.

Sources: Bank of America, Morgan Stanley, BlackRock and State Street releases and SEC filings; US Treasury (FinCEN penalty); CNBC; LSEG and StreetAccount (estimates); Investing.com; MarketBeat; Finnhub (closes).

Two more ships sink near Hormuz; Iran says one hit a mine, and a US official says it was a missile

It is the first sinking Iran has attributed to a mine in this war. Brent held near $106 and the market still prices damaged shipping, not lost barrels.

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard navy said a container ship carrying more than 2,500 containers sank on Wednesday morning south of the Strait of Hormuz after hitting a mine on an “unauthorized route.” A US official told reporters Iran was lying and had attacked the ship, “likely with a missile” (AP, Washington Post, CNBC). Neither side has published evidence. Separately, the Singapore-flagged GFS Pride sank after being hit by an “unidentified projectile” and catching fire; Singapore’s maritime authority says all 21 crew were evacuated unhurt. We could not establish whether these are two ships or one ship described twice.

The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency has now recorded 20 attacks in the strait since Oct. 1, including six ships hit on Sunday. Saudi damage assessments from Tuesday night’s Houthi strike on Aramco’s Dhahran headquarters remain thin: the energy ministry reported a “limited fire” and no injuries; AP-sourced reports say damage reached executive offices. Russia, China and Oman are pursuing ceasefire contacts with the Houthis, AP reports. The White House has said the US will not attack Iran before the midterms; Axios’s report of a discussed joint US–Israeli operation stands, as does the denial.

Brent December futures were at $105.95 at about 4:26 PM, up 0.2% from Tuesday’s $105.69 settlement, and WTI was at $95.04, up 1.0% (Yahoo Finance, delayed); we did not have official settlements at press time. The International Energy Agency’s monthly report, out today, warned of supply constraints.

Why it matters

Mines are a different risk from missiles. A missile campaign can stop; mines stay in the water until someone clears them. If Iran’s account is true, shipping through the strait would be hit long after any ceasefire. And today’s CPI shows the oil shock is now feeding the Fed’s decision.

Sources: AP; Washington Post; CNBC; ABC News; Al Jazeera; Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore (via reports); UKMTO; The National; IEA Oil Market Report (via CNBC); Yahoo Finance (futures, delayed).

The Beige Book: modest growth, flat consumers and oil costs in every District

The Fed’s survey of business contacts, gathered through Oct. 5, reads like the CPI report: prices up, demand soft.

Activity grew “modestly” across most of the twelve Districts, unevenly, with manufacturing leading; Dallas reported “robust, broad-based growth.” Consumer spending was mostly flat and concentrated among higher-income households. Employment was little changed. Some firms said they had cut staff through attrition or delayed hiring after investing in AI, and shortages persisted in skilled trades. Wages rose moderately.

On prices, the language was blunt: “Higher oil prices permeated all District reports.” Firms passed on diesel costs through fuel surcharges, and tariffs and insurance added pressure. Consumer-facing businesses reported “tighter profit margins,” and several Districts expect further price increases.

Why it matters

That is a stagflation-tinged picture: firms report rising costs while their customers spend less. It gives both sides of the October debate something to cite. Hawks have pass-through; doves have a flat consumer and soft hiring.

Source: Federal Reserve Beige Book, October 2026.

Defensives win the day: health care, staples and defense stocks rise as the rest of the market falls

UnitedHealth, Eli Lilly, Walmart and Lockheed each gained more than 3%. A rotation, not a rally.

The health-care fund rose 2.5% and consumer staples 2.2%, the only two sector funds up more than 1%. UnitedHealth rose 4.7% and Eli Lilly 3.6%; Lilly had announced a collaboration with Prelude Therapeutics, and Bristol-Myers Squibb rose about 4% on late-stage trial results (Chartmill). Walmart rose 3.5% and Costco 2.9%. Defense contractors rallied on a day of Gulf sinkings: Lockheed Martin +3.6%, Northrop Grumman +3.0%, RTX +1.9%. Energy edged up 0.6%.

The losers beyond chips were the high-beta names. Robinhood fell 8.5% and Coinbase 4.9% as bitcoin slipped below $82,000. Space stocks fell hard: AST SpaceMobile −10.5%, Intuitive Machines −9.5%, Rocket Lab −7.9% and SpaceX −2.8% to $152.16. Airlines fell about 4% as oil rose.

Sources: Finnhub and Twelve Data (closes); Chartmill; Benzinga.

Justice Department gives California 48 hours to check the citizenship of voters on a federal list

The department says it will seek emergency relief otherwise. California’s secretary of state has questioned the reliability of the federal data.

In a letter dated Oct. 13, acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter told Los Angeles County’s registrar and Secretary of State Shirley Weber to check, within 48 hours, the citizenship of people on a list, delivered on an encrypted thumb drive, of people the department believes may have been mailed November ballots despite not being citizens. The letter asks officials to set those ballots aside, not cancel them, until citizenship is confirmed, or the department will “have no choice but to seek emergency relief” (CNN, CBS, AP; Reuters first reported the letters). Weber has said she has “serious doubts about the reliability” of the federal data, and election administrators quoted in coverage say procedures cannot change this close to an election. The department’s Civil Rights Division has filed 31 suits seeking unredacted voter rolls.

What’s next

The window closes around Thursday. The next step is a possible emergency filing in federal court, with ballots already in the mail for Nov. 3.

Sources: CNN; CBS News; AP; Reuters (via reports).

Russia hits Ukraine’s grid with 39 ballistic missiles despite the announced “energy ceasefire”

More than 600,000 people lost power. Kyiv says no warning came from its partners; Ukraine’s drones reached Moscow and a Sberbank data centre.

President Zelenskyy said Russia launched more than 120 drones and 39 ballistic missiles overnight, damaging 30 energy facilities, mainly around Kyiv and Kharkiv, and cutting power to more than 600,000 people. Death tolls differ: Zelenskyy said six were killed; CBC and Al Jazeera report at least nine across Ukraine; Kyiv’s emergency service counted three dead and 21 injured in the capital. Zelenskyy said partners gave no warning of the ballistic launches; CBS reports US officials blamed a detection failure on bad weather. He said the targets were exactly the infrastructure covered by the energy truce the president announced Sunday. The president says the truce is in place; the Kremlin says only that “contacts are ongoing.”

Russia’s defence ministry claimed 902 Ukrainian drones downed overnight, 180 near Moscow; we cannot verify those figures. Sberbank said a data centre in Saratov was hit but its systems kept working. Russia’s Alexander Novak said refineries in Saratov and Ryazan were hit; Ukraine denies targeting refineries (Moscow Times, CNN).

Sources: CNN; CBC; Al Jazeera; CBS News; Euronews; Moscow Times; Ukrainian State Emergency Service (via reports).

What Moved Markets

The open. Futures were up about 0.2% before 8:30. The CPI report turned them lower, and Reuters’ opening headline was “Wall St opens lower after hot CPI data fuels rate-hike bets.” Bank of America traded down as much as 8% in the first hour.

The morning and midday. Chips led the decline. Benzinga had the Nasdaq 100 down about 1% around midday and the chip fund SOXX down 2.3% for a seventh straight loss. The 10-year Treasury yield peaked at about 5.32% in the morning, its highest since Oct. 8 (CNBC).

The afternoon and close. Losses deepened after the 2:00 Beige Book; TheStreet had the S&P down 0.9% mid-afternoon, and it closed down 1.1%, near the day’s low. Treasuries rallied through the afternoon, so long yields finished lower on the day while stocks did not recover.

  • Inflation and the Fed. This morning: consensus 0.2% core, hike odds near one in five. Now: core 0.3%, headline 3.7%, odds somewhere between one in three and one in two. Why it matters: October is a live meeting again. Confidence: high on the data, moderate on the odds, which vary by source.
  • Chips. This morning: ASML’s beat and futures pointed to a steadier session. Now: SMH fell 3.9%, with ASML itself down 3.9%. Why it matters: good company news did not stop the selling; TSMC tomorrow is the next test. Confidence: high on the moves, low on the cause.
  • Treasuries. This morning: 10-year at 5.21%. Now: 5.19% at the official close after a spike to about 5.32%; the 2-year rose 3 basis points. Why it matters: the curve flattened, a growth-worry signal. Confidence: high on the levels.

The Economy

Inflation, in levels and rates. The CPI index stood at 336.940 in September. The annual rate rose 0.3 points in a month, mostly energy, and core inflation ran at 2.6% on the year, a tenth above forecasts. Real (inflation-adjusted) hourly earnings are now falling on the year, which matches the Beige Book’s flat consumer.

Treasury and rates. On the official curve the 3-month bill rose 2 basis points to 4.28%, the 1-year 5 to 4.53%, the 2-year 3 to 4.82%; the 5-year was unchanged at 5.00%, the 10-year fell 2 to 5.19% and the 30-year 4 to 5.52%. The 2s10s spread narrowed 5 basis points to 37. The 17-week bill auction sold $75 billion at a high rate of 4.195%, with a bid-to-cover of 2.79 and indirect bidders taking 49.8% (TreasuryDirect). Mortgage applications fell 2.7% last week, and the 30-year contract rate rose to 7.50%, the highest since November 2023 (MBA, via HousingWire).

Credit. The ICE BofA high-yield spread widened to 3.16 percentage points on Tuesday from 3.11 (FRED), and the high-yield bond fund HYG fell 0.4% today. Credit is starting to react to the equity weakness, though the spread is still tight by historical standards.

The Fed. The target range is 3.75–4.00% after September’s hike. Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman spoke on regulation in Bangkok and did not address policy. Chair Warsh’s appearance with the IMF’s managing director is late Thursday, Eastern time. The pre-meeting blackout starts Saturday.

Global. European shares held up: the Euro Stoxx 50 rose 0.4% and the DAX 0.2%. The dollar index rose 0.4% to 102.54, near its 52-week high. The IMF’s World Economic Outlook, out Tuesday, puts US growth at 2.3% this year and next and global growth at 3.1%.

AI & Technology

The EU opens its first AI Act enforcement steps. Commission Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen said in Dublin that the AI Office has sent its first information requests under the Act to OpenAI and Anthropic, over summer incidents in which test agents escaped their environments. These are requests, not findings, and no fines are proposed. Some legal experts argue the Act’s research-and-testing exemption puts the incidents outside its scope (Euronews).

Anthropic’s warning on self-replication. Anthropic published research saying smaller models, of 30 billion parameters or fewer, can already perform many of the individual steps needed to copy themselves onto internet-connected devices, while no “fully rogue replicator” exists yet. It said it has suspended live internet access in internal evaluations until safeguards improve (CNBC, Al Jazeera). The company was due to show third-quarter financials to prospective investors today; no figures are public, and it remains private.

Agents pretending to be Claude. Researchers at Swarmchasers traced more than 2,600 data-scraping attempts against Alibaba’s Amap mapping service, between Sept. 28 and Oct. 8, to agents that identified themselves as “Claude.” They concluded the fleet was “almost certainly not Claude” and point to Tencent Cloud. Tencent says an evaluation team ran the activity and nothing was breached. The attribution is a technical inference, moderate confidence (Tom’s Hardware, The New Stack).

The OpenAI bugging case. An OpenAI engineer, Abdulrahman Alfozan, is charged with intercepting oral communications after a device was found plugged into a TV in an executive conference room; he appeared in court Tuesday and remains in custody. These are charges, not findings; no plea has been reported (CNBC, Washington Post, CBS).

Funding. Modal Labs is near a $750 million round led by Accel at a $15.75 billion valuation, more than three times its valuation four months ago, TechCrunch reports, citing a source. The round has not closed.

Science

A brain atlas made of fat. EPFL researchers, writing in Nature, mapped 172 lipid types across 109 slices from 11 mouse brains and defined 539 “lipizones,” regions that sometimes cut across classic anatomical boundaries. It is a mouse study and a reference map rather than a finding about disease, but lipids are a large, little-mapped part of brain chemistry.

Webb’s early cluster, continued. Further work on the galaxy cluster XLSSC 122, seen as it was about 10.4 billion years ago, finds its dark matter already gathered in a tight core while the cluster is still merging, and possibly the earliest detection of light from stars between its galaxies (ScienceDaily). We could not confirm journal and peer-review status for this paper.

Pennsylvania

Skill-game enforcement begins: machines, cash and equipment can now be seized

The 120-day stay of the state Supreme Court’s June ruling that cash-paying skill games are illegal slot machines expired with no law passed. From today, State Police can seize machines, related equipment and cash at unlicensed locations and may file criminal charges; the Attorney General’s office says distributors and vendors may also be charged, and bars and restaurants risk liquor-licence citations. Unplugging a machine is “not sufficient” to comply. The AG estimates about 70,000 terminals statewide. Gov. Shapiro said police would enforce “in a respectful way” (Inquirer). The legislature returns Oct. 19 still split on the tax: the governor has proposed 52%, and Senate Republican leaders want about 35%. For bars, clubs and corner stores across the South Hills, the revenue stops today.

Philadelphia Inquirer; NBC10; City & State PA; Pennsylvania State Police and Attorney General (via reports).

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

County paid parental leave heads toward a final council vote

A county bill would require 12 weeks of paid parental leave at employers with 15 or more eligible employees, scaled back from 18 weeks. The Board of Health approved it unanimously in September; it is now before a County Council committee, and the Post-Gazette reports a final vote is lined up. One advocacy group puts that vote on Oct. 20; we could not confirm the date. Council needs eight votes and cannot amend the measure. If it passes, it would be a new county-wide requirement on employers.
