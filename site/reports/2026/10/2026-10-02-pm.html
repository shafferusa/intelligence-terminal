Top Stories

Payrolls rise just 29,000 and unemployment hits 4.2%, but long Treasury yields end the day higher

The three-month average for job growth is now about 51,000. The 2-year yield fell about 5bp in the first minutes and then closed 5bp higher. The bond market treated a weak labor report as a reason to stop pricing an October hike, not as a reason to buy duration.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported at 8:30 that nonfarm payrolls rose by 29,000 in September. Forecasts had clustered between 84,000 and 98,000. Revisions made it weaker still:

  • July: revised to −10,000, from +21,000
  • August: revised to +133,000, from +162,000
  • Combined: 60,000 fewer jobs than previously reported

The three-month average is now about 51,000 a month. Unemployment rose to 4.2% from 4.1%, with about 7.1 million people unemployed. Part of the rise came from more people entering the labor force: participation rose to 61.8%, its highest in four months, and the broader U-6 underemployment rate fell to 7.6% from 7.7%. Average hourly earnings rose 5 cents, or 0.1%, to $37.81. That is 3.0% above a year ago, the slowest annual pace since 2021 and below the 0.3% monthly and 3.2% annual gains expected. Health care added 17,000 jobs, about half its average of the past year. Construction added 11,000, manufacturing 9,000, and financial activities lost 7,000. We read the BLS release and FRED series directly. The figures are seasonally adjusted, and the level of payrolls, 159.04 million, is only about 150,000 above June.

The bond market’s reaction came in two halves. Yields fell at 8:30, and the 10-year touched about 5.16%. From late morning they rose steadily, and the 10-year peaked near 5.30% around 1:30 PM. Treasury’s official closes:

  • 2-year: 4.83%, up 5bp
  • 10-year: 5.28%, up 4bp
  • 30-year: 5.63%, up 2bp

For the week, the 10-year rose 11bp and the 30-year 14bp. Market narrative Traders and commentators pointed to several factors: Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan’s call on Thursday evening for 50bp or more of further hikes, oil recovering from its morning drop, and next week’s auctions. None of them is confirmed as the cause. Germany’s 10-year Bund yield fell about 6bp on the day (Reuters), so the afternoon rise in Treasuries was a US move, not a global one.

Why it matters A soft jobs report with weak wages is the textbook case for lower yields. The front end did reprice the Fed: October hike odds roughly halved. But the 10-year closed higher on the day and the week. That says the long end is being priced on supply, term premium and inflation risk from oil, not on US growth. That leaves equity valuations exposed if the auctions go badly.
What’s next ISM services Monday. The $58 billion 3-year Tuesday, the $39 billion 10-year reopening and the September FOMC minutes Wednesday, and the $22 billion 30-year Thursday. September CPI is October 14.

How this morning’s calls did. We gave a 55–70% chance that the first print would be below 100,000, and it was. We gave 25–40% for unemployment at 4.2% or higher, and it was 4.2%. We said the tape’s most fragile assumption was that a soft US report could coexist with a stable 10-year. What would break it, we wrote, was the 10-year rising toward 5.35% on a soft payrolls day. It rose to 5.30% intraday and closed at 5.28%, short of that line but in that direction. These calls are graded formally on Saturday.

Real pay. Hourly pay is up 3.0% from a year earlier. Core PCE inflation was about 3.0% in August, so real wage growth is roughly zero. That weakens the “wage-price” argument for more hikes, but August CPI rose 0.40% on the month, and the Fed has said energy pass-through is its worry.

The curve. 2s10s is 45bp (from 46), 5s30s 57bp, and 3-month bills to 10-year 109bp. The 10-year real yield was 2.88% on Thursday and the 10-year breakeven 2.36% (FRED, October 1). Today’s split between those two is not yet published.

October hike odds collapse, but markets still lean toward a December move

The jobs report took most of the October risk off the table. Logan’s call for at least 50bp more kept December on it.

After the report, CNBC put the probability of a quarter-point hike at the October 27–28 meeting at about 17%, from about 36% a week earlier. Kalshi’s market showed about 18%, and Reuters said less than 15%. The figures differ by time of day. A hike by December is still priced as more likely than not: CNBC put it above 75% and Kalshi at about 65%. The funds rate is 3.75–4.00% after September’s 12–0 hike.

On Thursday evening Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said the target range “needs to rise an additional 50 basis points or more.” She also said inflation is unlikely to fall much below 2.5% without further tightening (Reuters, Investing.com, FXStreet). We found no Fed remarks today that changed the picture.

Why it matters A front end that eases while the long end does not is a bear steepener. It tells the Fed that financial conditions are tightening without its help, which is an argument for patience in October. FOMC minutes Wednesday will show how many officials wanted to go further in September.

The G7 agrees to release up to 100 million barrels, and Brent ends the day where it began

The release is front-loaded with diesel. Brent fell below $100 this morning and then recovered every cent of the drop, which says the market is pricing Hormuz, not stockpiles.

G7 countries and partners agreed through the International Energy Agency to release up to 100 million barrels of crude oil and diesel over four months, “with a frontloaded substantial diesel release within the first 20 days,” according to the joint statement reported by CNBC, NBC, BNN Bloomberg and The National. Separately, the EU is weighing its own release of about 50 million barrels, roughly 17% of its emergency diesel stocks, over 20 days. That follows US pressure on France and Germany and the threat of a US diesel-export restriction. The EU figure is not final.

Prices did not hold the move. WTI touched a one-month low near $88 (Trading Economics) and settled about $1.76 lower at $91.11. Brent traded near $99.70 this morning and settled at $102.25, down 6 cents, according to a wire report. Those settlement figures are from a single source, and Yahoo’s front-month quotes after 4 PM were close to them. The oil fund USO fell 1.8%.

Why it matters A 100-million-barrel release is about one day of world consumption spread over four months. It helps diesel, the fuel behind Europe’s 18.8% energy inflation, more than it helps crude. Brent finishing flat means traders are still pricing a closed Strait of Hormuz and a US military buildup that is due to be complete by November (see The World).

Tesla delivers 486,532 vehicles, beating its own consensus by about 24,500

Deliveries ran about 22,000 above production, drawing down inventory. The shares rose 4.7%.

Tesla’s release, filed as an 8-K exhibit, reported third-quarter deliveries of 486,532: 478,237 Model 3 and Model Y and 8,295 other models. Production was 464,391, and energy-storage deployments were 13.7 gigawatt-hours. The company-compiled consensus was 461,974 vehicles and 15.9 GWh. Deliveries were about 2% below the record 497,099 a year earlier. Storage came in below its consensus. Morgan Stanley wrote that Tesla may be “exiting the EV winter” (CNBC). The shares closed at $370.59, up 4.65%, on about 1.4 times normal volume. Third-quarter results are due October 21.

This morning we gave a 45–60% chance that deliveries would come in below the consensus. They did not; the forecast was wrong and will be graded Saturday.

ON Semiconductor cuts its Synaptics price to $123 a share, and both stocks jump

The amended all-cash deal values Synaptics at about $5.7 billion, against about $7 billion under the earlier terms. The revision followed an unsolicited third-party proposal.

ON Semiconductor filed an 8-K disclosing an amended merger agreement signed October 1. Synaptics holders will now receive $123 a share in cash. The filing says the terms were revised after Synaptics received an unsolicited proposal from a third party, which it does not name. Closing is still expected in mid-2027. Synaptics rose 14.1% to $121.10, below the cash price, and ON rose 6.0% to $84.89. A cheaper, all-cash deal pleased ON’s holders. The gap between Synaptics’ price and $123 reflects a long wait and regulatory risk.

The Economy

Labor. Payrolls rose 29,000 in September, and the July and August revisions subtracted 60,000. Unemployment rose to 4.2%, a level, not yet a trend. It was 4.2% in June too. Participation rose to 61.8% from 61.6%, so part of the rise in unemployment reflects people coming back into the labor force rather than layoffs, and U-6 fell to 7.6%. Jobless claims, at 197,000 last week, still show few layoffs. The picture is low hiring and low firing, with pay slowing. Average hourly earnings rose 0.1% in September and 3.0% from a year earlier, the slowest since 2021. The workweek held at 34.4 hours.

Factory orders. August orders rose 0.1% to $663.5 billion, after a revised 0.8% gain in July (Census, primary). Core capital-goods orders, nondefense excluding aircraft, rose 1.6%, a strong reading for business investment that is consistent with the AI capital-spending boom. Civilian aircraft orders fell 4.3% (Reuters). Durable goods slipped 0.1%.

Autos. One industry tracker puts September’s seasonally adjusted annual sales rate at about 16.4 million (Headlight.news, single source). That is above the 16.1–16.3 million forecasts and below August’s 16.8 million.

The Fed. The funds rate is 3.75–4.00%. The soft report cut October hike odds to roughly 15–18%. Logan’s call on Thursday for at least 50bp more is the hawkish marker. Williams and Bowman speak Tuesday, Logan and Williams again Wednesday, Musalem Thursday and Collins Friday, according to one published schedule we could not check against the Fed’s calendar. SOFR was 3.87% on Thursday (FRED).

Science

Nobel week starts Monday. The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet announces the prize in physiology or medicine on Monday, October 5, no earlier than 11:30 AM in Stockholm (5:30 AM Eastern). Physics, chemistry, literature, peace and economics follow through the week (nobelprize.org).

The bat family tree, redrawn. This week’s issue of Nature carries genomes for 103 bat species. The authors use them to argue that bats arose about 60 to 65 million years ago, in Europe. That is just after the asteroid impact that ended the age of dinosaurs. A genome set this broad is a resource as much as a finding: bats carry viruses that spill over into people, and better genomes help explain why the animals tolerate them. We saw the paper’s summary, not the full text.

Also in the News

An Atlantic hurricane season without a hurricane, so far. October has arrived with no Atlantic hurricane yet this year, which forecasters describe as a first of the satellite era. The season has produced eight named storms, against a normal of about eleven storms and five hurricanes by now. The National Hurricane Center expects no new storms in the next seven days. Forecasters attribute the quiet to a very strong El Niño, whose upper-level winds tear developing storms apart (NHC, Tampa Bay Times). For Gulf energy production and insurers, that is the cheapest hurricane season in years.

Pittsburgh region

Duquesne Light strike, day two: talks were expected to resume, and rain tests the contingency crews

Nearly 1,000 members of IBEW Local 29, linemen and customer-service staff, are on the second day of their strike against Duquesne Light. WPXI reported no deal on the first day. Union members said negotiations were expected to resume today; we found no report on how they went. The company’s description of its offer is now more specific: double-digit raises over the life of the contract and 120 more union positions. The union’s business manager, Josh Ewing, says the dispute is about staffing, equipment and safety rather than pay. He says the company has tripled its non-union workforce and leans on contractors.

Non-union employees and contractors, some from out of state, are covering the work under PUC-approved contingency plans, and picket lines are staffed around the clock. Thursday was dry and calm, and outages were restored quickly. Today’s rain is the first real test. The utility serves about 610,000 customers in Allegheny and Beaver counties, Bridgeville and the South Hills included. We found no statement from the PUC or local officials.

Skill games: three session days left before the October 13 deadline

Lawmakers met three days this week without taking up skill games. Both chambers return Monday for three more session days before the Supreme Court’s deadline. From October 14, State Police say, about 70,000 unlicensed machines in bars, stores and clubs can be seized and their owners charged, and Attorney General Dave Sunday has urged businesses to remove them.
