Top Stories

The Houthis claim the Riyadh airport strikes, a second missile sets a terminal on fire, and Iran calls the US reply on Hormuz “insufficient”

Three fronts of the Gulf war moved at once over the weekend. None of them is an attack on oil infrastructure, which is why Brent is up less than 1%, not 5%.

Yemen’s Houthis claimed responsibility on Sunday for the attacks on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh. Their military spokesman, Yahya Saree, described at least four operations against the airport since Saturday and called them retaliation for “Saudi aggression,” according to France 24, Al Jazeera and CBS. That settles the question this paper left open on Sunday. Saudi Arabia’s civil aviation authority still puts Saturday’s toll at 12 killed and 309 wounded. India’s embassy said a wounded Indian national later died, and CNN now counts “at least 13.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio confirmed that one American was killed. US and Gulf officials told CBC the weapon was a cruise missile.

A second projectile hit the airport’s Terminal 4 on Sunday afternoon, local time, and set it on fire, according to Al Arabiya, the Saudi Gazette, Middle East Eye and others. The authority confirmed injuries but has not given a number. The coalition says its air defences shot down another cruise missile near the airport minutes later. The airport stays closed today, with several hundred flights cancelled. Saudi Arabia called the attacks a “war crime” and promised to “respond decisively.” Houthi-run media say coalition jets struck Sanaa airport on Sunday. That is the Houthis’ account; Reuters said it could not verify it.

Washington has not struck. The president said on Saturday that the US “may” join Saudi strikes and would “look at it.” Axios, citing a US official, reports that the US is giving the Saudis intelligence and targeting support but is “not going to take kinetic action for now.” CNN reports that the president turned down two Saudi requests for US strikes last month.

On Iran, Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, called the US reply to Tehran’s seven conditions for reopening Hormuz “insufficient” (Haaretz, Times of Israel). He said Washington had accepted some conditions and left others unclear, that Iran is “no longer in the process of responding,” and that the strait stays closed to US ships. He also threatened undersea cables and AI data centres if fighting resumes. Iran’s foreign ministry struck a softer note, saying it is still reviewing the proposal and that messages continue through mediators. The White House did not comment. At sea, the UK’s maritime agency reported another outbound tanker hit by a projectile on Sunday, and the UAE National Guard rescued 22 crew from a burning tanker. Unconfirmed Iran’s Revolutionary Guard claims a supertanker hit a mine after leaving Hormuz by an “unauthorized” route; no one else has confirmed it.

Why it matters The president’s pledge of no strike on Iran before Nov. 3 does not cover Yemen. A US–Houthi exchange is a different war from a US–Iran one, but it lands in the same oil market. For now the market reads the weekend as more of the same: Brent is $105.59 at about 6:20, up 0.8% from Friday’s $104.72 settlement, and gold is slightly lower. JPMorgan estimates Gulf exports at 17.5 million barrels a day, 98% of pre-war levels (via Gulf News). Supply is flowing; what traders are paying for is the risk that it stops. Exposure, not direction: oil producers, refiners, airlines, defence.
What’s next Any US decision on Yemen; whether the Saudis retaliate inside Yemen at scale; the airport’s reopening; whether Iran’s security council or its foreign ministry speaks for Tehran on Hormuz; and any strike on Gulf oil or gas facilities, which is the line that would move Brent by dollars rather than cents.

Trump declares a Russia–Ukraine “energy ceasefire”; Russia hits a Kharkiv power plant hours later and the Kremlin has not confirmed it

If it holds, it would ease the diesel squeeze from Ukraine’s refinery strikes. So far it rests on one social-media post.

The president posted on Truth Social on Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an immediate “energy ceasefire.” He gave no details, and the White House referred questions to the post (CNBC, Axios, CBS, Time). The Kremlin said only that “contacts are ongoing” (NBC). President Zelensky said he learned of it from the media, and that Ukraine would stop striking Russian refineries if Russia stopped striking Ukraine’s power system. Hours after the post, Russia struck the CHP-5 power plant in Kharkiv (Kyiv Independent, RBC-Ukraine). On Monday Zelensky said Russia was ignoring the call: “Putin doesn’t read Truth Social” (Forbes). Time notes the president made a similar announcement last month that neither side confirmed.

The announcement came the day after the president said Ukraine should “get a new president,” blaming its refinery strikes for high diesel prices. Zelensky replied that this is “up to our people” and that elections could be held if Russia agreed to a 60-day ceasefire. Axios reports the US threatened to stop arms sales through NATO channels if Kyiv kept hitting refineries. The Treasury licence for Russian diesel exports runs to April 7, 2027. Ukraine’s drones knocked a Yandex data centre in Vladimir offline overnight Saturday, the third Yandex site hit in four days (AP).

Why it matters For markets the question is diesel. Ukrainian strikes have cut Russian refinery output and fuel exports. A real halt would ease the distillate prices behind Delta’s fuel warning and the recent rise in inflation expectations. Reuters linked an early dip in crude to the announcement, but oil has since recovered. Until Moscow says yes, treat this as an announcement, not an agreement.

Seoul’s chip giants drop more than 4% as Korea reopens, a warning shot before TSMC and ASML report

The Kospi fell 3.65% in its first session since Thursday, its tenth straight day of foreign selling. US chip futures are quiet.

South Korea’s Kospi closed at 6,384.24, down 3.65% from Thursday. Korea was shut Friday for Hangeul Day, so this was its first chance to trade last week’s AI wobble. Samsung Electronics fell 4.39% and SK hynix 4.52%, according to Korean market reports, and foreign investors sold a net 2.02 trillion won (about $1.4 billion), their tenth straight day of net selling. Elsewhere in Asia the move was contained: Hong Kong rose 0.25%, Shanghai slipped 0.44%, Sydney gained 0.8% and Tokyo was closed.

The trigger was in the US. On Oct. 8 the Financial Times reported that OpenAI told investors its annualised revenue was about $50 billion at the end of September, against the roughly $70 billion that had circulated. The Philadelphia chip index fell 3.4% that day. CNN reports that most of the gap is how partner sales are counted, and Bloomberg reported Friday that OpenAI expects to reach $70 billion or more by the end of 2026. US chip stocks recovered only partly: SMH, the semiconductor ETF, fell 0.65% on Friday and is down 4.8% from its Oct. 5 close. TSMC’s US shares are down 6.7% and ASML’s 4.3% over the same four sessions.

Why it matters Samsung and SK hynix make the high-bandwidth memory inside AI accelerators, so foreign selling in Seoul is a direct read on AI-spending confidence. ASML reports Wednesday and TSMC Thursday. TSMC’s monthly sales already point to a quarter at or above its guidance, so its outlook and capital spending will matter more than the quarter itself. Exposure: NVDA, MU, AVGO, AMD, TSM, ASML, the memory makers.

Economics Nobel goes to Robert Hall and Michael Woodford, whose work underpins how central banks set rates

The citation is “for the microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation.” Woodford’s framework is the one the Fed still uses to think about guidance and expectations.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2026 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences this morning to Robert E. Hall of Stanford and Michael Woodford of Columbia. The prize is worth 12 million Swedish kronor, shared (CNN, CNBC, Japan Times). Woodford’s 2003 book Interest and Prices is the standard statement of the “New Keynesian” model behind inflation targeting and forward guidance: the idea that what a central bank says about future rates matters as much as today’s rate. Hall’s work runs from consumption and the labour market to how business cycles are dated; he long chaired the committee that dates US recessions.

It closes a Nobel week that also honoured Navi Pillay for peace, Anne Carson for literature, and IceCube’s Francis Halzen for physics.

Why it matters The prize rewards the toolkit the Fed is using right now. Its September hike was explained as heading off an energy shock’s spread into expectations, which is Woodford’s logic.

Isaias’s death toll stands at four as power returns. The Gulf’s offshore oil restart has still not been reported

The last official count, from Friday, had 71.5% of Gulf of Mexico oil output shut in. The two refineries nearest the landfall appear to have been spared.

At least four people died, two in Florida and two in Alabama (NPR, CBC, ABC). Outages peaked near 900,000 customers across Florida, Alabama and Georgia and were about 390,000 on Sunday morning. Gov. Ron DeSantis said 95% of Florida Power & Light outages should be restored by tonight. Emergency declarations cover the three states; no major-disaster declaration has been reported.

The federal offshore regulator’s last update, on Friday, had 1.46 million barrels a day of oil (71.5%) and 1.26 billion cubic feet a day of gas (58.8%) shut in, with 129 of 371 staffed platforms evacuated. No weekend update has been published. Chevron said its Pascagoula refinery kept running, and there are no damage reports from Vertex’s Mobile plant. GasBuddy’s Patrick De Haan expects output to “resume significantly by early next week.”

Why it matters The storm missed the refineries, which was the bigger risk. Offshore shut-ins usually reverse within days, so the restart figures should show supply returning this week. Natural gas futures are down 2.5% this morning.

SpaceX’s spectrum deal hits telecoms in Australia too; the American carriers’ losses face their first follow-through test

Friday was T-Mobile’s worst day since 2013 and AT&T’s worst since 2000. Telstra fell 2.5% in Sydney on Monday.

SpaceX agreed on Oct. 8 to buy Grain Management’s nationwide 800 MHz licences, up to 14 MHz paired, to make Starlink Mobile a standalone US carrier. The deal needs FCC approval; Chairman Brendan Carr called the added competition “really good news.” Terms were not disclosed. Single source The Wall Street Journal reports about $8 billion in cash. T-Mobile’s filings show Grain paid $2.9 billion for the spectrum in August. On Friday T-Mobile fell 13.3%, AT&T 10.8% and Verizon 10.1% (closes, Yahoo Finance). Tower owners rose: Crown Castle 15.6% and American Tower 9.3%. SpaceX itself (SPCX) rose 1.25% to $162.57. AST SpaceMobile, a would-be satellite-to-phone rival, fell 10.5%. In Sydney on Monday, Telstra fell about 2.5% and TPG about 4.9%, with local reports citing the same threat (Market Index, ABC).

Why it matters The market is pricing a fourth national network, with a satellite layer the others don’t have. The question for today is whether Friday’s 10–13% losses were the whole adjustment. FINRA data show 57–59% of Friday’s AT&T and Verizon volume was short sales, above the market’s 51.6%. That is a sign of active trading, not of short interest, which is reported with a two-week lag.

The Justice Department opens an antitrust probe of the five TV networks over their pool boycott

The Antitrust Division has sent civil investigative demands to ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox and NBC. It is examining whether their Sept. 21 suspension of White House pool coverage was an illegal “group boycott” under the Sherman Act (first reported by The New York Times; confirmed by NBC, CNN, NPR and CBS). The pool has since resumed. The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press called the probe a “pretext.” The department has not described the investigation publicly. Separately, Judge Timothy Kelly is weighing whether to extend an injunction against the White House’s ban on CNN, MS NOW and Politico. The stage is investigation: no charge or lawsuit has been filed.

A federal judge temporarily blocks the Homeland Security Department’s $20 million TV ad campaign

Judge Jennifer Rochon of the Southern District of New York issued a temporary restraining order on Sunday, effective 6:45 p.m., halting the department’s roughly $20 million television campaign for two weeks (CNN, CNBC, The Hill). The administration calls them public-service announcements and says the campaign was ending anyway. About $19 million had already been spent. It is the first known court order against the ads.

US, UK agencies tie a wide hacking campaign to a Beijing company working for Chinese state actors

CISA, the FBI, the NSA and Britain’s National Cyber Security Centre published joint advisory AA26-281A on Oct. 8. It attributes, with the governments’ formal backing, a campaign against government, manufacturing, health-care and IT networks to Chinese state-linked actors supported by Integrity Technology Group, a Beijing company. The techniques are ordinary but effective: password spraying against Microsoft Exchange, botnets of hijacked devices and persistence on VPN appliances. The advisory’s practical advice is to patch edge devices and enforce multi-factor authentication. Separately, the ransomware group “TheGentlemen” claims to have hit MoneyGram and BigID. Science

NASA moves ahead on an all-glass telescope for the LISA gravitational-wave mission

NASA said on Oct. 9 that it is proceeding with a test telescope for LISA, the joint ESA–NASA mission that will measure gravitational waves with lasers fired between three spacecraft millions of kilometres apart. L3Harris is designing and building it. Glass keeps its shape as temperatures change, so the optics can hold the picometre stability the measurement needs. LISA is planned to launch in the mid-2030s and will listen to merging supermassive black holes that ground detectors cannot hear. Last week the Webb telescope also measured the distance to the most distant fast radio burst yet found, a source of millisecond radio flashes whose cause is still debated.
