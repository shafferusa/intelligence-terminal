Top Stories

Delta misses its own range and cuts its full-year outlook by about a fifth as jet fuel climbs

Demand held up: revenue was a September-quarter record. Fuel is what changed. Delta now expects to pay about $4.25 a gallon this quarter, against the $3.15 it assumed in July for last quarter, and calls the year a $6 billion fuel-cost increase.

Delta Air Lines reported adjusted earnings of $1.72 a share for the September quarter this morning, below the $2.00–2.50 range it gave in July. Analyst forecasts compiled by several outlets ranged from about $1.76 to $1.96, so it is a miss on those too. GAAP earnings were $1.15 a share. Adjusted operating margin was 9.4%, against the 11–13% Delta guided in July.

The revenue side was strong. Adjusted revenue was a record $17.6 billion, up 16% on flat capacity; GAAP revenue was $20.2 billion. Unit revenue rose 15%, with domestic up 16% and Latin America up 22%. Chief executive Ed Bastian said pre-tax profit of $1.5 billion matched last year’s.

The outlook is what moves the story. For the December quarter Delta expects revenue up about 20% and earnings of $1.15–1.65 a share, with an operating margin of 7–9%. For the full year it now expects $5.10–5.60 a share and about $2.5 billion of free cash flow. In July it expected $6.50–7.50 and $3–4 billion. At the midpoint, that is a cut of about 23% to earnings guidance. The new quarter assumes fuel at the October 2 forward curve, about $4.25 a gallon all-in, including a refinery benefit of about 40 cents. Delta says it will absorb “a $6 billion increase in fuel costs” for the year and still make about $4.5 billion pre-tax. Delta shares closed Thursday at $82.14, before the release. Pre-market trading had not been reported by the time this was written.

Why it matters This is the first big third-quarter report to show what months of $100-plus Brent are doing to a fuel-heavy business. Customers are absorbing higher fares, since unit revenue is up 15%, but not enough to offset fuel. Watch for the same pattern in other airlines, chemicals and freight. For the Fed, it shows the energy shock passing into prices, which is the risk officials named in their September minutes. Exposure, not direction: airlines (UAL, AAL, LUV), refiners (Delta owns one), travel.
What’s next The 10:00 call: how much of the $4.25 fuel assumption is hedged, and whether demand is holding into the holidays. Oil eased overnight; if it holds near $103 rather than $105, December-quarter guidance becomes easier to meet.

SpaceX buys nationwide low-band spectrum; AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile fall 7–8% after hours

The 800 MHz licenses give Starlink’s phone service the kind of frequencies carriers use to reach rural areas and the inside of buildings. The FCC still has to approve the deal.

After Thursday’s close, SpaceX said it will buy Grain Management’s nationwide 800 MHz spectrum portfolio, up to 14 MHz of paired low-band frequencies, for its Starlink Mobile service. Elon Musk called it the “last critical piece.” SpaceX did not give a price. The Wall Street Journal puts it at about $8 billion in cash. Price from one outlet

The carriers fell hard after hours: AT&T about 8%, Verizon about 7%, T-Mobile about 7% and Comcast about 2% (CNBC). In the regular session they had risen 1–2%. SpaceX (SPCX) was up about 3–4% after hours, recovering part of Thursday’s 4.2% fall to $160.57. European telecom stocks are lagging this morning (Reuters). Morgan Stanley expects the threat to carriers to build slowly, starting in rural markets.

The deal comes a day after the FCC’s Space Bureau authorized SpaceX’s plan for about 15,000 direct-to-cell satellites; a bureau-level order can still be reviewed by the full commission. AST SpaceMobile, the rival direct-to-phone company, fell 6.1% Thursday to $56.93. SpaceX’s first lock-up also expires today. A Motley Fool report from September puts the first tranche at about 328 million shares, with another on October 24. Single outlet We found no same-day confirmation of the schedule.

Why it matters Satellite service has been a complement to wireless networks. Low-band spectrum makes it a possible competitor for some customers. That is a direct threat to the most stable cash flows in US telecom, which are owned by income funds and are large weights in XLC and dividend ETFs.

Isaias strengthens to a strong Category 2, with landfall tonight and most Gulf oil output shut in

The season’s first hurricane could briefly reach Category 3 before landfall between Dauphin Island, Alabama, and Destin, Florida. About 2.7 million barrels a day of refining capacity is near its path.

The National Hurricane Center had Isaias at 100 mph at its Thursday evening update, and NBC reported 110 mph early Friday. The forecast is for a brief run at Category 3 today before slight weakening. AccuWeather expects landfall between 8 p.m. Friday and 1 a.m. Saturday, between Dauphin Island and Destin. A hurricane warning runs from Ocean Springs, Mississippi, to the Bay–Gulf county line in Florida. Forecasters warn of up to 7 feet of storm surge and up to 15 inches of rain.

Alabama, Florida and Georgia have declared emergencies. Gulf Shores, Orange Beach, Dauphin Island and parts of the Florida Panhandle are under mandatory evacuation. Pensacola’s airport closes to commercial flights this morning. We found no federal emergency declaration yet.

Offshore, the federal regulator reported Thursday that 62.9% of Gulf of Mexico oil output, about 1.28 million barrels a day, and 57.4% of gas output were shut in, with 121 platforms evacuated. Analysts estimate about 9 million barrels of lost output. CNN reports that about 2.7 million barrels a day of refining capacity, roughly 14% of the US total, is near the path. The track is east of the Louisiana and Texas refining centers.

What’s next Landfall overnight. Platforms usually restart within days of a storm passing unless they are damaged, so the oil impact should be short. Gasoline and diesel depend on whether the Mobile- and Pascagoula-area refineries lose power.

Oil eases and futures rise as Trump’s no-strike pledge sinks in

Brent is down 1.3% to about $102.95, but still up for the week. The blockade stays, Iran is still reviewing Washington’s reply, and tankers are still being attacked.

Brent crude was near $102.95 at 6:30, down from Thursday’s $104.28 settlement; US crude was near $90.65, down 0.9% (Yahoo, delayed). On Thursday the president posted that “we will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections,” citing “productive” talks. That came a day after reports that his team had discussed strike options, and after his Wednesday rally remark that a deal “isn’t really something that I want to do.” The US naval blockade remains.

Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said Thursday that Tehran is still reviewing the US response to its seven-day plan for reopening Hormuz, received through Qatari mediators, and may reply “within days.” The plan asks the US to lift the blockade, waive oil sanctions and release about $12 billion of frozen assets. In return Iran would reopen the strait on day six, with talks starting on day seven. The contents of the US reply have not been disclosed.

The shipping risk has not gone away. UKMTO has verified 14 tanker attacks since September 30. The ship tracker Kpler counted seven transits of the strait on Tuesday, a two-month low, and ten on Wednesday. A Houthi missile hit Riyadh’s airport on Thursday and killed three people (see The World).

Stock futures rose with the oil retreat: Nasdaq 100 +0.7%, S&P 500 +0.4% at 6:30. Gold is up 1.2% to about $4,208. The dollar index is flat at 102.14.

Two Fed officials say more hikes are coming as the bond market eases from its highs

Waller and Musalem used almost the same words. Markets see an October hike as unlikely and December as likely, and Wednesday’s CPI is the next test.

Governor Christopher Waller said in Istanbul on Thursday: “If economic data continue to meet expectations, I expect further rate hikes will be needed… We do not have to hike at consecutive meetings.” St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem said in New York that “more monetary policy firming will be required.” He pointed to the next six to nine months but said he has not prejudged the October 27–28 meeting. Secondary reports put futures-implied odds near 17% for an October hike and above 80% for December. Unconfirmed figures The target range is 3.75–4.00%.

Treasuries rallied anyway. The 10-year par yield closed Thursday at 5.22%, down 6 basis points, and the 30-year at 5.60%, down 7, after the $22 billion 30-year reopening sold at 5.618% with almost no tail (Treasury). The 2-year was 4.75%, so the curve flattened to 47 basis points from 51. The 10-year breakeven was 2.35% Thursday and has barely moved for two weeks. The bond market’s inflation expectations are not rising with oil.

Medicare star ratings: Humana up about 13% after hours, Alignment down about 20%

CMS released 2027 Medicare Advantage star ratings Thursday. Quality ratings decide bonus payments worth billions to insurers.

Humana said in an 8-K this morning that it beat its goal of “Top Quartile” results. It defines that as star-related revenue per member at least 10% above the median of a peer group that includes UnitedHealth, CVS, Elevance and Centene. Reports say its largest contract, about 2.4 million members, was upgraded to 4 stars from 3.5. The stock rose about 13% after hours, after closing Thursday down 2.4% at $387.12.

Alignment Healthcare fell about 20–23% after hours. Its main contract, about three-quarters of its members, reportedly dropped to 3.5 stars. The company filed an 8-K Thursday evening and plans to appeal. CVS, Clover and UnitedHealth were also active after hours, and Clover filed an 8-K too.

Why it matters Star ratings set 2028 bonus payments. For Humana, an upgrade on its largest contract changes its earnings path. For the sector, it shows how much insurer margins depend on a government formula rather than on medical-cost trends.

After Thursday’s AI sell-off, OpenAI has still said nothing on the record about its revenue

The gap between the $50 billion and $70 billion figures seems to come mostly from how cloud-partner sales are counted. Bloomberg reports that OpenAI expects to pass $70 billion by year-end.

Thursday’s fall started with a Financial Times report, later confirmed by CNBC and Reuters through their own sources. It said OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue was about $50 billion at the end of September, well below the roughly $68–70 billion that had been reported. People familiar with the figures say the higher number counted gross revenue from cloud partners, as Anthropic does in its own run-rate. OpenAI’s figure does not. Bloomberg reported hours later that OpenAI expects annualized revenue of $70 billion or more by the end of 2026. The company has made no on-record statement.

The market damage was large: Micron fell 4.8%, Broadcom 4.3%, Oracle 5.5%, CoreWeave 7.8% and AMD 3.9%. The semiconductor ETF (SMH) fell 2.8% and the Nasdaq 1.25%, its worst day since mid-August. Even so, more stocks rose than fell across the market as a whole: 2,993 against 2,537 on our count, and equal-weight S&P rose 0.6%. Money moved out of AI rather than out of stocks. Nasdaq futures are up 0.7% this morning.

The Economy

Today’s data. The University of Michigan’s preliminary October consumer sentiment is at 10:00. Consensus is about 47.6, against September’s final 48.1. That is already near record lows, and the year-ahead inflation expectation was 4.6% in September. With gasoline tracking crude above $100, the inflation-expectations line matters more than the headline. The Fed has said it watches expectations for signs that the energy shock is spreading into prices generally.

Where the labor market stands. Payrolls grew 29,000 in September and 133,000 in August, and unemployment rose to 4.2% from 4.1% (BLS via FRED; preliminary, subject to revision). Initial jobless claims were 197,000 last week, and the four-week average is the lowest since October 2022. Few layoffs, little hiring: a cooling but not breaking labor market. The Fed reads that as room to keep tightening against inflation.

Inflation. The August CPI index rose 0.39% from July, and core rose 0.29% (BLS via FRED; month-on-month, seasonally adjusted). September CPI comes Wednesday at 8:30, with the Beige Book that afternoon. It is the last CPI before the October 27–28 meeting, and the first full month of Brent above $100.

The Fed path. Waller and Musalem were both explicit Thursday that more hikes are coming. Waller’s point that they need not be at consecutive meetings is the important one: it allows the Fed to skip October and hike in December, which is roughly what futures price. Kansas City Fed President Jeffrey Schmid is listed for 9:30 this morning on one calendar; we could not confirm it on the Fed’s schedule.

Treasury. This week’s $119 billion of coupon supply is done. The 3-year stopped through, the 10-year stopped through strongly and the 30-year tailed by only 0.1 basis point. Foreign and domestic buyers met the supply at these yields, which helped long bonds rally Thursday. The bond market is closed Monday for Columbus Day, and stocks are open. Treasury says automatic enrollment of under-18s in the new “Trump Accounts” is complete. AP and the Treasury Secretary give different counts, about 70 million and “60 million plus.”

Abroad. No major central-bank decision overnight. The Bank of Japan, which hiked September 18, said Thursday that small and mid-size firms are only about halfway through passing on higher costs. That points to more inflation pressure in Japan with the yen at 158 per dollar. China’s third-quarter GDP and UK monthly GDP come next week.

Risks & Scenarios

Consumer sentiment. Michigan’s preliminary October reading is more likely than not to come in below September’s 48.1. We put that at 55–65%: consensus is 47.6 and fuel prices have risen through the survey window, but this series often surprises in both directions. The inflation-expectations line is the bigger market risk. A year-ahead figure above 4.6% would add to the case for a December hike.

Oil. Brent is more likely than not to settle today below Thursday’s $104.28. We put that at 65–80%: it is already $1.33 below, and the no-strike pledge removes a dated risk. Against: the storm’s refinery path and the continuing tanker attacks.

Isaias. We put landfall as a hurricane of at least Category 2, between Dauphin Island and Destin, by Saturday noon at 70–80%. That is the NHC’s and AccuWeather’s forecast, but intensity forecasts near landfall carry real error. The market risk is refinery power loss, not the offshore shut-ins, which are usually brief.

The AI trade. The worst case is that the revenue question spreads to the lenders: wider spreads on AI-linked private credit, or a delayed financing. We see no sign of that yet. High-yield spreads near 3.1% and a VIX of 15 say the market is not pricing it.

The World

Navi Pillay wins the Nobel Peace Prize

The 85-year-old South African, a former anti-apartheid lawyer, war-crimes judge and UN human rights chief, was honored for her work to make sure genocide and war crimes are prosecuted.

The Norwegian Nobel Committee said this morning that her cases showed legal accountability can help prevent war and violence. Committee chair Jørgen Watne Frydnes called the award “definitely a message to those who are undermining the international system and institutions.” Pillay defended anti-apartheid activists, sat as a judge on the Rwanda tribunal and the International Criminal Court, and was UN High Commissioner for Human Rights. More recently she chaired the UN commission of inquiry that accused Israel of genocide in Gaza, which Israel rejects. The committee could not reach her before the announcement, and she told reporters she “thought it must be a joke.” The prize is presented December 10.

Science

The most distant fast radio burst yet. A team led from the University of Sydney reports in Science that the burst FRB 20240304B came from redshift 2.148, when the universe was about 3 billion years old. That roughly doubles the previous record of about 1.0. JWST imaging traced it to a small, metal-poor, star-forming galaxy. The team says that fits a young magnetar as the source but does not prove it. Bursts this distant make it possible to measure the gas between galaxies, the so-called missing baryons, at much earlier times. (Science First Release, doi:10.1126/science.adz2675.)

Human brain tissue in mice. The cover paper of this week’s Nature, from Sergiu Pașca’s lab at Stanford, describes mice engineered to lack most of their own cortex and hippocampus. Grafted human cortical organoids then integrated into the animals’ brain circuits. It is a platform for studying human brain development and disease in a living system, and it will reopen the ethics debate about human-animal neural chimeras.

Why big dogs age faster. A study of about 900 dogs from roughly 120 breeds links faster aging in large breeds to changes in the epigenome and to activity of “jumping genes,” according to Nature’s news coverage.

SpaceX’s phone ambitions. In two days SpaceX got Space Bureau approval for about 15,000 direct-to-cell satellites and agreed to buy nationwide low-band spectrum. Satellite service is becoming a possible substitute for some wireless customers (see Top Stories). Artemis II science. NASA released more than 800 gigabytes of Artemis II lunar observations, including images, audio and crew notes, with a preliminary lunar science report.
