Top Stories

Oil settles at a one-month high as the Gulf outweighs Trump’s energy truce

Brent shook off an early 1.5% drop and closed up 1.8% at $106.65. Russia would not confirm the truce, Ukraine said Russia had already broken it, and Riyadh’s airport stayed shut under fire.

Crude opened the week on the defensive. The president’s Sunday post saying Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an immediate “energy ceasefire” pulled Brent down to about $103 in Asian trade. ICE gasoil, the diesel benchmark most exposed to Ukraine’s refinery strikes, fell about 3% (Investing.com). By the European afternoon both moves had reversed. Brent settled at $106.65, up $1.93 or 1.8%, its highest close since September 15, and WTI settled at $93.74, up $1.89 (Reuters, via Investing.com and CNBC). Reuters reported that the attacks on Saudi Arabia outweighed the prospect of more supply.

The truce did not firm up during the day:

  • The Kremlin. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov said only that “contacts are ongoing,” and declined to say whether Russia had agreed or what the truce covers (Moscow Times, AP via PBS). He rejected Zelensky’s offer of a 60-day general ceasefire, saying Moscow wants an overall peace deal.
  • Kyiv. President Zelensky said Ukraine was “horribly surprised” that Russia hit energy targets after the president’s post: the Kharkiv CHP-5 power plant, a gas production site and a gas station (AP, NPR). “If Russia does not agree,” he said, “we will respond.”
  • Washington. A White House official told Axios the president was likely describing a possible deal covering oil refineries. The White House has published no terms.

The Gulf moved the other way:

  • Riyadh. King Khalid International Airport remained closed, with a notice to airmen keeping it shut until at least October 17 (CNN). Saturday’s strike killed 12 by the Saudi aviation authority’s count and 13 by the coalition’s, with more than 300 wounded. Sunday’s strike on Terminal 4 killed one person and wounded 11, according to the authority (CNN, Gulf News). Saudi forces said they intercepted another ballistic missile aimed at Riyadh on Monday (CNN). CBS counts at least 20 people killed in Houthi attacks on Saudi airports over the past week.
  • Yemen. The Houthis said airstrikes hit two districts of Sanaa around midnight, killing one person and wounding 12, and blamed Saudi Arabia. Single source That account comes from the Houthi-run health ministry, reported by CBS. The coalition has not confirmed any strikes.
  • Shipping. A drone hit the tanker Petro Dolphin at Oman’s Sohar anchorage on Sunday; two Indian crew are missing (India’s shipping minister, via Outlook India and Seatrade). A projectile set the Valthera alight in the Strait of Hormuz; all 11 Indian crew are safe, according to India’s foreign ministry.

Goldman Sachs raised its December Brent forecast to $90 from $85. It estimates the war premium in today’s price at just over $20 a barrel (via CNBC). Hormuz crude flows were about 8.5 million barrels a day in the week to October 7, roughly 40% below normal (Kpler, via CNBC).

Why it matters

Today showed which force is stronger. A possible truce that would ease the diesel squeeze moved oil for a few hours; the Gulf moved it for the rest of the day. Brent is now about $17 above the $89 that the IMF’s July forecasts assumed, two days before a CPI report that consensus already expects to be lifted by gasoline. Exposure, not direction: refiners, airlines (Delta −2.5% today), chemicals and the inflation-linked end of the bond market.

What’s next

Whether the Kremlin confirms anything, or Ukraine resumes refinery strikes. Any US decision on striking the Houthis: the president said Saturday the US “may” join, and we found no sign of action today. Riyadh’s airport notice runs to Saturday. A strike on Gulf oil or gas facilities would move prices by dollars, not cents.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com and CNBC (settlements, Goldman, Kpler); Moscow Times; AP via PBS; NPR; Axios; CNN; Gulf News; CBS News; Outlook India; Seatrade Maritime; Yahoo Finance (delayed quotes).

Seoul’s chip rout spreads to US memory and optical stocks

The Kospi fell 3.65% on a record Samsung quarter; in New York, Sandisk, Micron and the optical names led the semiconductor index lower. Several explanations are in circulation, and none is confirmed.

South Korea’s benchmark fell 241.69 points to 6,384.24, its steepest drop since September 2, and is now down about 8.9% since October 6 (Korea Times, Yonhap). Samsung Electronics fell 4.4% and SK hynix 4.5%. Foreign investors sold a net 2.02 trillion won. This came days after Samsung’s preliminary third-quarter operating profit of about 107.4 trillion won, the first time a Korean company has made more than 100 trillion won in a quarter.

The selling crossed the Pacific (closing prices, Finnhub):

Market narrative Several explanations are circulating, and we cannot confirm any of them as the cause:

The AI trade did not fall as one block. Meta rose 3.4%, Microsoft 1.1% and Apple 1.3%: the companies that buy chips held up while the companies that make them fell.

Hammack backs “smaller, frequent” moves as the dollar climbs to a one-year high

The Cleveland Fed president said restrictive policy is appropriate and that an entrenched inflation mindset is the biggest risk. She did not commit to an October hike, and futures barely moved.

In prepared remarks to the National Association for Business Economics in Cleveland, Beth Hammack said a restrictive stance is appropriate to bring inflation back to 2% in a timely way. She also said that she would rather make “smaller, frequent” rate adjustments fairly quickly than a larger correction later (Bloomberg, CNBC). She described price pressure as broad, coming from energy, tariffs and AI-driven demand, not only oil, and called an entrenched “inflationary mindset” the biggest risk. She did not explicitly back a hike at the October 27–28 meeting.

Futures put the chance of an October hike at about 18–19% after her remarks, little changed from the morning, and price a December hike as likely (reports range from 83% to 88%). The policy range is 3.75–4.00% after September’s increase. Governor Waller said last week he expects “additional hikes” if the data come in as expected.

Likely contributor The dollar index rose 0.37% to about 102.59, its highest close in at least a year, and the euro fell to about $1.116. FXStreet tied the euro’s weakness partly to French fiscal and political worries and the dollar’s strength to haven demand and Hammack. Dollar-yen reached 158.6, close to the levels that have drawn talk of Japanese intervention.

Why it matters

“Smaller, frequent” describes a Fed that keeps hiking in quarter-point steps while energy feeds into other prices. That is roughly what futures price for December. Wednesday’s CPI decides whether October comes into play. A strong dollar is tightening financial conditions too: it hurts US exporters and emerging-market borrowers, and it weighed on bitcoin today.

Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland remarks via Bloomberg and CNBC; FXStreet; fed funds futures via CME FedWatch reports; Yahoo Finance (DXY, FX).

Federal agents search Rep. Ilhan Omar’s home in a Justice Department investigation

Homeland Security Investigations carried out a judge-signed warrant at the Minnesota Democrat’s Washington-area residence three weeks before the midterms. She has not been charged.

The Justice Department said Homeland Security Investigations executed a “judicially authorized search warrant secured by the Department of Justice” at Omar’s residence Monday morning (ABC, NPR, CNN, Star Tribune). Border czar Tom Homan confirmed that “a search warrant was executed this morning, signed by a judge,” and said DOJ and DHS are leading the investigation. Neither agency described what it is about:

  • ABC’s sources say the investigation concerns Omar’s immigration paperwork.
  • NPR reports that a former official described it as an immigration-fraud inquiry, and a current official as a multi-year investigation that includes whistleblower complaints.
  • NBC’s sources say it goes beyond immigration.

The investigation is at the search stage. No charges have been filed, and it is unclear whether Omar was at home. She called the search “an attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement” and said, “I will not be silenced.” She has long denied the immigration allegations, which have never been proven in court. Administration officials, including Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, had said publicly that she was under investigation. Separately, House Oversight Chairman James Comer has opened an inquiry into her husband’s disclosed investments (Fox News).

What’s next

Whether the Justice Department describes the case or a court unseals the warrant. Expect reaction from House leaders of both parties, who are out of session until after the November 3 election.

Sources: Justice Department statement via ABC News, NPR and CNN; Star Tribune; NBC News; Newsweek; Fox News.

Microsoft unveils its own inference chip, and Meta answers OpenAI with “Muse Wonder”

Two of Nvidia’s biggest customers made AI news on a day the chip makers fell: Microsoft with in-house silicon for 2027, Meta with a new frontier model family.

At the Open Compute Project summit, Microsoft announced Maia 300, its third-generation inference accelerator. It is built on TSMC’s N2P process and will be used only for Microsoft’s own services, such as Copilot, starting in 2027 (UPI, TechRadar, Yahoo Finance). Microsoft claims about 40% more tokens per megawatt than “leading GPU systems,” without naming the comparison. Reuters reports it may deploy more than a million of the chips in 2027. Nvidia remains its partner for training.

Meta released “Muse Wonder,” frontier models it had developed under the codename Watermelon, through its Model API and Muse Code (Axios). Meta claims they match or beat OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s models; no independent tests have been published. Likely contributor CNBC tied Meta’s 3.4% gain, to $742.96, to the release.

Why it matters

The largest buyers of AI chips are building more of their own. Inference, running models rather than training them, is where custom silicon competes most directly with Nvidia. A single announcement does not change 2026 orders, but it feeds the question hanging over chip stocks this month: who captures the margin as AI spending scales.

Sources: Microsoft via UPI, TechRadar, Yahoo Finance; Reuters; Axios; CNBC; Finnhub (prices).

The economics Nobel goes to Robert Hall and Michael Woodford

Two American economists won for the theory behind modern central banking, including the forward guidance the Fed under Warsh has said it wants to rely on less.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the prize to Robert E. Hall of Stanford and Michael Woodford of Columbia, “for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation,” as the citation was relayed by CNBC, Reuters and CNN. Their models of forward-looking households and firms underpin how central banks set rates and communicate. Woodford’s work in particular is the intellectual basis of forward guidance: the idea that what a central bank promises about future rates matters as much as today’s rate. The committee cited the work’s usefulness during the pandemic and the energy crises.

Fortune notes the timing: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has argued that forward guidance has gone too far, and he speaks with the IMF’s Kristalina Georgieva late Thursday.

Sources: Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announcement via CNBC, Reuters, CNN and CBC; Fortune.

North Korea fires a salvo of ballistic missiles; one flew more than 900 kilometres

The first launch in nine days followed Pyongyang’s hypersonic tests this month. Seoul held an emergency security meeting.

South Korea’s Joint Chiefs of Staff detected several ballistic missiles of “various types” fired from the Pyongyang area toward the Sea of Japan at about 9:20 AM Monday, Korean time, which was Sunday evening in New York (Al Jazeera, Euronews, ABC News). Japan said at least two were launched 17 minutes apart and fell outside its exclusive economic zone. Seoul’s military reported one flew more than 900 km and leans toward a short-range type rather than a hypersonic one (SBS). Al Jazeera counts this as North Korea’s 16th launch event of 2026, after a hypersonic intermediate-range test on October 3. Seoul’s presidential office called for an immediate halt.

Sources: South Korean Joint Chiefs of Staff and Japanese defence ministry via Al Jazeera, Euronews, ABC News and SBS.

Science

  • A second-generation planet? Archival Hubble data suggest the white dwarf HS 0209+0832 may host a planet that formed from debris after its star died. The paper is in Nature Astronomy, reported by Universe Today. If confirmed, the planet would have formed after its star died, not survived it.
  • Bethe strings, seen at last. An ultracold-gas experiment has created and directly observed “Bethe strings,” bound states of interacting spins that Hans Bethe’s 1931 solution predicted (ScienceDaily). It confirms one of the foundational exact results in quantum many-body physics.
  • A self-stabilising nuclear clock. A Vienna group reports the first nuclear clock that keeps itself locked to the thorium-229 transition (ScienceDaily). It is a step toward timekeeping beyond today’s best atomic clocks.
  • Rain that outlasted El Niño. A UCLA study in Science Advances finds that 2024’s globally widespread extreme rainfall persisted after El Niño faded, which points to warming rather than the cycle alone (Phys.org).

Red-light cameras start ticketing, including on Saw Mill Run Boulevard

After a 60-day warning period, Pittsburgh began mailing $100 citations Sunday at three intersections: Saw Mill Run Boulevard and Woodruff Street on Route 51, Penn Avenue and North Dallas Avenue, and Anderson Street and East General Robinson Street (WPXI, CBS Pittsburgh). WPXI; CBS Pittsburgh.

Pennsylvania

Skill games must be gone by midnight Tuesday; enforcement starts Wednesday

The 120-day stay of the state Supreme Court’s June 15 ruling, which held that cash-paying skill-game terminals are illegal slot machines, expires at midnight Tuesday. The court refused on Friday to extend it. From Wednesday, State Police can seize the machines and the cash in them, and unplugging a machine does not count as removing it (Post-Gazette, Capital-Star, abc27). A request from the Skill Games Business Consortium to intervene and seek another 120 days is still pending. The legislature passed no regulation in the four months and does not return until October 19. Bars, clubs, VFW posts and convenience stores lose the income.

Pittsburgh Post-Gazette; Pennsylvania Capital-Star; abc27; Pennsylvania State Police.
