Top Stories

The September jobs report lands into a bond market that just backed off a 24-year high

The consensus is a soft one, under 100,000 jobs. The bond market’s problem this week has been the long end, not the Fed, so even a soft number may not settle it.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the September Employment Situation at 8:30 this morning. The federal government is funded through December 11 under the continuing resolution signed in early September, so there is no shutdown risk to the release. Forecasts differ more than usual. Dow Jones’s survey (cited by CNBC and Schwab) is +84,000. FactSet and Morningstar are +95,000, and Newsquawk’s is +98,000, with private payrolls at +85,000. All of them expect unemployment at 4.1% and average hourly earnings up 0.3% on the month, or about 3.2% on the year. Payrolls rose 162,000 in August by the BLS count (FRED), with unemployment at 4.1% and hourly pay up 3.1% from a year earlier.

The rest of this week’s data argued for strength. Jobless claims were 197,000 in the latest week, near the lowest of the year. ADP reported +90,000 private jobs against roughly 70,000 expected. ISM manufacturing rose to 54.5 on Thursday, and its prices-paid index jumped to 77.9. Newsquawk flags a risk that August is revised down even if September beats.

The bond market reached this report with a two-day round trip. On Thursday the 10-year traded as high as about 5.34% overnight, its highest since 2002, then reversed. It closed at 5.24% on Treasury’s official curve, while the 2-year fell 10 basis points to 4.78%. No single catalyst for the reversal has been confirmed. This morning the 10-year is near 5.243% and the 30-year near 5.618%, both little changed (CNBC). The Fed raised rates a quarter-point to 3.75–4.00% on September 16, and futures now price about a 72% chance it holds on October 28. Vice Chair Jefferson on Thursday spoke of a “cascade of shocks” to inflation but said a further move “may take more time.”

Why it matters The two ends of the curve are reacting to different things. The front end trades the Fed: a soft print pushes October hike odds lower still, and the 2-year has already rallied hard. The long end has been trading supply, oil and global term premium. A weak number that does little for the 30-year would tell you that the long end is no longer about US growth.
What’s next Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan speaks today. Next week brings ISM services Monday, the 3-, 10- and 30-year auctions Tuesday through Thursday, and the minutes of the September hiking meeting on Wednesday at 2:00 PM.

Reading the curve. Thursday was a bull steepener: 2-year −10 bp, 5-year −8, 10-year −5, 30-year −3. The 2s10s spread widened to 46 basis points, the widest in this run, and 5s30s is 60. The 3-month bill fell to 4.17%, so the gap from bills to the 10-year is 107 basis points. FRED’s 10-year breakeven inflation rate held at 2.36%, and the 10-year real yield was 2.93% on Wednesday. Real yields, not inflation expectations, carried this rise. That is a term-premium story, and it is why a soft jobs number may not do much for the 30-year.

What would change the picture. Three things together: unemployment at 4.2% or higher, payrolls under 75,000, and hourly pay at +0.2% or less. That combination would make October hike talk hard to sustain and would test whether the long end can follow the front end down. The opposite (payrolls above 150,000 and pay +0.4%) would put October back in play.

Euro-area inflation jumps to 3.8%, the highest in three years, on an 18.8% energy surge

The headline beat the 3.6% forecast, but core held at 2.5%. The ECB’s problem is energy passing through, not runaway underlying inflation.

Eurostat’s September flash estimate put annual inflation in the euro area at 3.8%, up from 3.2% in August and above the 3.6% consensus. It is the highest reading since September 2023. Energy prices were up 18.8% on the year. Core inflation, which excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, was 2.5%, in line with forecasts (CNBC, Euronews). The national prints earlier in the week had pointed this way: France 3.4% and Italy 4.1% on the harmonised measure, and Germany 3.3% on its national CPI.

The ECB raised its deposit rate a quarter-point to 2.50% on September 10, and President Lagarde said on September 28 that “measured” further hikes remain appropriate. European stocks are higher this morning regardless, led by technology, and the euro is barely changed at about $1.125 (AP).

Why it matters A hotter Europe adds to the global long-end selling. Gilts and Bunds have been moving with Treasuries all week, and Britain’s 30-year traded as high as about 6.03% this week, its highest since 1998. Our forecast that the flash would come in at 3.7% or higher was right; it will be graded formally on Saturday.

Nike falls about 9% after hours as its turnaround slips a year

The quarter beat, but the full-year earnings guide is roughly a third below what Wall Street expected, and China sales fell more than 20%.

Nike reported fiscal first-quarter earnings of $0.48 a share on Thursday after the close, ahead of the $0.44 forecast. Revenue was $11.21 billion, below the roughly $11.35 billion consensus and down about 4%. The guidance did the damage. Nike now expects fiscal 2027 revenue to fall by a high-single-digit percentage and adjusted earnings of $1.15 to $1.35 a share, against a consensus near $1.67. Its 8-K also discloses a new cost program, “Pace,” which targets $2.5 billion of savings and carries about $300 million of restructuring charges this year. Greater China revenue fell about 22% by the company’s figures. Some reports cite 26% on a different basis.

The shares closed Thursday at $35.15, down 0.7%. They traded as low as about $32.09 after hours, down 8.6% to 8.7% (Newsquawk, StockTitan).

Why it matters Nike is a read on the global consumer and on China. A double-digit cut to an earnings path that was already reset lower says discretionary demand outside the US is weak. Expect pressure on other athletic and apparel names at the open.

Oil gives back Thursday’s jump as Europe weighs releasing diesel and crude stockpiles

Washington has been pressing France and Germany to tap their reserves, and the threat of a US diesel-export ban appears to have moved them.

Brent crude fell about 3% this morning, to about $99.50 a barrel, after reports that European governments are considering releasing emergency diesel and crude stocks. Newsquawk had Brent down 3.2% and European gasoil futures down 5.5%. Reuters reported on Thursday that the US told France and Germany to release diesel stocks or face a possible US restriction on diesel exports. The White House and Energy Secretary Chris Wright had earlier ruled out a flat ban. Germany and France hold about 35% of the EU’s strategic diesel reserves, and the bloc holds about 39 million tons in total, more than two months of consumption.

On Thursday Brent had jumped 4.4% to $102.31 and WTI 2.7% to $92.87, after reports of a third US carrier group heading to the Middle East. Quotes this morning differ: Trading Economics shows WTI near $92.6, little changed, while other reports show a larger fall. Treat the size of this morning’s move as preliminary.

Why it matters Diesel is the fuel behind Europe’s 18.8% energy inflation and much of freight costs everywhere. A coordinated stock release would be the first policy response aimed at the price rather than the supply route, and it would ease one of the forces pushing long-term yields up.

Paramount’s $52 billion financing is done; the Warner Bros. Discovery deal is set to close Tuesday

The combined company starts life with about $80 billion of debt and a junk rating from S&P.

Banks finished syndicating about $52 billion of debt for Paramount Skydance’s acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery by Wednesday, Bloomberg reported. That includes $30 billion of investment-grade first-lien notes in eight maturities from 2028 to 2066, about $11.4 billion of second-lien notes plus a €885 million tranche, and about $9.5 billion of loans. Paramount had announced the offering on September 28 at about $44.4 billion in its 8-K. Bloomberg reported that pricing on the high-grade portion was cut as demand built. The court-entered consent decree with 12 states removed the last legal barrier, and the deal is expected to close October 6. Reports put combined debt near $80 billion, about 7.6 times earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization.

Paramount shares fell 9.6% to $9.34 on Thursday. S&P had already cut the company to BB from BB+.

Trump says heavier strikes on Iran after the midterms are “possible” as Hormuz talks stall

Tehran received Washington’s reply on a seven-day reopening plan on Tuesday. The President called Iran’s position “not good enough.”

In an interview with TIME published Thursday, President Trump was asked whether the US might bomb Iran more heavily after the November 3 election. He said it was “possible.” He said the US is “stocking up at levels we’ve never done before” and that he does not think “you could ever have peace” with Iran. On Thursday night he said Iran was “ready to fold up” (CBS, ABC/CNN, Iran International). Foreign Minister Araghchi received the US response to the seven-day Hormuz plan through Qatari mediators in Doha on Tuesday evening. AP says its details are still unclear. Kurdistan24 reports that the gap is over the order of steps rather than their substance. Iran wants sanctions relief, freedom to export oil and access to frozen funds.

The strait remains effectively closed to commercial traffic. Britain’s UKMTO reported that “unknown projectiles” struck three tankers on September 29 and a fourth vessel separately. It did not attribute the attacks.

Why it matters Oil’s two-way swings this week (−3% Tuesday, +4% Thursday, −3% this morning) track these headlines more than any supply data. An explicit post-election timetable for escalation puts a date on the market’s main tail risk.

Supreme Court agrees to rule on denying bond hearings to immigrants arrested inside the US

The circuits have split, and the case could affect millions of people. The Court’s new term opens Monday with a climate-liability case.

On Thursday the Supreme Court agreed to review the administration’s mandatory-detention policy. The policy reads the 1996 immigration law (IIRIRA) to deny bond hearings to noncitizens arrested in the interior of the country, not only at the border. Most appeals courts that have considered it ruled against the government. The 5th and 8th Circuits upheld it. The ACLU, which represents detainees, says millions of people could be affected. The administration argues the statute requires detention (Washington Post, CNN, PBS/AP).

The term opens Monday, October 5. The first argument is Suncor Energy v. Boulder County Commissioners, on whether federal law blocks state-court suits seeking climate damages from oil companies (SCOTUSblog). Separately on Thursday, Judge Stanley Bastian ruled that the President’s firing of court-appointed Seattle US Attorney Roger Rogoff was unlawful and reinstated him. The Justice Department says it will appeal.

The Economy

Labor. Today’s report is the first look at September. The level in focus is payrolls: 159.08 million in August, up 162,000 on the month (FRED, seasonally adjusted). The rate of change in focus is wages, which grew 3.1% a year in August, only slightly above core PCE inflation of about 3.0% (August, after the annual revision). Real wage growth is thin. Unemployment has held at 4.1% for two months after 4.2% in June. Jobless claims (197,000, week of September 26) have been flat near 198,000 for three weeks, which does not suggest a cooling labor market.

The Fed. The funds rate is 3.75–4.00% after the September 16 hike, which passed 12–0. It was the first hike since July 2023. Vice Chair Jefferson on Thursday pointed to a “cascade of shocks” (energy, the AI buildout and trade policy) without endorsing an imminent move. New York Fed President Williams said on Monday there was “no need for urgency,” though “one further upward adjustment” might fit late in the year. Dallas’s Lorie Logan speaks today. FedWatch puts the chance of a hold on October 28 at about 72%. The next meeting after that is December 8–9. SOFR was 3.90% on September 30 (FRED).

Coupon supply next week totals $119 billion across three auctions: the $58 billion 3-year Tuesday, the $39 billion 10-year reopening Wednesday and the $22 billion 30-year reopening Thursday. AI & Technology

Chip smuggling. Federal prosecutors on Thursday arrested Greg Lui, owner of California-based Earthmade Computer, on charges of smuggling servers containing about $300 million of Nvidia AI chips to China in 2023 and 2024 (Bloomberg, SRN News). Bloomberg separately examined whether Nvidia missed warning signs. The company has not been accused of wrongdoing. The case keeps export-control enforcement in the headlines just as Nvidia’s China exposure is back in focus for investors.

Anthropic’s IPO. Reuters obtained Anthropic’s draft prospectus earlier this week. It devotes about 80 of 261 pages to risk factors, and those include a warning about AI’s “existential risk to humanity.” CNBC, citing Reuters, reported a 2025 net loss of about $42 billion and roughly $518 billion of compute and infrastructure commitments. Bloomberg reports a pre-IPO investor day. Secondary reports date it October 14 and put formal marketing in the week of November 9, with a Nasdaq listing after the midterms. No price, ticker or date has been confirmed, and valuation figures as high as $2 trillion are unconfirmed reports. Anthropic remains private.

OpenAI. GPT-6.1 “Sol,” released at Tuesday’s DevDay, is priced at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, one-fifth of the flagship. It has a context window of about 1.05 million tokens (The Next Web, Dataconomy). A reported $30 billion raise at about $1.4 trillion has not been confirmed by the company.

Cyber. The Pentagon has begun mailing notices for the Defense Manpower Data Center breach. It exposed Social Security numbers and other personal data for about 3.05 million people, about 2.8 million of them living. Access began in October 2025 through a flaw in a file-sharing system and was discovered July 16. The department says it has seen no sign of misuse and is offering 12 months of credit monitoring (TIME, ABC, Fedweek). No attacker has been publicly identified.

Science

The bat family tree, redrawn. The Bat1K consortium, led by Emma Teeling, published reference-quality genomes for 103 bat species covering all 21 families in this week’s Nature (October 1). The new tree places the origin of bats about 60 to 65 million years ago in Europe. Bats matter beyond taxonomy: their unusual immune tolerance to viruses and their long lifespans for their size are active areas of medical research, and a full genome set makes that work far easier.

Nobel week. Announcements begin Monday: medicine on October 5 (5:30 AM ET), physics October 6, chemistry October 7, literature October 8, peace October 9 and economics October 12 (Nobel Foundation).

Space

Crew-13 is aboard the station after a record-fast trip. Dragon Grace docked to the Harmony module’s forward port at 7:05 PM Thursday, 7 hours 55 minutes after launch from Kennedy. NASA says that is the fastest launch-to-docking by a US spacecraft in station history. Aboard are NASA’s Watkins and Delaney, Canada’s Kutryk and Russia’s Teteryatnikov, for about six months (NASA).

A SpaceX tripleheader. Falcon Heavy launched NROL-97 at 11:54 PM Thursday from pad 39A. It was the rocket’s first National Reconnaissance Office mission, sending a classified payload to a high-energy orbit. Both side boosters landed back at the Cape, and the new center core was expended. Earlier, the Transporter-18 rideshare carried about 130 satellites. Three launches in one day is a reminder of the cadence behind SpaceX’s valuation. SPCX nonetheless fell 1.8% to $148.07 on Thursday (Space.com, Spaceflight Now, Finnhub).

Ahead. A Falcon 9 carries part of the Space Development Agency’s Tranche 1 Transport Layer on Monday, and South Korea’s Nuri flies NeonSat satellites on October 7. Japan’s Martian Moons eXploration mission is set for October 19 on an H3 (Launch Library). Artemis III, the 2027 crewed test in Earth orbit with both Blue Origin’s and SpaceX’s landers, had its booster nose cones installed this week.

Measles. The CDC counts 3,659 confirmed US cases this year as of September 24, in 45 states and DC. That is the most in 35 years, against 2,289 in all of 2025, and 95% of cases were unvaccinated or of unknown status. Pennsylvania reported a fifth measles-related death this week (AP).

A quiet Atlantic. The Atlantic season reached October without a single hurricane, a first in the satellite era (Tampa Bay Times). The Hurricane Center expects no Atlantic activity for seven days. In the eastern Pacific it is tracking Hurricane Rachel off Baja California Sur.

Pittsburgh region

Duquesne Light strike enters day two with no talks scheduled, on a gusty, rainy day

About 1,000 members of IBEW Local 29 walked out at 12:01 AM Thursday after contract talks failed. They had authorized the strike 592–14. No deal was reached on the first day (WPXI). On Thursday the union challenged the company to return to the table. Both sides say they are willing to talk, but we found no session scheduled as of this morning. The union’s stated issues are safety, staffing, equipment and working conditions, including problems it says surfaced during the April 2025 windstorm that left some customers without power for about five days. Duquesne Light calls its offer “industry-leading” and says trained non-union employees and contractors are covering the work. There was tension at the gates when out-of-state crews arrived, and pickets are running around the clock at the Marshall-Shadeland facility (KDKA Radio, CBS Pittsburgh, Patch).

Why it matters The strike does not cut anyone’s power. The risk is slower repairs, and today brings showers and gusts to 25 mph. We found no statement yet from the governor, the mayor or the PUC.

Pittsburgh

O’Connor’s $728 million budget holds taxes and staffing but drains $26.5 million of reserves

Mayor Corey O’Connor sent City Council a $728 million preliminary 2027 operating budget and a five-year plan on Wednesday, with no tax increase and no layoffs. Balancing it takes $26.5 million from reserves. The mayor says the reserve runs out in about five years on current trends, blaming past mismanagement, reliance on federal pandemic money and federal cuts. The Stop the Violence Fund and the Housing Opportunity Fund are frozen, moving about $20 million to the general fund. Spending rises for paving, city vehicles, and landslide and stormwater work (WESA, WPXI, PublicSource). Council members are now weighing options, and the council’s schedule for votes is still to come.

UPMC enters Ohio, taking over the Trinity hospitals in Steubenville

CommonSpirit completed the transfer of Trinity Health System to UPMC effective October 1. Pennsylvania

Eleven days to the skill-games deadline, and no deal in sight

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruled in June that skill games are illegal, unregulated slot machines. State Police plan to start seizing unlicensed machines on October 14 unless lawmakers act by October 13. Attorney General Dave Sunday is urging businesses to remove them now. The Senate bill, SB 1079 from Sens. Gene Yaw (R) and Anthony Williams (D), would charge $500 a month per terminal under a 50,000-terminal cap, raising about $300 million a year. A House stopgap from Reps. Brad Roae and Manny Guzman would legalize the games temporarily until April 13, 2027. House members blame Senate GOP leaders, who are reported to have said they lack the votes. Only about 11 session days remain (Spotlight PA, City & State PA, abc27). Bars, VFW posts and gas stations around the South Hills that host the machines face losing that income in under two weeks.

Spotlight PA, City & State PA, abc27, NBC10, WITF (late September – October 1).

Pennsylvania

The state’s measles outbreak reaches about 943 cases and a fifth death

Pennsylvania’s outbreak, its worst in more than 35 years, has reached about 943 cases in 39 counties, and a fifth measles-related death was reported this week (CNN, AP). At least 176 people have been hospitalized. About 40% of cases are in Lancaster County, and fewer than 1% are in vaccinated people. Allegheny County reported no resident cases as of late September. Pittsburgh schools are planning MMR clinics, and more than 5,300 shots have been given at 173 pop-up clinics statewide.

CNN (September 30), AP, Pennsylvania Department of Health, WESA (September 28).
