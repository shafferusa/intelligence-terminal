An Equation That Solves for a Future, Not a Number

Tuesday, October 13, 2026 · 13 min read

Most equations you’ve ever solved hand you a number; today’s kind hands you a rule for how something changes instead — and a single extra fact about where it starts is all it takes to turn that rule into one exact, unfolding future.

Mathematics

Day 12 of 300 · Mathematics 12 of 20 · Differential equations: writing down how things change

The Big Idea

An equation like x + 5 = 12 asks a narrow question and has a narrow answer: find the one number that makes the statement true. Almost every equation from Day 2’s algebra onward works this way — solve for a number, or a short list of numbers, and you’re done. A differential equation asks a different kind of question entirely. Instead of relating a quantity to some other numbers, it relates a quantity to its own rate of change — how fast it is currently growing or shrinking, written as part of the very equation that describes it. Solve one of these, and the answer isn’t a number. It’s an entire function: a complete rule for how the quantity behaves at every moment in time.

Here is why that is a genuinely useful thing to be able to write down. Nobody can hand you a direct formula for how hot a cup of coffee will be in exactly six minutes, or how many bacteria will be in a petri dish by Thursday — those numbers depend on physics and biology too complicated to summarize in one line. But you often can say something simpler and just as powerful: how fast the coffee is losing heat right now depends only on how much hotter it currently is than the room around it; how fast the bacteria colony is gaining new cells right now depends only on how many cells are already there to divide. Neither of those statements mentions a specific time or a specific temperature or count at all — they just describe the rule the system is following. A differential equation is simply that rule, written in mathematical notation. Everything else — the actual temperature at 6 minutes, the actual bacteria count on Thursday — falls out of the rule once you know where things started.

This lesson is about learning to see that kind of equation for what it is, and about the two ideas — a whole family of possible futures, and the one extra fact that picks a single one out of it — that make it usable. The actual toolbox for finding a solution from scratch, for an arbitrary differential equation, is a skill of its own; today is about the shape of the question, not yet the full machinery for answering every version of it.

How It Works

Start with the notation. If y is some quantity changing over time t, its instantaneous rate of change — the same idea Day 10 built out of a shrinking time interval — is written dy/dt. A differential equation is any equation that includes a derivative like this one, where the thing being solved for is not a number but the function y itself. Building one is called a rate-of-change model: take a plain-English description of how a process behaves (“the colony grows faster the more bacteria are already in it”) and turn it directly into an equation relating the rate of change to the quantity itself, without ever having to say what the quantity’s value actually is at any particular moment.

The single most common rate-of-change model in basic science is almost insultingly short: dy/dt = ky, for some constant k. In words: the rate of change of y is directly proportional to y itself. When k is positive, this describes a quantity whose growth keeps accelerating simply because there is now more of it to grow — more bacteria means more cells currently able to split, so the colony adds new cells faster and faster as it gets bigger. When k is negative, it describes the mirror image: a quantity that shrinks, and shrinks more slowly as there is less of it left to lose. Nothing about the equation names a specific process — population growth, radioactive decay, and compounding of a quantity over time are all the identical rule, differing only in the sign and size of k.

Here is the first subtlety worth sitting with. The equation dy/dt = ky does not describe one curve. It describes every function that obeys that rule — and it turns out there are infinitely many of them, one for every possible starting value. A colony that begins with 10 bacteria and one that begins with 10 billion, growing under the exact same rule with the exact same k, trace out two wildly different curves in absolute numbers, yet both are equally valid solutions of the identical differential equation. That entire family of possible curves is called the general solution. What turns the general solution into one specific, actually-happening future is a single extra fact: the quantity’s value at some starting moment, almost always written as its value when t = 0. That one fact is called the initial condition, and supplying it selects exactly one curve out of the whole family — the particular solution that matches the real situation you actually care about.

The second idea worth building now is equilibrium, and it shows up most clearly in a close cousin of the growth equation: Newton’s law of cooling, first studied experimentally by Isaac Newton in a 1701 paper on how a heated block of iron loses heat, and given its familiar modern form somewhat later. Written as a rate-of-change model, it says dT/dt = −k(T − Troom): the rate at which an object’s temperature T changes is proportional to how far it currently is from the room’s temperature, and always points back toward it. A 180°F cup of coffee in a 70°F room cools quickly at first, because the gap is large; as it approaches 70°F the gap narrows and the cooling slows, exactly the way the population equation’s growth accelerates as the population grows, only running in reverse. Now ask: what happens if the coffee somehow starts out already at exactly 70°F, the room’s own temperature? The gap is zero, so the rate of change is zero — nothing happens, forever. That constant value, where the rate of change is permanently zero, is an equilibrium: a value a system stays at once it’s there, and which other starting values drift toward (or, in other models, away from) over time. The plain growth equation has an equilibrium too, at y = 0 — a population of zero stays zero forever, for the unglamorous reason that there is nothing left to reproduce — but it is the cooling equation where the idea actually earns its keep, since 70°F is a genuinely useful long-run prediction, not just a mathematical curiosity.

One more thing is worth being honest about before moving on: nothing above has shown you how to find the curve that satisfies a differential equation like dy/dt = ky from a blank page. That is a real skill, with its own techniques, and it is not today’s job. What you can already do, and what the Equation / Model section below walks through, is check whether a candidate function actually satisfies a given rule once someone hands you a guess — which turns out to be most of what you need to understand what a “solution” to a differential equation even means.

Key Concepts

Differential equation
An equation that relates a quantity to its own rate of change (one or more derivatives). Its unknown is an entire function, not a number.
Rate-of-change model
The act of turning a plain description of a changing process into a differential equation, by writing the quantity’s rate of change directly in terms of the quantity itself (and sometimes time), without needing its value at any one moment.
General solution
The entire family of functions that satisfy a given differential equation — every member obeys the identical rule for change, differing only in where each one happens to start.
Initial condition
One extra fact — the quantity’s value at a starting moment — that selects a single curve, the particular solution, out of the general solution’s whole family.
Equilibrium
A constant value at which a system’s rate of change is permanently zero. A system already there stays there forever; starting elsewhere, it typically drifts toward or away from it over time.

Example / Application

Many real populations really do grow, at least before resources run short, in almost exactly the shape of dy/dt = ky. Escherichia coli bacteria reproduce by splitting cleanly in two, and in a nutrient-rich lab culture held at an ideal temperature, a culture can double in number in about 20 minutes — a figure widely cited in microbiology (Gibson et al., 2018). That is the rate-of-change model in its rawest form: the number of new cells appearing in any instant is proportional to how many cells already exist to split, which is exactly what makes the growth rate proportional to the current population.

Start a culture with 1,000 cells, growing under dy/dt = ky with a 20-minute doubling time. How many cells are there after one hour?

Find k from the doubling time. A doubling time of 20 minutes means k = ln(2) / 20 ≈ 0.0347 per minute (the reasoning: plugging a doubling, y → 2y, into the solution form below and solving for k is exactly this calculation run in reverse).

Apply the solution. Using y(t) = y0ekt (derived in Equation / Model below) with y0 = 1,000 and t = 60 minutes: y(60) = 1,000 · e0.0347×60 ≈ 1,000 · e2.08 ≈ 8,000 cells.

Check against simpler reasoning. Sixty minutes is exactly three 20-minute doubling periods, so the population should double three times over: 1,000 → 2,000 → 4,000 → 8,000. Both routes agree. Nothing in the calculation ever referenced a clock reading directly — only how many cells already existed, at every step along the way.

The same bare equation, applied to something with no biology in it at all, is one of the most famous arguments in the history of ideas. In his 1798 An Essay on the Principle of Population, the economist Thomas Malthus argued that an unconstrained human population grows “geometrically” — in the language of this lesson, following dy/dt = ky with k > 0 — while he argued food production could only grow “arithmetically,” adding a roughly fixed amount each year rather than multiplying. Whatever one makes of the conclusions Malthus drew from that contrast, or of how later economists and demographers have challenged them over the two centuries since, his underlying mathematical point stands on its own: a quantity growing in proportion to its own size will eventually overtake any quantity that only adds a fixed amount per year, no matter how large that fixed amount is. It is the same curve as the bacteria culture, just relabeled.

Equation / Model

dy/dt = ky

dy dt equals k times y.

y
the changing quantity, as a function of time
t
time, the independent variable the rate of change is measured against
dy/dt
the instantaneous rate of change of y with respect to time
k
the constant of proportionality: positive k means y grows faster the larger it already is; negative k means y shrinks, and more slowly as it gets smaller

Finding the general solution to an arbitrary differential equation from a blank page takes a technique of its own, not today’s job. But you can already check a good guess, and doing so once is worth it, because it shows exactly what “satisfies the equation” means. Guess that y = Cekt, for some constant C, might work — exponential growth is exactly the shape a rate proportional to the current amount tends to produce (Day 6’s lesson on exponents already named this shape). Differentiate the guess, the way Day 10 taught:

d/dt (Cekt) = kCekt = k·y

The derivative of C times e to the kt equals k times C times e to the kt, which equals k times y.

The derivative of Cekt with respect to t is kCekt — the chain rule carries the constant k out front and leaves the exponential otherwise unchanged. But Cekt is just y again, by the guess itself, so that last expression is exactly k·y. The derivative of the guess equals k times the guess, for every value of t and for any constant C whatsoever — which is precisely what dy/dt = ky demands. The guess checks out.

And “for any constant C” is exactly the general solution promised above: every choice of C traces out a different curve, and all of them obey the identical rule. Set t = 0 in y = Cekt and C is just y(0), the quantity’s value at the very start — which is exactly the initial condition, the one fact that reaches into the infinite family and pulls out the single curve matching reality.

Why It Matters

Day 10’s derivative gave you a tool for measuring how fast something is changing right now. Day 11’s integral and its Fundamental Theorem gave you a tool for turning a known rate back into a total. A differential equation is the natural next question raised by both: what do you do when the rate of change is all you’re given, with no ready-made formula for the quantity itself handed to you in advance? That is the situation almost every real quantitative model of a changing system actually starts from — nobody discovers a direct formula for population or temperature by observation; they discover the local rule it obeys, moment to moment, and then have to recover the global behavior from it.

That is also why this equation keeps reappearing far outside mathematics. Equations shaped like dy/dt = ky, or close variations on it, describe radioactive decay, the cooling or heating of an object, the charging of a capacitor, the growth of an investment compounding continuously, and the early stage of almost any population or epidemic, long before any of those fields had a name for what they were writing down. The same short equation, same reasoning, same two extra ideas — a whole family of curves, and one starting fact that picks the real one out of it.

It is also worth noticing something a little strange about the reasoning in Equation / Model: checking that a guess works only required differentiating it and comparing the result to the original equation — a purely mechanical step. Nothing about that check explains why exponential growth, of all possible shapes, is the right guess for this particular rule. That deeper question — why a rate proportional to the current amount forces exponential behavior, and not some other curve — is exactly what a genuine method for solving differential equations from scratch would need to answer. Today’s lesson deliberately stops at verification, so that the shape of the question is solid before the machinery for answering it gets built on top.

Remember These
  • A differential equation relates a quantity to its own rate of change; solving one means finding a function, not a number.
  • dy/dt = ky is the most common basic model: a rate of change directly proportional to the quantity itself — growth if k is positive, decay if k is negative.
  • A differential equation alone describes a whole family of curves, the general solution; one initial condition — the starting value — picks out the one particular solution that actually happens.
  • An equilibrium is a constant value where the rate of change is zero; a system already there stays there, and other starting values typically drift toward or away from it.
  • You can check whether a guessed function solves a differential equation by differentiating it and comparing the result to the rule — long before you know how to find that guess from scratch.
Think About It

Under dy/dt = ky with k > 0, a population grows without any limit at all, forever — the model has no equilibrium except zero, and nothing in the equation ever slows the growth down. No real population, bacterial or otherwise, actually does this indefinitely; every real colony eventually runs short of food, space, or something else it needs. Using the idea of equilibrium from today’s lesson, what would have to be true of a more realistic growth equation — one built for a population approaching a limited environment’s capacity — that isn’t true of this one?
