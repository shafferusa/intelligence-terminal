Top Stories

Chips end a nine-day slide and tech leads a narrow rebound, while the rest of the market slips

The Nasdaq won back about half of Friday’s loss. The equal-weight S&P fell, and rate-sensitive sectors led the decliners.

Futures were higher all night and the bounce held. The Nasdaq Composite closed up 1.11% at 26,430.89 and the S&P 500 up 0.41% at 7,690.73 (Yahoo Finance; CNBC reports the same closes). That recovers about half of Friday’s Nasdaq loss of 2.15% and leaves the index 3.0% below where it was a week ago. The Dow slipped 0.07%.

The gains were concentrated. The VanEck semiconductor fund (SMH) rose 1.6% to 548.46, its first gain after nine straight losses that took it about 18% below its June high. Marvell rose 4.1%, Arista 3.4%, Lam Research 3.1%, Micron 3.0%, Intel 2.4%, Broadcom and AMD 1.8% each and Nvidia 1.4%. Amazon rose 5.0% to $262.45 after Morgan Stanley named it a top pick and raised its price target to $350, citing faster growth at its cloud business, according to several outlets; that is a documented catalyst. Tesla rose 3.8% ahead of its results on Wednesday.

The rest of the market did not join in. The equal-weight S&P 500 fund fell 0.3%, the mirror image of last week, when it beat the cap-weighted index. Utilities fell 2.0%, real estate 1.2% and financials 0.8%. CoreWeave, the GPU-rental company at the centre of last week’s credit scare, rose about 4% early after a Goldman Sachs upgrade, then gave it back to close down 0.4% at $61.12. Volume in CoreWeave was more than twice its 20-day average.

Why chips rose today has no single confirmed answer. Reuters points to easier oil prices and Treasury yields, but yields rose. The likelier explanation is the simplest: after the longest losing streak for the Philadelphia chip index since 2008, by Reuters’ count, buyers came back, helped by named analyst upgrades of Intel, Amazon and CoreWeave.

Why it matters

A rally that leaves the average stock behind is a fragile one. Today’s gain rested on a few large technology names while the parts of the market most exposed to interest rates fell, which is what you would expect if investors are buying back last week’s losers but still worry about 5% yields.

What’s next

Netflix and possibly SpaceX report Tuesday; Tesla, IBM, Lam Research, Texas Instruments and Meta on Wednesday. Meta’s spending plans are the week’s real test of the AI trade.

Sources: Yahoo Finance closing data (checked against Twelve Data); CNBC; Reuters via the Detroit News; Bloomberg; Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan and Goldman notes as reported by Yahoo, GuruFocus and Investing.com.

The 10-year yield climbs back to 5.15% as Treasuries give up part of last week’s rally

Long bonds fell on a day with no major data. The curve steepened, and anything priced off long rates suffered.

The 10-year par yield rose 6 basis points to 5.15% and the 30-year 5 to 5.48%, while the 2-year rose 3 to 4.77% and the 3-month bill 1 to 4.27% (Treasury par yield curve). Last week the 10-year fell 15 basis points; today took back about 40% of that. The gap between the 2-year and 10-year widened to 38 basis points. Inflation-protected Treasuries (TIP −0.3%) and investment-grade corporate bonds (LQD −0.5%) fell with them.

No single release explains it. There was no major US data besides builder sentiment, and the Fed is silent until Oct. 28. Axios reports hedge-fund-driven swings in Treasuries; CNBC described the 10-year as back near its highest in about 24 years. That is a description of the move, not a cause.

Why it matters

At 5.15% the 10-year sets the floor under mortgage rates, corporate borrowing and stock valuations. Utilities (−2.0%) and real estate (−1.2%), which compete with bonds for income investors, had the worst days of any sector.

Sources: US Treasury daily par yield curve, read directly; CNBC; Axios; Yahoo Finance.

Builder confidence falls to its lowest since 2012, and 43% of builders are cutting prices

The NAHB index dropped three points to 29 in October, below every forecast.

The National Association of Home Builders/Wells Fargo index fell to 29 from 32, its lowest since June 2012 and below the median forecast of 31 (NAHB; Bloomberg survey). Every part fell: current sales to 33, expected sales over the next six months to 32, and buyer traffic to 18. The share of builders cutting prices rose to 43%, the highest of this cycle, and 65% used incentives such as mortgage-rate buydowns. NAHB’s own headline: “Builder Sentiment Falls on Rising Rates.” The index is a survey of sentiment, not a count of homes, but it has led housing starts in the past. September housing starts come out Tuesday at 8:30 a.m.

Homebuilder stocks had already fallen with rates. The read-through is wider: price cuts by builders feed into new-home prices, and a weaker housing market is one of the few channels through which the Fed’s September hike is plainly working.

Sources: NAHB release as reported by RISMedia and Inman; Bloomberg consensus as reported.

Cleveland-Cliffs jumps 16% after a quarter that more than doubled its operating profit

The steelmaker beat on earnings, raised its outlook and won an upgrade; Steel Dynamics missed after the bell.

Cleveland-Cliffs reported adjusted earnings of $0.28 a share, against estimates of about $0.19–0.24, and adjusted EBITDA (operating profit before interest, taxes and depreciation) of $612 million, up from $286 million in the second quarter and above the company’s own guide of about $575 million (8-K). Revenue was $5.6 billion, slightly below the roughly $5.71 billion expected; shipments were 4.2 million tons. GAAP net income was $165 million. It guided fourth-quarter EBITDA to about $715 million, according to reports of its call. Wells Fargo upgraded the stock to overweight. The shares rose 16.3% to $14.10 on about five times their usual volume.

After the close, Steel Dynamics reported earnings of $5.26 a share on sales of $6.5 billion; analysts expected about $5.48 on $6.43 billion, and the company’s own September guidance was $5.34–5.38 (company release; Investing.com). One quote feed showed its shares sharply lower after hours; we have not seen that confirmed.

Sources: Cleveland-Cliffs 8-K (SEC EDGAR); Steel Dynamics release; Investing.com; Yahoo Finance.

Oil eases as Iran claims a third mined tanker, and no one confirms any of them

WTI fell about 3% and Brent held near $109; the gap between the two benchmarks widened to about $11.

West Texas Intermediate traded near $98.40 late in the day, down about $3, and Brent near $109.30, down about 50 cents (Yahoo Finance; these are late-session futures quotes, not official settlements). Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said a third laden tanker hit a mine south of the Strait of Hormuz, after claiming two on Saturday; the Joint Maritime Information Center said there was “no substantiation.” Some traders pointed to Vice President JD Vance’s weekend remark that the US had “won decisively” in some ways against Iran, read as a hint of de-escalation; that is commentary, not a documented cause. Energy shares fell 0.6%.

Brent, the global benchmark priced off seaborne crude, is the one exposed to the Gulf; WTI, priced in Oklahoma, fell more as the weekend’s US waiver of Russia oil sanctions eased supply worries, a plausible but unproven link. Brent is up 80% this year.

Sources: Yahoo Finance; Joint Maritime Information Center and Tasnim via Middle East Eye; FX Leaders; CBS News.

Regional banks report clean credit, but bank shares fall with the market’s rate-sensitive half

Zions earned $1.78 a share with almost no loan losses; the big banks fell 1.6–2.5%.

Zions reported third-quarter earnings of $262 million, or $1.78 a diluted share, against $1.48 a year earlier, with net charge-offs at an annualized 0.02% of loans and a release of $8 million from loan-loss reserves (company release). One consensus figure was about $1.65. Its net interest margin slipped to 3.23% from 3.28%. Western Alliance earned $2.45 a share, up 7.5% from a year ago, with deposits steady at $81.8 billion; BOK Financial earned $2.64, down from $2.92 (company filings). W.R. Berkley’s operating earnings rose to $1.17 a share from $1.10, and Crown Holdings guided full-year adjusted earnings to $8.35–8.45 a share.

The shares told a different story from the results. Wells Fargo fell 2.5%, Schwab 2.6%, Citigroup 2.1%, JPMorgan 1.8% and Bank of America 1.6%; the regional bank fund KRE fell 0.8%. No single bank event explains it. A steeper yield curve usually helps bank margins, so the selling fits better as part of the day’s rotation than as a judgement on earnings.

Sources: Zions, Western Alliance, BOK Financial, W.R. Berkley and Crown Holdings filings (SEC EDGAR); Yahoo Finance.

What Changed Today

  • The chip selloff. This morning: futures pointed to a bounce. Now: the bounce happened, but it was narrow, and the average stock fell. Confidence that the AI-credit worry has passed: low. CoreWeave’s fade shows it has not.
  • Treasury yields. This morning we thought the 10-year would most likely close at 5.12% or lower. It closed at 5.15%. That call was wrong, and the long end, not the stock market, is where today’s stress showed. Confidence: high (Treasury).
  • Housing. Previous understanding: high rates were cooling housing slowly. New information: builder confidence at a 14-year low and 43% cutting prices. Housing is now cooling fast. Confidence: moderate (one survey).
  • Oil. This morning Brent was near $108.70. It ended the session near $109.30, little changed, while WTI fell about 3%. Iran’s third mine claim did not move prices much, which suggests traders are discounting unconfirmed claims. Confidence: moderate (indicative quotes).

The Economy

Today’s data. The NAHB housing index fell to 29 (covered in Top Stories). It is a sentiment level, not a rate of change: builders are gloomier than at any point since 2012, not merely gloomier than last month. No other major US release.

The Fed. In its quiet period until Oct. 29; no officials are speaking. The target range is 3.75–4.00% after September’s increase, the first since 2023. CME futures, as reported, put the chance of a hold on Oct. 28 at about 84%. Goldman Sachs still expects a final quarter-point increase in December. Chair Kevin Warsh said on Friday that he does not prejudge meetings. Official policy, market pricing and economists’ forecasts remain three different things.

The Treasury. The 13-week bill sold at 4.095% with a bid-to-cover of 2.77, and the 26-week at 4.220% with 2.84, on $95 billion and $82 billion of offerings (TreasuryDirect). Foreign and other indirect bidders took $55.2 billion and $52.2 billion of the competitive awards. Demand for short bills is solid; the worry is at the long end. Tuesday brings $95 billion of 6-week bills; Wednesday’s $13 billion 20-year reopening is the week’s test of long-bond demand. The curve today: 3-month 4.27%, 2-year 4.77%, 5-year 4.94%, 10-year 5.15%, 30-year 5.48%. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Sunday’s talk shows that September’s inflation was an energy shock that will pass with the Iran conflict.

Inflation expectations and real rates. On Friday the 10-year breakeven, the bond market’s inflation forecast, was 2.35%, and the 10-year real yield (TIPS) 2.74% (FRED). Most of the 10-year’s level is real yield, not expected inflation: investors are demanding more to lend, not forecasting runaway prices.

Credit. The high-yield spread widened to 3.31 percentage points on Friday from 3.23, its widest since our record began in late July but still narrow by history (ICE BofA via FRED). High-yield bond funds were flat today.

Global. European stocks fell about 1% (Stoxx 600 −0.9%, per the Irish Times, which cited unrest in France; we could not confirm what it referred to). Japan’s Nikkei fell 0.6%. Hong Kong was closed for a holiday. China’s central bank sets its loan prime rates tonight; it has held the one-year at 3.00%. The yen weakened to about 158.5 per dollar.

The United States

Justice Department charges 28 in a California voter-fraud sweep. Federal prosecutors charged 28 people across California with lying on naturalization applications and registering to vote illegally, the Associated Press reports. The department says all 28 are lawful permanent residents, not citizens, and that it has charged 113 people in 20 states with illegal voting or registration offenses in recent weeks. It blamed California officials for poorly maintained voter rolls; it is already suing the state over them. Sen. Alex Padilla (D-Calif.) called that lawsuit “baseless, partisan.” Studies have found noncitizen voting to be rare, and it has not been shown to change outcomes. These cases are at the charging stage; no one has been convicted.

Supreme Court leaves the president’s NLRB firing in place. Today’s order list declined, without comment, to disturb an appeals ruling that Congress’s limits on removing National Labor Relations Board members are unconstitutional (Bloomberg). The fired Democratic member stays out. The Court also turned away appeals over the Pentagon’s decision to void plea deals for Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and his co-defendants, so pretrial proceedings at Guantanamo go on (CNN). Turning away a case sets no nationwide precedent, but it leaves the lower-court ruling in force.

Trump waives the Russia oil sanctions Congress passed. Late Friday the president used the national-interest waiver in the Russia sanctions law sponsored by the late Sen. Lindsey Graham to stop sanctions, and tariffs on the largest buyers of Russian oil, that were due to take effect Sunday (CBS News, Fox Business). On Sunday he wrote that he did not want to push Russia away from talks and that Ukraine’s refinery strikes were hurting diesel markets. The waiver is tied to a US purchase of Russian diesel agreed on the Oct. 9 Trump–Putin call. Supporters say it keeps Moscow negotiating and eases diesel prices; critics in Kyiv and in Congress say it removes the pressure the law was written to apply.

Midterms, 15 days out. Early voting began today in Texas, where a University of Houston poll of likely voters has James Talarico (D) at 48% and Ken Paxton (R) at 46% for the Senate, within its margin of error of about 2 points (KERA). The RealClearPolitics average has Talarico ahead by about 2. Nationally, polling averages of the generic congressional ballot show Democrats ahead by 8 to 9 points (Silver Bulletin D+8.9, RCP D+8.0); individual polls range from D+2 to D+12. Polls are snapshots, not predictions. Congress is out: the Senate held only a pro forma session, and the government is funded through Dec. 11 under September’s stopgap.

The World

Iran claims a third tanker hit a mine; no one else has confirmed any of them. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said late Sunday that a third laden tanker struck a mine south of the Strait of Hormuz and caught fire, after claiming two on Saturday (Tasnim, via Middle East Eye and Gulf News). It has named no ship, flag or owner. The Joint Maritime Information Center, which tracks Gulf shipping for Western navies, said there was “no substantiation” for the claims. What is better documented: UKMTO counted at least 10 vessels attacked in or near the strait in the week to Saturday, the most of the war, and says it is under-counting. Ship counts disagree sharply. US Central Command says about 400 merchant ships transited in the week to Oct. 16 and that the strait is open; independent trackers and Kpler show Gulf crude flows of about 8.5 million barrels a day last week, down from more than 20 million. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi says Iran’s seven-day plan stands: if the US lifts its blockade and oil sanctions, the strait reopens on day six and talks begin on day seven. Washington says the nuclear program comes first.

The mine claims rest on Iranian state media alone and are disputed.

Ukraine and Russia trade their heaviest blows on energy. Ukraine’s weekend drone attack on St. Petersburg, the largest on the city in the war, killed one person and injured 15, set an oil terminal and depot on fire and cut power to about 20,000 people (Meduza, France 24, Moscow Times). Russia’s Defense Ministry claims it downed 1,015 drones across 15 regions; that number cannot be checked. Overnight into Monday, Russia hit Kyiv and the energy grid with ballistic and cruise missiles and 269 drones, killing one person in the capital (AP). President Zelensky says more than 10 energy sites were damaged and about 550,000 families lost power.

Putin floats a three-way summit without Zelensky. The Wall Street Journal reports that Vladimir Putin has proposed a Trump–Putin–Xi meeting on Ukraine, possibly at the APEC summit in Shenzhen on Nov. 18–19. A senior US official says Trump has not agreed; Secretary of State Marco Rubio said “there’s been talk”; the Kremlin says discussions are under way. One outlet’s report, confirmed by both governments only as talk.

Israel votes in eight days. A Times of Israel poll gives Gadi Eisenkot’s party 26 seats to Likud’s 20, with neither bloc reaching a 61-seat majority; Trump endorsed Prime Minister Netanyahu today (Time). Lebanese, Israeli and US military officers meet in Tampa on Tuesday on the June framework linking Hezbollah’s disarmament to Israel’s phased withdrawal (Axios).

AI & Technology

Banks shut the door on outside AI agents. Charles Schwab chief executive Rick Wurster told Bloomberg Television that Schwab blocks outside AI agents that try to move client money, naming Meta’s Muse assistant, and that it stops the big agents “in the tens and twenties” of times a week. Read-only access through Schwab’s own connectors is still allowed, and clients are steered to Schwab’s assistant. JPMorgan imposed a similar block on Oct. 13, Jamie Dimon has said (American Banker, TNW, Cybernews). The read-through: the banks intend to own the AI layer that touches customer money, which slows any agent business that depends on access to it.

An AI-cloud IPO meets price resistance. Investors want Nscale, the Nvidia-backed AI-cloud company that filed to list on the NYSE on Oct. 8, to accept a valuation of $16–19 billion rather than the $30–35 billion it sought, Bloomberg reports. Its filing shows first-half revenue of $140.6 million and a $1.02 billion net loss, and a $45 billion, six-year contract with Anthropic that makes one customer most of its future. Science

A possible “dark galaxy.” Astronomers using China’s FAST radio telescope report a cloud of hydrogen, J1351+0039, with the gravitational mass of about 130 million suns and no stars seen in it (Phys.org, Oct. 17). If it holds up, it would be one of the few known galaxies made almost entirely of gas and dark matter. The authors themselves say deeper optical images are needed to rule out a faint galaxy hiding there, so for now it is a candidate.

Bandwagon-5 scrubbed; Tuesday’s try opens at 6:30 a.m. SpaceX called off this morning’s Falcon 9 rideshare from Cape Canaveral because of weather in the booster recovery area (Spaceflight Now). It carries 32 payloads, including the second flight of SpaceX’s uncrewed “Starfall” reentry capsule. There is still no official date for Starship’s 15th flight.
