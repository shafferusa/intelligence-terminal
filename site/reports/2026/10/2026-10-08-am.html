Top Stories

Oil jumps 4% after report the White House wants Iran strike options before the midterms

The Atlantic reports the White House asked the Pentagon for strikes it could launch before November 3. No decision has been made. A tanker was hit off Qatar, deep inside the Gulf, and the hurricane has shut a quarter of US Gulf output.

Brent crude was near $104.42 a barrel at about 6:20 this morning, up about 4.2% from Wednesday’s settlement near $100.20. US crude (WTI) was near $92.06, up about 4.3% (Yahoo, delayed).

The move followed a report in The Atlantic late Wednesday, citing two administration officials. It says the White House asked the Pentagon to prepare options for new strikes on Iran that could be carried out before the November 3 midterm elections. The size and targets are undecided, and a wider operation could follow after the vote. Axios separately reported that Central Command was told to prepare options. A White House official said the president “has all options available at any time.” Bloomberg, CBS and Israel Hayom have relayed the report, but none has independently confirmed the details. No strike has been ordered.

Shipping risk added to it. UK Maritime Trade Operations reported a tanker hit by several projectiles Wednesday evening, about 51 nautical miles north of Madinat ash Shamal, off Qatar. That is in the western Gulf, well inside the strait, and one outlet called it the first such attack there in weeks. No one has claimed it and Qatar has not commented. On Tuesday, Indian officials said 12 crew were hurt when the Panama-flagged On Peace was struck in transit. A Navy-led centre counted at least a dozen attacks from September 28 to October 5. The ship tracker Kpler says Tuesday’s flows through the strait were the lowest since late July. The IEA puts traffic at under 10% of pre-war levels.

There is now a supply problem at home too. Ahead of Hurricane Isaias, about 511,000 barrels a day of Gulf of Mexico output, roughly a quarter of the total, is shut in and eight platforms are evacuated (CNBC and Reuters, Wednesday). Diplomacy has not helped. President Trump rejected Iran’s latest proposal because it would only partly open the strait. Iran’s president, Masoud Pezeshkian, called talks with the US “meaningless.”

Why it matters The Fed’s September minutes, out Wednesday, said most officials expect another rate hike this year, and many cited energy prices. Oil at $104 makes that harder to ignore. It also feeds the inflation expectations the bond market is already demanding more yield for. Exposure, not direction: energy producers and tanker owners on one side, airlines (Delta reports tomorrow), chemicals and consumer staples on the other.
What’s next Any White House or Pentagon comment on the report. Whether Isaias, now forecast to reach Category 2, tracks east of the Louisiana refining belt as expected. The IEA board settles the details of a new G7 crude and diesel release on October 14–15.

Deeper analysis: why this oil move is different from last week’s

Brent has spent two weeks between about $100 and $103 on a steady run of Hormuz attacks. Today’s jump has three causes stacked together: a possible US military escalation, an attack in a new part of the Gulf, and a domestic hurricane outage. Only the hurricane has a clear end date. Unlike a Hormuz incident, a US strike would put Iran’s own export and Gulf infrastructure in play. That is the scenario this year’s emergency stock releases were meant to cushion: the IEA says about 325 million of the 400 million barrels announced in March have been delivered. Natural gas is up about 1.7% and gold barely moved, so this is an oil-specific move, not a broad flight to safety. Inflation expectations in the bond market had been flat, with the 10-year breakeven at 2.36% for a week.

After a blowout 10-year sale, the 30-year reopening is today’s test

The 10-year sale was strong on every measure: it priced below where it traded beforehand, foreign and other indirect bidders took 80%, and dealers were left with almost nothing. The 30-year comes with oil up 4% and the long bond near 2002 highs.

Treasury sold its $39 billion 10-year reopening Wednesday at a high yield of 5.300%. That is a stop-through, meaning below the yield it traded at just before the 1:00 deadline. The bid-to-cover ratio was 2.77. Indirect bidders, the group that includes foreign central banks, took $31.06 billion, about 80%. Direct bidders took $6.62 billion and primary dealers only $0.98 billion, about 2.5% (TreasuryDirect results). The 10-year had touched 5.365% earlier in the day, its highest since 2002. It ended at 5.28% on Treasury’s official curve, up 1 basis point on the day.

Today Treasury reopens the 30-year bond for $22 billion, with bids due at 1:00 (TreasuryDirect). The 30-year closed at 5.67% on the official curve and the 20-year at 5.71%. The curve keeps getting steeper: 2s10s is 51 basis points, and 5s30s is 64. The 2-year, at 4.77%, has fallen 6 basis points since last Friday as markets weighed a softer labor market. The 10-year has not.

Why it matters Wednesday showed there is strong demand at 5.3% for 10-year debt. The 30-year is the auction most exposed to how much more investors want to be paid for lending long. A weak result with oil spiking would put the 2002 highs back in play. A second strong one would suggest the selloff is being bought.

Waller: more hikes likely, but not necessarily in October

The Fed governor spoke in Istanbul hours after the minutes showed most officials expect another hike this year.

Speaking at 4:30 this morning at an economic forum in Istanbul (Fed calendar), Governor Christopher Waller said more rate increases are likely needed. He added that the Fed has “flexibility” on timing and that hikes need not come at consecutive meetings, according to Reuters and Bloomberg summaries. Reuters read that as leaving room to hold on October 28. We have not read the text. A claim on one crypto news site that he discussed a 75-basis-point move appears nowhere else, and we are not reporting it.

Wednesday’s minutes of the September 15–16 meeting said “most participants” saw another increase as likely by year end, and many pointed to energy prices. They gave no timing. That was the meeting where the Fed raised its range to 3.75–4.00%. Futures-derived estimates relayed by CNBC put the odds of an October hike at about 18–22%, and about 69% for a hike by December. We could not reach CME’s own tool. Today Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari and St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem are scheduled to speak, according to a market preview; their times are not confirmed.

Hurricane Isaias, now 80 mph, is aimed between Mississippi and the Florida Panhandle

The season’s first hurricane is expected to reach Category 2 before landfall late Friday or early Saturday.

The National Hurricane Center’s 5 AM ET advisory put Isaias about 420 miles south-southwest of the mouth of the Mississippi River, with maximum winds of 80 mph and moving east-northeast at 9 mph. A hurricane warning runs from Ocean Springs, Mississippi, to the Bay–Gulf county line in Florida, which includes the Alabama coast. A storm surge warning covers the coast from the mouth of the Mississippi to the Steinhatchee River. Florida has declared a state of emergency. The center forecasts strengthening to about 110 mph late today or early Friday. AccuWeather says Category 3 over the central Gulf is possible. Rainfall of 3–7 inches, locally 10, is forecast from southeast Louisiana to the Panhandle. We saw the advisory as quoted by CNN, CBS and weather.com; the hurricane center’s site did not load for us.

Why it matters The forecast track keeps the core east of the Louisiana refining belt, but offshore production is already a quarter shut in. Also exposed: property and casualty insurers and Gulf Coast utilities.

PepsiCo beats on revenue, cuts its profit outlook and promises more cost cuts

Sales are up on price and currency, but rising input costs are squeezing earnings. The stock closed Wednesday at a 52-week low.

PepsiCo reported third-quarter net revenue of $25.27 billion, up 5.6% (organic +3.1%), before the open. That is above the roughly $24.96 billion analysts expected (LSEG, via CNBC). Core earnings were $2.34 a share, up 2%, against a consensus near $2.30. It cut its full-year core EPS growth outlook to 2.5–3.5%, from the low end of 5–7%. In constant currency the outlook falls to 1–2%. It raised revenue growth to about 6%. Core operating profit included a 4-point benefit from tariff refunds. Chief executive Ramon Laguarta cited “rising input cost inflation” and promised “additional structural cost reduction actions” (8-K exhibit 99.1). The stock closed Wednesday at $123.73, its lowest in a year, and was about 0.5% higher in early premarket trade (Yahoo, delayed). The call is at 8:15.

The most fragile assumption. The S&P 500 is pricing an energy shock that stays contained: oil high but not rising, long yields high but bought at auction, and a Fed that waits until December. The leadership is narrow, which is how an index can sit 0.5% from a record while most stocks fall. What would invalidate it: a US strike on Iran that takes Brent through about $110, a tail at today’s 30-year auction, or a jump in breakevens that makes an October hike the base case. Any one of those would test whether the mega-cap leaders can keep carrying the index.

Risks & Scenarios

The 30-year auction. We put the odds that today’s $22 billion reopening clears at or through its pre-auction level (no tail) at 50–65%. Basis: Wednesday’s 10-year stopped through with a very small dealer takedown, and 5.6–5.7% yields have drawn buyers all week; against that, the long bond is most exposed to oil-driven inflation fears and to term-premium selling on a 4% oil day.

An October Fed hike. We put the odds of a hike on October 28 at 15–30%, unchanged. Basis: futures price about 20%. Waller explicitly allowed for skipping a meeting, and payrolls were soft. Oil above $100 and the minutes’ emphasis on energy argue the other way. The September CPI on October 14 decides it.

A US strike on Iran before the election. We put the odds of new US strikes on Iranian territory announced before November 3 at 20–40%. Basis: The Atlantic and Axios report that options are being prepared and no decision has been made. Trump has rejected Iran’s latest proposal, and talks are stalled. But preparing options is routine and does not mean they will be used. That is a labelled analytic judgment, not a prediction of policy.

Isaias. We put the odds that Isaias makes US landfall as a hurricane by October 11 at 85–95%. Basis: it is already a hurricane, warnings are up, and the forecast calls for strengthening before a late-Friday or early-Saturday landfall between Mississippi and the Florida Panhandle.

Science

Nobel week. The Literature prize is announced at 7:00 this morning and Peace tomorrow; economics follows Monday. The science prizes went to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel for optogenetics (medicine), Francis Halzen for the IceCube neutrino observatory (physics), and Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai for non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric synthesis (chemistry).

Optogenetics in the clinic. In a timely coincidence, this week’s New England Journal of Medicine carries GenSight Biologics’ phase 1/2 PIONEER results for GS030, a gene therapy that makes surviving retinal cells light-sensitive in late-stage retinitis pigmentosa. The paper is about safety; vision gains are exploratory and the trial is small (company release via BioSpace).

In Nature this week, peer-reviewed: an on-chip optical frequency comb generated between two laser pumps, a step toward chip-scale clocks; a chiral superlattice in the antiferromagnet UOTe with possible uses in spintronics; and a study of gut-microbe species that travelled with human populations and are disappearing from industrialized guts. We saw abstracts, not full papers.

Climate. A peer-reviewed study in Nature Geoscience led by Rutgers’ Robert Kopp finds that emissions choices made now largely set how much Antarctica adds to sea level by 2100. Swiss glacier monitors (GLAMOS) report the country’s glaciers lost 5.5% of their volume this year, the second-worst on record.

Up to about 328 million SpaceX (SPCX) shares become sellable Friday in the first post-IPO unlock, with a second tranche of similar size in late October and most of the rest after third-quarter results, according to trackers working from the prospectus; Musk’s shares stay locked until June 2027. NASA’s moon-base launch buy. Bloomberg reports NASA is close to a bulk purchase of launches for its lunar base effort, likely across four or five rocket types each paired with a commercial lander, in a program valued around $30 billion. Single outlet No award has been announced.

Korea’s Nuri succeeds again. South Korea’s Nuri rocket put all five NEONSAT Earth-observation satellites into a roughly 570-kilometre orbit Wednesday and deployed nine of ten cubesats, its fourth straight success and the first launch led by Hanwha Aerospace rather than the state research institute (KASA/KARI via Korean press).

Next up. A Falcon 9 carrying 21 Space Development Agency Tranche 1 transport satellites from Vandenberg has been listed for early this morning and for Saturday by different sources; we could not confirm whether it flew. China’s Long March 12 is set for Friday from Wenchang, and the CRS-35 cargo flight to the station around October 13 (Launch Library). Blue Origin’s New Glenn remains grounded after May’s pad explosion.

Also in the News

Ebola reaches Kenya. Kenya confirmed its first Ebola case, a man who travelled by road from Beni in eastern Congo to Kampala and flew to Nairobi on October 3; he died late October 5, and the Bundibugyo strain was confirmed October 6 (Health Cabinet Secretary Duale, via AP, Al Jazeera, Washington Post). Twenty-nine family members and health workers are in quarantine and 27 passengers and crew are being traced. Kenya is the fourth country with a case linked to the eastern Congo outbreak, now above 8,000 cases. WHO advises against travel restrictions.

A death at a Russian plague institute. A worker at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Institute died after being hospitalised September 29 with severe pneumonia. Russia’s health regulator says the cause is unknown and denies a lab breach; Russian outlets report pneumonic plague. Unconfirmed About 197 contacts are isolated. The WHO said Wednesday it lacks the information for a risk assessment and has asked Russia for details (NPR, CBS).

Pennsylvania

Skill games must go by Monday; State Police begin seizures Tuesday

The state Supreme Court ruled in June that so-called skill games are illegal slot machines outside licensed casinos and qualifying truck stops. The removal deadline is Monday, October 13. State Police say machines may be seized, and owners charged, from October 14, and that unplugging a machine is not enough. The Attorney General estimates about 70,000 machines statewide, in bars, clubs and convenience stores. A proposed carve-out for fraternal and social clubs from Sen. Wayne Fontana (D-Allegheny) is still seeking co-sponsors, and the biggest supplier, Pace-O-Matic, says it will disable its machines by the deadline. We found no legislative fix that would stop enforcement.

Why it matters For VFWs, fire halls and bars across the South Hills, machine revenue has paid bills for years. That ends next week unless Harrisburg acts, and the legislature shows no sign of it.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

City Council proposes tighter oversight of the mayor’s budget

Pittsburgh City Council budget chair Erika Strassburger presented a reform package Tuesday. It would put quarterly council budget meetings into law and give Controller Rachael Heisler access to the city’s budgeting software. It would create a public dashboard of purchasing-card spending and require notice to council before money moves between funds. Some pieces are bills not yet introduced. Mayor Corey O’Connor’s preliminary 2027 budget, released September 30, is $728 million with no tax increase or layoffs. It draws about $26.5 million from reserves and projects a gap of about $250 million over five years. Formal introduction is in early November, with votes by year end. Separately, County Executive Sara Innamorato’s $1.25 billion county budget, with no property-tax increase, is now before County Council.
