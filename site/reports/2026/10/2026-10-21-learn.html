Nobody Could Measure How Fast BP’s Well Was Gushing. A Federal Court Still Had to Add Up 87 Days of It.

Wednesday, October 21, 2026 · 13 min read

An integral is nothing more than a rate multiplied by a thin slice of time, added up across every slice until the slices vanish — the one move that turned 87 days of a Gulf oil well’s disputed, ever-changing flow into a single number a federal court could fine.

A leak a mile down, and a number nobody could see

On 20 April 2010 the Deepwater Horizon drilling rig exploded in the Gulf of Mexico, killing 11 of the 126 workers aboard. The rig had been drilling BP’s Macondo well, nearly a mile below the surface, and when the blowout preventer failed, oil began pouring from the wellhead into the Gulf. It kept gushing for 87 days, until a capping stack finally sealed it on 15 July.

No instrument sat inside the well measuring the flow directly — the leak was a mile down, through a tangle of broken pipe, and nobody could put a meter on it. The first official estimate, given with BP, was 1,000 barrels a day; on 28 April the Coast Guard and NOAA raised it to 5,000. Independent scientists working from video of the plume gushing out of the broken riser argued the true number was many times higher, triggering a public fight over whose estimate to trust. The government’s own Flow Rate Technical Group, led by USGS Director Marcia McNutt with Energy Secretary Steven Chu’s scientists, spent months refining the number. On 2 August it published a final figure: the well had leaked at roughly 62,000 barrels a day when it first blew out, declining to about 53,000 barrels a day by the time it was capped as the reservoir’s own pressure fell — some 4.9 million barrels in total, give or take 10%. BP’s own experts, defending the company against Clean Water Act penalties that ran as high as $4,300 for every barrel spilled, argued for a far smaller total: about 2.45 million barrels. Debated

Somebody had to turn 87 days of a disputed, ever-changing daily rate into one number a federal court could put a fine on. How do you add up a flow nobody could watch directly, that changed every day, when the two sides’ own estimates differ by more than a factor of two?

The Big Idea

An integral adds up a rate, multiplied by a tiny slice of time, over and over, to find a total. If you know how fast something is happening at every moment — barrels a day, meters per second, dollars per year — and you want to know how much happened in total over some stretch, you multiply the rate by a thin slice of time and add the slices together.

If the rate never changed, this would be plain multiplication: 50 miles an hour for two hours is 100 miles, rate times time. The whole difficulty, and the whole reason calculus needs a name for this, is that real rates change constantly — Macondo’s daily flow fell from 62,000 to 53,000 barrels across 87 days, never holding still. The integral is what multiplication becomes once the rate is allowed to wiggle: chop the stretch of time into pieces so short the rate is nearly constant across each one, multiply rate by width on every piece, and add. As the pieces shrink toward nothing, that sum settles on an exact total — the area under the graph of the rate, plotted against time.

How It Works

Start with the crudest possible way to total a changing rate: pretend it holds steady over short stretches, and add. Imagine a reading of the well’s flow once a day for all 87 days. For day one, multiply that day’s rate by one day’s width; for day two, multiply that day’s rate by another day’s width; and so on, adding every day’s contribution. Each piece is a rectangle on a graph of rate against time: its height is the rate during that stretch, its width is how long the stretch lasted, and its area — height times width — is how much flowed during that one piece. Mathematicians call this a Riemann sum, after Bernhard Riemann, who gave the idea of approximating area under a curve by rectangles its rigorous footing in the 1850s. Add up every rectangle’s area, and you have an estimate of the total.

A Riemann sum built from daily readings is a blunt tool, because it assumes the rate sits exactly constant across an entire day and then jumps instantly to a new value the next — real flows do not behave that way. One easy improvement is the trapezoid rule: instead of a flat-topped rectangle on each interval, draw a straight line connecting that interval’s two endpoint readings, making each piece a trapezoid rather than a rectangle. A trapezoid’s area is the average of its two parallel sides’ heights, times its width — so the trapezoid rule is really just “average the two endpoints, then multiply by the width,” repeated on each interval and summed. It tracks a smoothly changing rate more closely than flat rectangles, because the estimate is allowed to rise or fall continuously across each interval instead of jumping.

Both methods are approximations built from a finite number of pieces, and both grow more accurate as the pieces get thinner — more rectangles, or more trapezoids, each one narrower. The definite integral is what either approximation approaches as the number of pieces grows without bound and each piece’s width shrinks toward zero: an exact total, not an approximation of one. Mathematicians write it ∫ from a to b of r(t) dt — read “the integral, from time a to time b, of the rate r of t, with respect to t” — naming exactly the limiting value every one of those sums is approaching. The symbol ∫ itself is an elongated S, chosen by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the 1670s as shorthand for “sum,” because that is literally what an integral is: a sum of infinitely many, infinitely thin pieces.

None of this is a new kind of mathematics, unconnected to Day 4. Day 4’s derivative took a quantity — the Keeling curve’s carbon dioxide record — and extracted its instantaneous rate of change, the slope of the curve at a single point. The integral runs that process backwards: given a rate, it rebuilds the quantity that was changing. The fundamental theorem of calculus, discovered independently by Leibniz and Isaac Newton in the late seventeenth century and still the hinge the whole subject turns on, states this precisely: if A(t) is the running total accumulated from some starting point up to time t — A(t) = the integral from a to t of r(s) ds — then A’s own derivative, A′(t), equals r(t) right back again. Accumulate a rate, then differentiate the result, and you return to the original rate; differentiate a quantity, then integrate the result, and you return to the original quantity, up to a starting value. Rates and totals are two faces of the same relationship, and the fundamental theorem is the statement that going one direction and then the other undoes itself exactly.

The units carry this logic before any arithmetic does. A rate’s units are always some quantity’s units divided by time — barrels per day, meters per second, dollars per year — and a tiny slice of the integral, rate times a sliver of time, cancels that “per day” back out, leaving plain barrels, meters, or dollars. An integral’s units are always the rate’s units times time, the exact inverse of what a derivative does to units by dividing by time. That is not a coincidence worth memorizing separately; it is the fundamental theorem showing up in the units before you have computed a single number.

Apply this to Macondo. Treat the flow as falling in a roughly straight line from about 62,000 barrels a day at the start to about 53,000 barrels a day near the end — a simplification, since the real decline was not perfectly linear, but close enough to show the method. A straight-line decline’s trapezoid-rule total collapses to one line of arithmetic: average the two endpoints, (62,000 + 53,000) / 2 = 57,500 barrels a day, and multiply by the 87-day width: 57,500 × 87 ≈ 5.0 million barrels. That simple hand calculation lands within about 2% of the Flow Rate Technical Group’s own, far more careful 4.9 million-barrel estimate, built from pressure readings, video analysis and acoustic sensors rather than one straight line. The method, done crudely with two numbers, already gets most of the way to the method done rigorously with months of data.

Back to the story

Every contested number in the Macondo dispute lived in the rate, never in the arithmetic of adding it up. BP’s lawyers and the government’s scientists agreed, in effect, on how integration works; they disagreed about what the rate actually was on any given day, because nobody had a meter a mile under the Gulf of Mexico. Once the Flow Rate Technical Group settled on a rate curve — falling from roughly 62,000 to roughly 53,000 barrels a day as reservoir pressure dropped — turning that curve into a total was the easy part: the same trapezoid arithmetic above. Judge Carl Barbier’s January 2015 finding of about 4 million barrels discharged, less roughly 0.8 million captured at the wellhead before it reached the water, for a net 3.19 million barrels, was a legal judgment about which rate estimate to credit, landing between the government’s and BP’s competing curves — not a disagreement about what an integral is or how to compute one.

Key Concepts

Accumulation
A total built up from a quantity’s rate over time — the area under the graph of the rate, plotted against time.
Riemann sum
An approximation of that area using narrow rectangles: rate times a thin slice of time, added across every slice.
Trapezoid rule
A more accurate approximation that averages each slice’s two endpoint rates before multiplying by its width, tracking a smoothly changing rate more closely than flat rectangles.
Definite integral
The exact value a Riemann sum or trapezoid-rule estimate approaches as the slices become infinitely many and infinitely thin; written ∫ from a to b of r(t) dt.
Fundamental theorem of calculus
The rule connecting the two halves of calculus: accumulating a rate, then taking that accumulation’s own rate of change, returns you to where you started.
Units of an integral
A rate’s units (quantity per time) times time, which cancels the “per time” back out — the opposite of what a derivative does to units.

Example: An Electric Car’s Dashboard Runs on an Integral

An electric car’s trip energy sits at the car chain’s physical-constraint link: however clever the engineering, the battery can only deliver the energy the trip’s physics actually demands. The dashboard’s instantaneous power reading, in kilowatts, is a rate — exactly like the well’s barrels per day — and it swings constantly: tens of kilowatts climbing a grade, briefly negative when regenerative braking turns the motor into a generator and pushes energy back into the battery instead of pulling it out. The battery gauge’s kWh-used figure is that power curve’s integral: the signed area under the power-versus-time graph, with braking’s negative power literally subtracting area rather than adding it. A car’s two dashboard numbers, kW and kWh, are a derivative-and-integral pair exactly like the ones Day 4 and today have been discussing, and the EPA’s own kWh-per-100-miles efficiency label is that accumulated energy divided by distance traveled.

1. Break the trip into stretches of roughly steady power. 20 minutes of level driving at 15 kW; 10 minutes climbing a pass at 40 kW; 10 minutes descending the other side at −10 kW (regenerative braking returning energy).

2. Convert minutes to hours, since power is in kilowatts. 20 min = 1/3 hour; 10 min = 1/6 hour, twice.

3. Multiply each stretch’s power by its own width in hours. (1/3) × 15 = 5 kWh; (1/6) × 40 ≈ 6.67 kWh; (1/6) × (−10) ≈ −1.67 kWh.

4. Add the three pieces — a Riemann sum with only three rectangles. 5 + 6.67 − 1.67 = 10 kWh used for the whole trip.

5. See what the negative piece did. Without regenerative braking’s −1.67 kWh, the trip would have cost 11.67 kWh; the descent gave back almost 15% of that.

Real dashboards do this with power sampled many times a second rather than three blocks, which is exactly the move from a coarse Riemann sum to something much closer to the true integral — more, thinner rectangles, converging on the number the battery gauge actually displays.

Equation / Model

The integral, as the limit of a sum of rate times a thin slice of time:

Total = ∫ from a to b of r(t) dt ≈ Σ r(ti) Δt

The total equals the integral, from a to b, of the rate r of t, with respect to t — approximately equal to the sum, over every slice i, of the rate at that slice times the slice's width, delta t.

r(t)
the rate being measured at time t — for example, barrels of oil leaking per day
Δt

a, b

The fundamental theorem of calculus, linking today’s integral to Day 4’s derivative:

If A(t) = ∫ from a to t of r(s) ds, then A′(t) = r(t)

If A of t is the integral from a to t of the rate r of s, then A prime of t — the derivative of A — equals r of t again.

A(t)
the running total accumulated by time t
A′(t)
A’s own derivative — its instantaneous rate of change

Applied to the spill: treating the flow as declining in a straight line from 62,000 to 53,000 barrels a day across 87 days, the trapezoid rule’s single-interval case gives Total ≈ [(62,000 + 53,000) / 2] × 87 ≈ 5.0 million barrels — close to, but not identical to, the Flow Rate Technical Group’s own 4.9 million-barrel figure, which modeled the real, not-quite-linear decline.

Why It Matters

The reason to separate a rate from its total is that mistaking one for the other is one of the most common ways a true number gets used to make a false argument. A well’s flow rate falling — 62,000 down to 53,000 barrels a day — sounds like good news on its own, and in one narrow sense it is: less oil leaking each day, later on, than at the start. But the total kept climbing every single day the well flowed, right up to the moment it was capped, because a falling rate is still a positive rate, and a positive rate integrated over time never subtracts from the running total. Whenever you hear that something is “slowing down” — emissions growth, a budget deficit, a disease’s case count — the next question has to be whether the thing itself has started shrinking, or whether it is still only growing more slowly, which is a very different, often far less reassuring, fact.

It is also why a disputed total, like Macondo’s, is never really a dispute about adding. Once you can name exactly which part of a calculation is contested — the rate curve, not the integration — you know where to put your scrutiny: not on the arithmetic, which anyone can check, but on the measurement or model that fed it.

Remember These
  • An integral adds up a rate times a thin slice of time, across every slice, to build a total — the area under the rate’s own graph.
  • The fundamental theorem of calculus says accumulating a rate and then differentiating the result return you to where you started.
  • A falling rate still adds to the total every moment it stays positive — “slowing down” is not the same claim as “shrinking.”
  • Uncertainty in a total almost always comes from uncertainty in the rate, not from the arithmetic of adding it up.
  • A dashboard’s power reading (kW) and its energy gauge (kWh) are a derivative-and-integral pair.
