Today’s News

Iran says two loaded tankers hit mines in Hormuz and offers to reopen the strait within a week on its terms; Saudi Arabia shoots down more missiles overnight

The claim is Iran’s alone and the US has called similar ones false. For the oil market, which reopens at 6 PM, uncertainty about whether ships can pass safely does damage on its own.

Iran’s state news agency IRNA, quoting the Revolutionary Guard’s navy, said on Saturday that two oil tankers caught fire after hitting naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz. It said each carried about 2 million barrels of crude and that both had switched off their tracking transponders and radar to use an “unauthorised route.” Iran has not named either ship, given its flag or said what happened to the crews. Reuters and Al Jazeera carry the claim. We have found no independent confirmation from UKMTO, Britain’s maritime security agency, or from shipping trackers. US Central Command has not responded to this claim. It called earlier Iranian mine claims false: it said the container ship GFS Pride, which sank on Wednesday, was hit by a Revolutionary Guard projectile, and that the main lanes have been cleared of mines. Iranian claim only

On the diplomatic track, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told a meeting of Islamic states in Baghdad on Saturday that Iran would talk again if it received “credible guarantees” against new attacks. He also restated a seven-day plan passed to Washington through Qatar. Under it, the strait would reopen on day six and talks would begin on day seven, if the US lifts its naval blockade and oil sanctions and a ceasefire takes hold on every front, Lebanon included. “The choice is now with America,” he said (Tasnim, Middle East Monitor). The president rejected a version of these terms in September and has said he has “offered them NOTHING.” On Sunday Iran’s and Pakistan’s foreign ministers spoke about de-escalation. Campaigning in Kentucky on Saturday, the president said the war would end “very quickly.”

The fighting with the Houthis continued overnight. Shortly after 1:30 AM local time on Sunday, civil-defence alerts sounded in Riyadh and Khamis Mushait. The Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed two ballistic missiles aimed at Khamis Mushait and three drones, two of them near Riyadh, and reported no damage (Saudi Gazette, Reuters). Riyadh’s King Khalid airport has been shut since the Oct. 10 strike that killed 12. Its closure notice lapsed at 5 PM Eastern on Saturday, but we have not seen Saudi Arabia’s aviation authority confirm a reopening. Several airlines are staying away: Oman Air until Oct. 22, Air France until Oct. 24, British Airways until Nov. 3.

What it means for the week Brent settled at $109.86 on Friday, its highest since May. Ships are still moving: the US military says about 400 crossed the strait last week, and the tanker tracker Kpler puts flows at about 70% of pre-war levels. The price is high because nobody can say how safe the passage is, not because barrels have been lost. A mine claim, even one that is never confirmed, raises insurance costs and makes shipowners slower to sail. The first read is when futures reopen at 6 PM; the second is whether Washington answers the seven-day plan.
What’s next A US Central Command or UKMTO account of the two ships; the US reply to Iran’s proposal; whether Riyadh’s airport actually reopens. The president has pledged no US strike on Iran before the Nov. 3 election.

Ukraine sends about a thousand drones into Russia overnight, setting a St. Petersburg oil terminal on fire

It is one of the largest attacks of the war, a week after the president announced an “energy ceasefire” that neither side ever confirmed, and a day after he relaxed parts of a new Russia sanctions law.

Russia’s defence ministry said it brought down about 1,015 Ukrainian drones between 8 PM Saturday and 8 AM Sunday Moscow time, over Moscow and the western regions. St. Petersburg’s governor, Alexander Beglov, said one person was killed and five injured. Fires broke out at the St. Petersburg Oil Terminal and the Ruchyi oil depot, and a dozen apartment buildings were damaged. In the surrounding Leningrad region, a logistics centre and a data centre were reported hit and about 20,000 people lost power (CNN, ABC, Anadolu, Kyiv Independent). The drone count is Russia’s own figure. We have seen no reaction yet from the Kremlin or the White House.

Russia attacked Ukraine’s grid all last week and launched 332 drones and missiles overnight into Saturday. Separately, Bloomberg, TIME and Al Jazeera report that the president signed an order on Friday waiving parts of the new Russia sanctions law, including tariffs of up to 100% on countries that buy Russian oil and gas. Sen. Thom Tillis says the waiver may be illegal; Ukraine’s president called it a gift to Moscow. It comes on top of the US license, issued two weeks ago, for Russian diesel exports.

Why it matters St. Petersburg’s oil terminal is one of Russia’s Baltic outlets for fuel exports, and diesel was already tight before the Gulf war. Strikes on refineries and terminals keep diesel prices high, and diesel costs feed directly into the producer prices that rose 1.3% in September. Washington is easing on Russian oil as Ukraine hits it harder, and markets will watch which wins.

Pope Leo leads the Angelus a day after leaving hospital following lung-cancer surgery

He said he will “gradually resume” his work; his surgeon sees no medical reason to cancel next month’s trip to Latin America.

Pope Leo XIV, 71, was discharged from Rome’s Gemelli hospital on Saturday, earlier than expected, five days after surgeons removed the lower lobe of his right lung for an early-stage adenocarcinoma. At noon Rome time on Sunday he led the Angelus from the Apostolic Palace window. He thanked people for their prayers, said he felt “particularly close to all those who are living with illness,” and said he would “gradually resume my activities.” His surgeon, Stefano Margaritora, said there was “no clinical reason” against the planned November trip (NPR, OSV News, ABC).

France’s cost-of-living protests turn violent as Limoges’s town hall burns

The 1883 building is “largely lost,” the prefect says, as the government seeks votes to pass its budget.

Protests over living costs across France on Saturday ended with looting in several cities and a fire that brought down the roof and spire of Limoges’s town hall. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said 1,200 people were arrested and 150 police officers injured, and blamed the “ultra-left.” Turnout estimates differ: about 550,000 according to the interior ministry, 800,000 according to the CGT union (Euronews, i24). Other outlets give lower arrest figures. Lecornu needs Socialist votes to pass his 2027 budget and avoid a no-confidence vote. We have found no motion yet scheduled for this week.

A fire at a Canadian army base in Alberta kills an unconfirmed number of people

The defence minister confirmed deaths but gave no number; one outlet, citing military sources, reports at least seven.

The fire started in a training area at Canadian Forces Base Wainwright around 4 PM local time on Saturday and spread beyond the base on high winds. Defence Minister David McGuinty confirmed deaths and injuries without giving figures, and Edmonton hospitals declared a “code orange.” Single source Global News, citing unnamed military sources, reports at least seven dead and dozens injured, and says a live-fire exercise may have hit a gas line or well. The military said on Sunday morning that the situation was “still evolving” (CBC, Globe and Mail, CNN).

Canadian Department of National Defence via CBC, the Globe and Mail, CNN, NBC · Global News (casualty count and cause, single source).

Two Pacific storms: Trudy restrengthens off Mexico, and a super typhoon passes just south of Guam

Neither had caused reported deaths by Sunday morning.

The AP reported Hurricane Trudy back at Category 2, with 100 mph winds, about 115 miles west-southwest of Manzanillo and moving north-west. A hurricane warning runs from Manzanillo to Cabo Corrientes. The centre is forecast to run along the coast rather than come ashore, with 4–8 inches of rain, and up to a foot in places, in Michoacán, Colima and Jalisco, then weaken from Monday. In the western Pacific, Champi became a super typhoon as its eye passed about 10 miles south of Guam. The National Weather Service there said a direct hit “would have been devastating.” First reports, according to the Guam Daily Post, show no widespread serious damage, but the island’s south had not yet been surveyed.

Top Themes

1. Who pays for AI. Last week’s selloff was not about whether demand for AI is real: TSMC and ASML both said it is. It was about the companies that borrow to build data centres, and whether lenders will keep funding them. This week the biggest buyers and builders report. Meta’s spending guidance, Tesla’s AI and robot-taxi plans, Lam Research’s and Texas Instruments’ order books and IBM’s software growth will show whether the build-out is slowing. Nothing has yet answered whether CoreWeave can refinance.

2. Oil at $110 against a bond market betting on slowdown. Brent is at a five-month high, and the weekend added risk on two fronts: the Hormuz claims and the strike on St. Petersburg. Yet the 10-year fell 15 basis points last week, which says bond investors think oil will hurt growth more than it stokes lasting inflation. Wednesday’s 20-year auction and Friday’s business surveys test that bet.

3. A Fed that has gone quiet. No Fed speeches until the decision on Oct. 28. Futures price about an 85% chance of no change. With no Fed comment until then, the only things that can move those odds are oil, Friday’s inflation expectations from the University of Michigan survey, and the business surveys’ price gauges.

4. Rotation, or the start of something broader? Last week the average stock rose while the index fell: equal-weight S&P +0.5%, staples +5.0%, energy +4.9%, technology −7.7%. If big-tech results stop the chip slide, that is a healthy rotation. If they don’t and credit starts to widen, it becomes a correction. High-yield spreads, at 3.23 points, are the gauge.

5. Sixteen days to the election. Pennsylvania’s registration deadline is Monday, Texas starts early voting the same day, and the Justice Department’s suit over California mail ballots awaits a first ruling.

The Economy Ahead

The Fed is silent; the market decides. The target range is 3.75–4.00% after September’s hike. The blackout runs until the day after the Oct. 27–28 meeting, so no official will steer expectations this week. On Friday, CME FedWatch put the chance of an October hike at about 13–18%, down from roughly 50% after Wednesday’s CPI. One secondary reading puts December at close to 90%. Goldman Sachs expects a pause in October and a hike in December. That shift came from the bond rally, not from anything the Fed said. Chair Warsh’s last word was “I don’t prejudge meetings.” With no speeches to watch, the October odds will follow oil and Friday’s inflation-expectations figure.

The data. A light week. Tuesday: September housing starts, expected around 1.30 million at an annual rate after 1.275 million, with the 30-year mortgage at 7.40%. A miss would support the bond market’s slowdown view. Thursday: jobless claims, expected around 200,000. They have stayed between 198,000 and 207,000 for two months, so a jump above 220,000 would be the first sign of layoffs reaching the numbers. The Conference Board’s leading index comes out the same day. Friday: the S&P Global flash PMIs, which were very strong in September (composite 58.4); a preview expects them to “ease slightly.” Their input-price gauges will show whether energy costs are moving through to what businesses charge. Then the University of Michigan’s final October survey. The preliminary reading was 46.3, close to May’s record low of 44.8, with one-year inflation expectations at 4.7% and five-to-ten-year at 3.5%. If long-run expectations move above 3.5%, it would be the clearest sign yet that the oil shock is changing how people expect prices to behave. That matters more to the Fed than another hot monthly number. All of these are first prints, and none of them can settle the October decision by itself.

The Treasury. Supply this week is $95 billion of 13-week and $82 billion of 26-week bills on Monday, $95 billion of 6-week bills on Tuesday, a $13 billion reopening of the 20-year bond on Wednesday at 1 PM, and $26 billion of new 5-year TIPS on Thursday at 1 PM (TreasuryDirect). The 20-year par yield is 5.47%, the highest point on the curve, and since it was reintroduced in 2020 it has often drawn softer demand than its neighbours. A weak auction, with a high yield well above where it traded beforehand and a light take from end investors, would show whether last week’s rally rested on short-covering. The TIPS sale tests demand for inflation protection with 10-year breakevens at 2.35%. Next week brings two-, five- and seven-year notes, and the November refunding announcement follows the Fed meeting. Separately, Treasury’s waiver of parts of the Russia sanctions law (see Today’s News) adds to the questions senators of both parties have raised about the diesel license.

Abroad. China reports third-quarter growth tonight at 10 PM ET; the Reuters poll expects 4.4% from a year earlier, below the 4.5–5% target. The People’s Bank is expected to hold its loan prime rate on Tuesday. A miss would weigh on copper and on Asian markets already hit by the chip selloff. Britain’s September CPI comes out Wednesday (August 3.1%). Japan’s national CPI comes out Thursday evening our time (August core 1.7%); the Bank of Japan meets Oct. 29–30, the day after the Fed, and Governor Ueda said last week that rates will keep rising. Flash PMIs for the euro area, Germany and Britain come out Friday; the euro area’s September composite was a 41-month high of 53.1. Turkey’s and Hungary’s central banks meet. The European Central Bank (Oct. 29) and the Bank of Canada (Oct. 28) follow next week.

Scenarios

Base case, about 45–55%: earnings steady the market without settling the debt question. Meta keeps spending, Tesla and IBM are mixed, and chips stop falling but don’t rally much. The S&P 500 ends Friday between 7,506 and 7,812, within 2% of its current level. The 10-year stays between 4.95% and 5.25% and Brent between $100 and $115. October hike odds stay below 30%. Confirmers: SMH within 3% of 539.79 on Thursday; the VIX back below 20; the 20-year auction clears close to where it traded beforehand. Invalidators: a Meta spending cut, Brent above $115, the VIX above 25.

Bull case, about 15–20%: the selloff proves overdone. Meta raises spending and beats, Lam reports strong orders, and CoreWeave finds financing. SMH rises 5% or more, the S&P ends Friday above 7,812, and the VIX falls below 17. Iran and the US move toward talks and Brent eases toward $100. Confirmers: a CoreWeave financing announcement; high-yield spreads back near 3.10; a Washington reply to Iran’s seven-day plan.

Bear case, about 20–30%: AI-debt fears spread. A big buyer trims spending or a lender pulls back, CoreWeave is downgraded or fails to raise money, and the chip slide resumes. The S&P ends Friday below 7,506, more than 4% off its record. The VIX closes above 25 at some point and high-yield spreads reach 3.40. Bonds rally further unless oil spikes. Confirmers: SMH down another 5%; HYG at a new 52-week low (it is 0.6% above it); the Nasdaq-100 losing its 200-day average.

Shock case, about 5–10%: the Gulf hits supply. A confirmed strike damages Gulf production or exports, or a ship is confirmed lost to a mine in the main lanes. Brent settles above $120 at least once this week, the October hike comes back into play and stocks and bonds fall together. This would override the other scenarios.

What would change the outlook. Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

The Wabash Tunnel vote is Friday; county budget hearings start Wednesday

Pittsburgh Regional Transit’s board votes on Friday, Oct. 23 on staff’s recommendation to close the 123-year-old Wabash Tunnel to traffic for good on Dec. 1. Staff say keeping it open would need about $43 million of work, with lighting, ventilation and electrical systems rated poor to critical, for about 1,500 cars a day and no buses. Federal regulators have already approved the closure. The tunnel would not be demolished and the Wabash park-and-ride stays open. South Hills drivers would shift to the Fort Pitt and Liberty tunnels. We put approval at 65–80%, since it is a staff recommendation with federal sign-off in hand (WESA, CBS Pittsburgh, TribLive). County Council’s budget committee holds hearings on Wednesday, Oct. 21, and Oct. 29 at 5 PM in the Gold Room on County Executive Sara Innamorato’s $1.25 billion plan, which has no tax increase and cuts 150 vacant positions; it must be adopted by Dec. 6. In the city, Mayor Corey O’Connor’s 2027 plan draws about $26–27 million from reserves, and the controller warns reserves are nearing critical levels.
