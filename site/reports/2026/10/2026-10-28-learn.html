Willard Libby Checked His Atomic Clock Against Timber From a Pharaoh’s Tomb. Within a Few Centuries, It Kept Time.

Wednesday, October 28, 2026 · 13 min read

A differential equation states how fast a quantity changes in terms of how much of it is currently there — the same rule, run forward as decay and backward as a clock, that let Willard Libby turn a fixed half-life into a working atomic calendar, and that he had to check, before anyone would trust it, against timber from a pharaoh’s tomb.

Mathematics

Day 6 of 293 · Mathematics 6 of 24 · Differential equations: rules for change

A chemist’s clock, checked against a pharaoh

In the late 1940s, Willard Libby, a chemistry professor at the University of Chicago, worked out a new kind of clock. Every living thing takes in a small, steady trickle of radioactive carbon-14 from the air while it is alive; once it dies, that intake stops, and the carbon-14 already in its tissue decays away at a fixed pace. If Libby could measure how much was left, he reasoned, he could say how long ago the thing had died. The trouble was that no one had ever tested such a clock against anything independently dated.

So Libby and a young chemist named James Arnold went looking for wood and charcoal whose ages were already known by other means, with no radioactivity involved in fixing those ages at all. One sample, a fragment of a giant California sequoia known as the Centennial Stump, felled in 1874, carried tree rings the dendrochronologist A. E. Douglass had already counted back to a known span, 1031 to 928 B.C. — a date fixed simply by counting rings, decades before anyone measured the wood’s radioactivity. Another sample came from Egypt: acacia wood from the tomb of the pharaoh Djoser (then usually written Zoser) at Saqqara, supplied by Ambrose Lansing of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, whose age — from Egyptian chronology and the written record, not from radioactivity — was put at about 4,650 years. Libby and Arnold measured each sample’s faint carbon-14 activity with shielded Geiger counters and compared the radiocarbon age the measurement implied against the age historians and dendrochronologists had already assigned, independently, by entirely different methods. Their paper, “Age Determinations by Radiocarbon Content: Checks with Samples of Known Age,” appeared in Science on December 23, 1949. The agreement, they wrote, was satisfactory — within the few centuries their method’s precision allowed. Plotted as one chart, known age against radiocarbon age, it became known as the Curve of Knowns. Libby won the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the method it validated.

How can a rule about how fast atoms vanish tell you how long ago something died? And why would anyone trust that rule enough to publish ages from it before a single independent check had been run?

The Big Idea

A differential equation is a rule that states how fast a quantity is changing right now in terms of how much of that quantity currently exists — not a formula for the quantity itself, but a rule connecting the quantity to its own rate of change. Carbon-14’s decay obeys one of the simplest kinds there is: dN/dt = −kN, read “the rate at which the amount N is changing equals a negative constant k times N itself.” In plain words: the fewer carbon-14 atoms remain, the slower they disappear, in direct proportion.

A rule like that, by itself, describes a whole family of possible histories — it says how any amount would change, not which amount you actually have. Pin down one more fact, the quantity’s value at some starting moment (its initial condition), and the differential equation fixes everything else: the quantity’s entire future, and, run in reverse, its entire past. That reversibility is exactly what turns a decay rule into a clock.

How It Works

Start with what the equation is actually claiming. dN/dt is calculus notation — Day 4’s derivative — for the instantaneous rate at which N, the number of carbon-14 atoms in a sample, is changing at a given moment. Setting that rate equal to −kN says the rate of loss is always proportional to the amount currently present: twice as much carbon-14 decays twice as fast, in absolute terms, even though the fraction lost per unit time, k, never changes. That constant-fraction behavior is Day 2’s exponential growth running backwards: instead of a quantity multiplying by a fixed factor greater than one each period, it multiplies by a fixed factor less than one. The mathematics is identical; only the sign of the rate, and the direction the quantity moves, differ.

Because the relative rate of change (dN/dt)/N is pinned at the constant −k, the only function that satisfies the equation is an exponential: N(t) = N0·e−kt, where N0 is the amount present at time t = 0 and e is the same base used for continuous compounding. You can check this solves the equation by differentiating it: the derivative of N0e−kt with respect to t is −k times N0e−kt itself — that is, −kN, exactly what the equation demands. The decay rate k is a property of the carbon-14 nucleus alone, fixed by nuclear physics; nothing about temperature, chemical form or pressure changes it, which is why it makes a trustworthy clock mechanism in the first place.

A convenient way to describe that constant k is the half-life: the time it takes for exactly half of any starting amount to decay, written t½. Set N(t½) = N0/2 in the solution above: 1/2 = e−k·t½. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides and rearranging gives t½ = ln 2 / k — a fixed number, the same whether you start with a gram of carbon-14 or a thousand grams, because halving time depends only on the fraction remaining, never on the starting amount. Carbon-14’s currently accepted half-life is 5,730 years, set at the 1962 Radiocarbon Conference in Cambridge; Libby’s own 1949 work used an earlier value, about 5,568 years, now called the Libby half-life and still used by convention in some older date listings even though it is roughly 3% short of the modern figure.

Running the equation backwards is what makes it a clock rather than just a decay law. If you measure how much carbon-14 activity N remains in a sample today and you know (or can reasonably assume) the activity N0 it started with when the organism died, you can solve N = N0e−kt for the elapsed time: t = (t½ / ln 2) × ln(N0 / N). Measure half the original activity left, and the elapsed time is exactly one half-life, 5,730 years; measure a quarter left, and it is two half-lives, 11,460 years. The differential equation’s forward direction — predicting how much will remain after a known time — and its backward direction — inferring elapsed time from how much remains — are the same equation, solved for different variables.

That backward solution rests on two separate assumptions, and they are not equally solid. The first, that k itself stays constant, is settled nuclear physics: a radioactive nucleus’s decay rate does not drift with time, climate or chemistry, so this half of the method needed no empirical rescue. The second assumption is far less automatic: that N0, the starting level of carbon-14 relative to ordinary carbon in a once-living thing, has stayed roughly constant across the span the method is used for — because that starting level depends on how much carbon-14 the atmosphere itself was carrying when the organism was alive, which in turn depends on cosmic-ray intensity, the strength of Earth’s magnetic field, and (in the twentieth century) human activity. Libby could not derive that assumption from physics the way he could the first one; it needed direct testing against samples whose ages were already known by some other method entirely. That is exactly what the Curve of Knowns did: it is a test of the second assumption, not the first, and the physics behind the first assumption is part of why Libby felt justified publishing before every wrinkle in the second was ironed out.

The Curve of Knowns passed for samples a few thousand years old, but the second assumption did not hold up perfectly once the method was pushed further back. Libby himself raised the possibility, as early as the mid-1950s, that the atmosphere’s carbon-14 level might have drifted over longer stretches of time than his first checks could probe. In 1967 the chemist Hans Suess published the first real calibration curve, built by radiocarbon-dating wood from bristlecone pines — trees whose rings, in California’s White Mountains, run back thousands of years further than the samples Libby had used — and found exactly the drift he had worried about: radiocarbon ages and true calendar ages diverge by centuries once you go back far enough. That is why every radiocarbon date reported since has to be run through a calibration curve (today’s IntCal series, continually extended and revised) before it is read as a calendar year, and why “radiocarbon years” and calendar years are not quite the same unit.

Back to the story

That division between the two assumptions is the answer to both halves of the opening puzzle. The decay rule itself, dN/dt = −kN, is a statement about nuclear physics that holds however humans use it, which is why Libby could trust the clock’s mechanism — its forward law — before running a single check. What genuinely needed testing was whether the atmosphere's carbon-14 level had actually stayed roughly constant for as far back as the method would be asked to reach, and the Curve of Knowns tested exactly that, by comparing radiocarbon ages against ages already fixed by tree rings and Egyptian chronology. The timber from Djoser’s tomb, at about 4,650 independently known years, and the sequoia whose rings Libby and Arnold could count by hand both came back within a few centuries of their radiocarbon ages — satisfactory agreement for a first test, and good enough to tell Libby the second assumption held, at least approximately, over that stretch of the past.

Key Concepts

Differential equation
A rule stating how fast a quantity changes in terms of the quantity’s own current value, rather than a direct formula for the quantity itself.
Initial condition
The quantity’s known value at some starting moment, which, together with the differential equation, pins down one specific history rather than a whole family of them.
Exponential decay
The solution to dN/dt = −kN: a quantity that shrinks by the same fraction every equal stretch of time, never reaching exactly zero.
Half-life
The fixed time for exactly half of any starting amount to decay, t½ = ln 2 / k — the same number regardless of the starting amount.
Rate constant
The k in dN/dt = −kN: the fixed fraction of what remains that decays per unit time, set by the physics of the specific nucleus and unaffected by its chemical environment.
Calibration
Correcting a radiocarbon age for the fact that the atmosphere’s carbon-14 level has not actually stayed perfectly constant, using an independent clock — tree rings, today extended into the IntCal curves — to translate “radiocarbon years” into calendar years.

Example: A Drug Leaving the Bloodstream

The same differential equation governs how most drugs, including ordinary caffeine, leave the bloodstream: the elimination rate is proportional to how much of the drug is currently circulating, dC/dt = −kC, the identical rule with concentration C in place of carbon-14 atoms N. The National Academies’ Institute of Medicine, reviewing the pharmacology in a 2001 report, put caffeine’s elimination half-life in healthy adults at about five hours on average, with a wide individual range of roughly 1.5 to 9.5 hours; pregnancy and some medicines lengthen it, and smoking shortens it.

1. Start with a dose and its half-life. 200 mg of caffeine at 8 a.m.; assume an average five-hour half-life.

2. Halve it once per half-life. By 1 p.m. (one half-life later), about 100 mg remains.

3. Halve it again. By 6 p.m. (two half-lives), about 50 mg remains.

4. Halve it a third time. By 11 p.m. (three half-lives), about 25 mg remains — an eighth of the original dose, still enough to disturb some people’s sleep.

5. See what repeated dosing does. Taken on a regular schedule rather than once, the same equation says the drug builds toward a steady level, reached after roughly four to five half-lives — which is exactly why a dosing interval is set relative to a drug’s own half-life, not to the clock on the wall.

Equation / Model

dN/dt = −kN  ⇒  N(t) = N0·e−kt,   t½ = ln 2 / k

The rate of change of N with respect to t equals negative k times N. This implies N of t equals N-naught times e to the negative k t, and the half-life equals the natural log of 2, divided by k.

N
the amount of the decaying quantity (carbon-14 atoms, or a drug’s blood concentration) present at time t
N0
the amount present at the starting moment, t = 0 — the initial condition
k
the rate constant: the fixed fraction of what remains that decays per unit time
t½
the half-life: the fixed time for exactly half of any starting amount to decay

The same equation, solved backwards to read elapsed time from a measured fraction remaining:

t = (t½ / ln 2) × ln(N0 / N)

Elapsed time t equals the half-life divided by the natural log of 2, multiplied by the natural log of N-naught divided by N.

t
the elapsed time since the starting moment — the quantity radiocarbon dating actually wants
N0 / N
how many times smaller the remaining amount is than the starting amount; a ratio of 2 gives one half-life elapsed, a ratio of 4 gives two

With carbon-14’s modern half-life of 5,730 years, wood retaining half its original activity is about 5,730 years old; wood retaining a quarter is about 11,460 years old. Those two numbers are the entire method, before any instrument or calibration curve enters the picture.

Why It Matters

Once you can recognize dN/dt = −kN, you can recognize the same rule wearing different clothes everywhere a quantity’s rate of loss (or growth) depends only on how much of it currently exists: a drug clearing the bloodstream, a capacitor discharging, a population declining under a fixed death rate, a radioactive tracer used in medical imaging. The equation does the same two things every time — it predicts the future from the present, and, with enough independent confirmation that its constants genuinely hold, it can be run backwards to infer the past. The second half is the harder-earned one, and the Djoser-timber test is the model for how to earn it: find cases where the answer is already known some other way, and see whether the rule gets them right before trusting it on cases where it is the only clock available.

Across. History’s own chronology was rewritten by this equation’s calibration problem. Colin Renfrew’s Before Civilization: The Radiocarbon Revolution and Prehistoric Europe (1973) argued that once radiocarbon dates were calibrated against tree-ring sequences — correcting for exactly the non-constant atmospheric carbon-14 level this lesson flagged as the method’s weaker assumption — Malta’s megalithic temples came out older than Egypt’s pyramids, and Stonehenge older than the Mycenaean civilization once credited with inspiring it. That overturned the assumption that monument-building in prehistoric Europe had simply diffused outward from a more advanced Near East.

Remember These
  • A differential equation states a quantity’s rate of change in terms of the quantity itself; add a starting value, and it fixes the whole future and past.
  • dN/dt = −kN solves to exponential decay, N(t) = N0e−kt, with a fixed half-life t½ = ln 2 / k that does not depend on the starting amount.
  • Carbon-14’s rate constant k is settled nuclear physics; the method’s real assumption is that the atmosphere’s starting carbon-14 level stayed roughly constant, which needed independent testing and still needs calibration today.
  • Libby tested that assumption, before trusting the method, against samples of already-known age — tree rings and timber from a pharaoh’s tomb.
