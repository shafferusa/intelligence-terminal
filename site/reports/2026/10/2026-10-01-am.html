Top Stories

Global bond rout: the 10-year Treasury tops its 2002 high and Britain’s 30-year gilt yields 6%

Wednesday’s lower inflation reading did nothing for the long end. Overnight, yields rose in London, Tokyo and New York together. That points to a worldwide repricing of long-term debt, not a US inflation story.

Reuters, citing LSEG data, said the 10-year Treasury yield rose as high as about 5.34% overnight, its highest since 2002 and above its 2007 peak. The 30-year touched about 5.66%. By early morning both had eased, with the 10-year near 5.28% and the 30-year near 5.62%, according to a Tickmill market note. Treasury’s official closes on Wednesday were 5.29% for the 10-year and 5.64% for the 30-year, both the highest of this run. Reuters reported that Treasuries had their worst quarter since 1994 in the three months to September.

The selling was not only American. Britain’s 30-year gilt yield rose above 6%, the highest since 1998, according to FXStreet and a Bloomberg headline. One market summary put the 10-year gilt at about 5.49%, up 6 basis points, but we could not confirm that figure from a second source. Japan’s 10-year yield was quoted near 3.1%, also from a single source. The FTSE 100 fell almost 2% at the open, and Investing.com’s headline tied the drop to the gilt move.

The reasons Reuters gives are oil, strong US data and “not enough buyers.” This week’s data fit the last two. Tuesday’s ADP and Chicago PMI readings were strong, and Wednesday’s revision raised second-quarter GDP growth to 2.2% from 1.5%. Supply is the other concern: Treasury auctions 3-, 10- and 30-year debt next week, and Britain and Japan face similar questions about who will buy their long bonds. We are not assigning a single cause. The long end is selling off in all three markets at once, and that is the clearest sign that the pressure is a term premium, the extra yield investors demand for holding long bonds.

Why it matters Mortgage rates, corporate refinancing and stock valuations are priced off the 10-year, not the Fed’s overnight rate. The front end has been easing as hike odds for October fell. The long end has gone the other way. That is a steepening the Fed cannot easily fix, and it is now pulling down rate-sensitive stocks: the Dow and equal-weight S&P lost far more than the Nasdaq in September.
What’s next Jobless claims at 8:30 and ISM manufacturing at 10:00 are the first tests. Payrolls on Friday morning are the big one. A strong report would add to the growth-and-supply story. Then Treasury’s 3-year, 10-year and 30-year auctions run Tuesday through Thursday.

Go deeper

The shape of the curve. On Wednesday’s official curve the 3-month bill was 4.20%, the 2-year 4.88%, the 5-year 5.09% and the 20-year 5.68%. The gap between 2- and 10-year yields widened to 41 basis points, the widest of the past week. The gap between 3-month bills and the 10-year is 109 basis points. The 20-year still yields more than the 30-year, a quirk of thin demand for that maturity. FRED puts the 10-year breakeven inflation rate at 2.36% and the 10-year real (TIPS) yield at 2.91% as of Tuesday. Most of the rise in long yields is real yield, not inflation expectations, which is what a term-premium story looks like.

Micron’s outlook lifts chip stocks worldwide, but its own shares are flat to lower before the open

The memory maker guided about $4.5 billion above consensus. The rally is happening in Tokyo, Seoul and Nasdaq futures more than in Micron’s own stock.

Micron reported fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of $54.23 billion after Wednesday’s close and guided the current quarter to $61.5 billion, plus or minus $1.5 billion, against a consensus near $57 billion. Reuters reported that customers had raised their commitments under Micron’s long-term supply agreements to $32 billion. Premarket reports conflict: Reuters had the shares down about 1%, while Benzinga had them up about 0.4%. The stock closed Wednesday at $1,065.11, unchanged on the day and up about 270% this year. The guided gross margin, about 86.25%, is slightly below the quarter just reported, and reports said investors also focused on higher spending planned for fiscal 2027.

The read-through was stronger elsewhere. The Nikkei rose about 3%, to roughly 68,700–68,840; sources differ on the exact close. Korea’s Kospi rose about 1.6%, according to one market wrap. Korea also reported record September exports of $120.9 billion, up 83.5% on the year. Semiconductor exports rose 263% to $60.3 billion, the first month above $60 billion, according to the Korea Times, Seoul Economic Daily and Xinhua. AMD was up more than 1% before the bell, per Benzinga.

Why it matters Two independent numbers, Micron’s guide and Korea’s chip exports, show the AI hardware boom accelerating into the fourth quarter. That is what keeps the S&P near its highs while rising yields hit everything else. Micron’s flat reaction says much of this is already in the price.

Oil slips to about $97 Brent as Gulf exports near pre-war levels; Iran is still weighing the US reply

The benchmark is now the December contract, about $6 below the November contract that expired Wednesday. Physical flows, not diplomacy, are doing the work.

Brent was about $96.8 a barrel early Thursday, down about 1.3%, according to Trading Economics. TASS said it briefly fell below $96. WTI was near $90.1, down about 0.4%. Oilprice.com cited estimates from Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Kpler that Persian Gulf exports are close to pre-war levels. Kpler put flows through the Strait of Hormuz at about 80% of normal, and JPMorgan’s estimate was as high as 98%. Business Standard reported that Saudi Arabia has resumed exports from its Red Sea port of Yanbu.

Readers should not take the drop from Wednesday’s $103 headline as a crash. That price was the November contract, which expired Wednesday. The December contract, now the benchmark, closed Wednesday near $98.

Diplomacy is unchanged. Iran confirmed Wednesday that Foreign Minister Araghchi had received the US response, sent through Qatari mediators, to Tehran’s seven-day plan to reopen the strait. The government spokesperson gave no verdict, according to the AP. Reports say the sides disagree on sequencing. Iran wants US military and economic pressure lifted, frozen assets released and a safe shipping route agreed before it opens the strait. Washington wants the nuclear file handled at the same time. President Trump called an Axios report that he had offered sanctions relief “untrue,” writing, “I offered them NOTHING.” Iran’s rial hit a record low above 2.5 million per dollar. Eight OPEC+ members meet on Sunday.

Why it matters Oil was one of the reasons Reuters gave for the bond selloff, and Europe’s inflation jump this week was mostly energy. Brent near $97 and falling helps both. If Gulf flows hold near normal, the remaining war premium is in the diplomatic headlines, which can move it either way.

A judge clears the way for Paramount Skydance to close its Warner Bros. Discovery deal, with five years of theatrical-release promises

Twelve states dropped their antitrust suit in exchange for a consent decree. The combined studio must release at least 30 films a year and keep them in theaters for 45 days.

Paramount Skydance told the SEC on Wednesday evening that the US District Court for the Northern District of California had entered a consent decree with California and 11 other states. The decree settles their Clayton Act suit seeking to block the merger and changes the court’s earlier order so the deal can close. For five years, the combined company must release at least 30 films in the US in each of the first two years and 32 a year after that. That includes at least 20 wide releases a year, rising to 21, and at least four independent films. Each film needs a theatrical window of at least 45 days and cannot reach subscription streaming for 90 days. Paramount Skydance shares rose 3.4% on Wednesday to $10.33 (Finnhub). Warner Bros. Discovery rose 0.3%.

Why it matters This was the last major legal obstacle we were tracking for one of the largest media deals in years. The release commitments matter for theater chains, because they guarantee a supply of films and a theatrical window the studios had been shortening.

Russia accuses NATO of planning a Kaliningrad blockade and threatens its “entire arsenal”

Reuters saw the diplomatic note. NATO’s secretary-general told Moscow to stop the nuclear threats.

Russia sent NATO a diplomatic memo on Wednesday, seen by Reuters, claiming the alliance is preparing an air and naval blockade of Kaliningrad, Russia’s exclave between Poland and Lithuania. The memo warned that Russia could respond with its “entire arsenal,” including nuclear weapons, and could strike “decision-making centers.” NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte called the memo unhelpful and told Russia to “stop the nuclear threats.” We found no evidence of a planned blockade. The note follows one of the longest Russian air attacks on Kyiv of the war, which we reported Wednesday.

Why it matters Kaliningrad is the most likely place for a direct Russia–NATO incident, because it sits inside alliance territory. So far markets have treated Russian nuclear rhetoric as noise. European defense stocks and the gas price would react first if that changes.

The White House renames AI “Super Intelligence” and signs a voluntary safety accord with industry leaders

The executive order changes the government’s wording, not the law. The accord has no penalties.

President Trump signed an executive order on Tuesday titled “Inaugurating The Era of Super Intelligence.” It directs federal agencies to use “Super Intelligence” or “SI” instead of “artificial intelligence” in official documents and communications. It gives the President’s science and technology adviser 60 days to propose legislative language defining the term. Lawyers at Freshfields and the IAPP noted that the statutory definition of AI in federal law is unchanged, so the order is linguistic for now. At the same event, executives signed a voluntary “White House Accord on Super Intelligence.” It covers internal controls on frontier models, monitoring, independent outside assessments and board-level oversight, and it has no enforcement mechanism, according to SiliconANGLE. Reports name leaders of Google, Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, xAI and Nvidia among the signers, but the lists differ by outlet. Micron used the new term in Wednesday’s earnings release.

Futures and what they price. The tape is split between AI and everything else. Volatility. The VIX closed Wednesday at 16.34. Its term structure is in normal contango: the 9-day VIX is at 14.2 and the 3-month at 18.4. Options are not pricing stress, even with long yields at multi-decade highs. That is calm, and it could be complacent.

The most fragile assumption. The market assumes rising long yields will hurt only the parts of the index that do not matter for its level. That holds as long as AI earnings keep surprising, and Micron and Korea’s exports say they are. It breaks if the 10-year’s move turns into a drop in mega-cap valuations. The tell would be the Nasdaq falling on a day the 10-year rises 10 basis points or more, with no tech-specific news. On Wednesday it did not.

Risks & Scenarios

The long end keeps going. We put the chance that the 10-year closes at or above 5.35% on Treasury’s official curve on at least one day through October 9 at 40–55%. It touched about 5.34% overnight, next week brings three coupon auctions, and gilts and JGBs are moving the same way. Against that, a soft payrolls print and falling oil are real offsets.

Payrolls. We put the chance that September payrolls are below +100,000 in the first print at 55–70%. The consensus is about 90,000, Bank of America is at 60,000, and claims have stayed low. A weak number helps the front end but has done little for the long end this week.

Europe’s inflation. We put the chance that the euro-area September flash is 3.7% or higher at 40–55%. Germany, France and Italy all beat forecasts this week, MUFG is tracking 3.8%, and the consensus is 3.6%.

Upside scenario. A soft payrolls report, Brent below $95 and a solid 10-year auction could pull the 10-year back toward 5.1–5.2% and broaden the rally beyond tech. Downside scenario. A hot payrolls report, an above-forecast euro-area print and a weak 30-year auction could push the 30-year toward 5.75–5.8%. That would hit rate-sensitive stocks harder and eventually tech multiples too.

Science

A neutron-star merger that left a magnetar behind. A paper in Science Bulletin describes a July 2025 merger event, EP250704a, detected by China’s Einstein Probe and the Chinese-French SVOM satellite. Its X-ray flash lasted about 10 minutes, the longest prompt X-ray emission yet recorded from a merger. The authors argue the energy came from a newborn magnetar, a rapidly spinning, intensely magnetic neutron star, rather than from a black hole. If that holds, some mergers leave a star rather than a black hole. That would tighten estimates of the maximum mass a neutron star can have. The interpretation is the authors’ and has not yet been independently tested.

Pittsburgh region

Duquesne Light’s union contract expired at midnight; whether workers walked out was unconfirmed at press time

IBEW Local 29, which represents about 850 to 1,000 of Duquesne Light’s workers by different counts, had said it would strike at 12:01 AM Thursday without a “fair and equitable contract.” Business manager Josh Ewing told KDKA on Wednesday afternoon that the union had sent its final proposal Tuesday night and had no response. WPXI reported workers gathering around 11 PM. As of this writing we found no report of a deal, an extension or a walkout. Search results showing a “tentative agreement on the eve of a strike” are from September 2023, not this year.

The company’s offer, as reported by TribLive and CBS Pittsburgh, includes raises of 4% this year and 3.25% in each of the next two years, larger increases for some job categories, a $3,000 ratification bonus and 120 more full-time positions. The union says its dispute is mainly about safety, staffing, equipment and training. The company says it is ready to activate “operational continuity plans,” which the union says means bringing in non-union crews from out of state.

Why it matters Duquesne Light serves more than 600,000 customers in Allegheny and Beaver counties, including Bridgeville. A strike would not cut anyone’s power. It could slow outage repairs, which matters with storms in Friday’s forecast, and delay new-service work.

CBS Pittsburgh/KDKA, WPXI, TribLive, Post-Gazette (September 30) · all at search-summary level; we could not open the articles directly.

Pittsburgh

Mayor O’Connor’s first budget: no tax increase, no layoffs, and a reserve fund drawn toward empty

Mayor Corey O’Connor sent City Council a 2027 operating budget of about $728 million and a capital budget of about $145 million on Wednesday. Spending is about $13 million lower than this year. The plan has no tax increase and no layoffs. It eliminates 27 vacant positions, lowers the city’s voluntary extra pension contribution by about $7 million a year, and freezes the Stop the Violence and Housing Opportunity funds, moving about $20 million into the general fund. It also draws about $26.5 million from reserves. The administration projects a gap of about $250 million over five years without further changes, according to WESA and PublicSource. Council hearings come next. Some council members support the Stop the Violence Fund and are likely to contest the freeze.

City of Pittsburgh release · WESA, PublicSource, WPXI, Post-Gazette.

Pennsylvania

Skill-game machines become illegal outside casinos in two weeks, and Harrisburg looks unlikely to act first

The state Supreme Court ruled in June that “skill games” are slot machines under the Gaming Act and stayed its ruling for 120 days. The Pennsylvania State Police say that from October 14, machines outside licensed casinos are illegal and subject to seizure. Senate Majority Leader Joe Pittman said this week he is “not overly optimistic” that a regulation-and-tax deal will pass before then, according to abc27. Thousands of bars, clubs, gas stations and convenience stores across the state, including in the South Hills, host the machines. For many fraternal clubs they are a large share of revenue.

Pennsylvania State Police (primary) · abc27, CDC Gaming Reports.
