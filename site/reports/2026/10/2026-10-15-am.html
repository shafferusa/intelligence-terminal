Top Stories

The chip rout goes global: Seoul’s Kospi falls 7.6% after a trading halt, Tokyo drops nearly 5%, and Nasdaq futures follow

This was the worst day yet of a selloff that started in Korean memory stocks on Monday. It has now spread to Japan, Taiwan, European chipmakers and US futures, and TSMC’s record profit did not stop it.

Seoul’s Kospi closed down 7.57% at 5,554.22 on Thursday, its seventh straight fall and the sharpest of the run (Yahoo Finance). Trading was halted for 20 minutes at 12:38 local time when a circuit breaker tripped, the tenth of the year, Reuters and the Korea Herald report. SK hynix fell about 11% and Samsung Electronics about 10%. It has fallen about 16% since closing at 6,625.93 on Oct. 8. Tokyo’s Nikkei 225 closed down 4.90% at 64,425.95 and Taiwan’s Taiex fell 3.41%. Hong Kong (−1.03%) and Shanghai (−1.59%) fell less. Hang Seng Tech fell 2.7%.

Europe’s chip stocks followed. Infineon, Soitec and Aixtron were down 6–8% in early trade and ASML about 5%, Reuters reports, while the broad Stoxx 600 was only 0.2% lower. In the US, Nasdaq 100 futures were down 1.13% at about 6:23 and S&P 500 futures 0.32%, but Dow and small-cap futures were higher: money is leaving technology, not stocks as a whole. On Wednesday the VanEck semiconductor ETF (SMH) fell 4.0% for a sixth straight loss, AMD 5.7%, Micron 4.1%, ASML 3.9% and Nvidia 3.2%. Health care (+2.5%) and staples (+2.2%) rose.

Three explanations are on offer, and nobody has shown which one matters most. One is rates: hotter-than-expected core inflation on Wednesday revived the case for another Fed hike, and expensive, long-duration growth stocks are the most sensitive to that. The second is financing: the IMF’s financial-stability report this week named an AI valuation or debt correction as a leading risk, and CNBC cites growing worry about how the build-out is being paid for. The third, the trigger in Korea on Monday, is competition: Chinese memory maker CXMT said it had made progress on a new DRAM cell design and plans a server DDR5 product by year-end. Mizuho and the Korea Times both caution that an announcement is not a demonstrated yield or cost advantage. CNBC calculates that the 20 most valuable AI and chip stocks worldwide have lost more than $1 trillion in market value this week.

Why it matters AI hardware has been the market’s leadership for two years. SMH is now 6.3% below its 20-day average but still 10.8% above its 200-day, so this is a sharp correction inside an uptrend, not yet a broken trend. The cash market has not followed Asia’s scale: the S&P 500 is 1.6% below its 52-week high and the Nasdaq 100 futures about 5.6% from their high. Exposure, not direction: SMH, Nvidia, AMD, Micron, Broadcom, ASML, TSMC; the power names tied to data centres (Vistra −6.5% and Constellation −5.5% on Wednesday); Korean and Taiwanese ETFs (EWY, EWT).
What’s next The 8:30 data, then the US open. Whether the cash market follows Seoul and Tokyo, or treats Asia’s fall as catching up, is the morning’s question. Netflix and other large-cap results arrive next week. Warsh speaks Friday.

TSMC posts a sixth straight record quarter and guides higher, but its shares slip on margins

The most important supplier in AI hardware says demand is still rising. The market focused on a gross margin that fell 1.3 points as the newest factories ramp.

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing reported third-quarter net income of NT$763.1 billion (about $23.6 billion), up 68.7% from a year earlier, CNBC reports. Analysts polled by LSEG had expected about NT$746 billion. Revenue was about $46.7 billion, up 41% and above the company’s own guidance of $44.6–45.8 billion. Gross margin was 66.4%, down from 67.7% in the second quarter; TSMC put that down to the ramp of its 2-nanometre process and its overseas factories. It guided fourth-quarter revenue to $52.3–54.3 billion with a 65–67% gross margin, kept 2026 capital spending at $60–64 billion, and said 2026–28 spending would be “significantly higher” than the previous three years.

The Taipei-listed shares were part of the Taiex’s 3.4% fall, and the US-listed ADR was about 2% lower before the bell, Benzinga reports, after closing Wednesday down 3.4% at $430.00. The results are another strong read on demand, like ASML’s on Wednesday, and they did not stop the sector’s fall.

Why it matters This week’s debate is whether AI hardware demand is cracking. TSMC’s numbers and guidance say it is not; the slipping margin says the next generation costs more to build. The guidance supports the equipment makers it buys from (ASML, Applied Materials, Lam, KLA). It says nothing about memory pricing, which is what spooked Seoul.

Producer prices, retail sales and jobless claims at 8:30, with an October hike now close to a coin toss

Wednesday’s 0.3% core CPI moved the Fed debate. Today’s numbers show whether the oil shock is reaching business costs and whether consumers are still spending through it.

September consumer prices rose 0.6% on the month and 3.7% on the year, the highest annual rate in this run, with core prices up 0.3% and 2.6% (BLS, released Wednesday). Gasoline rose 9.1% in the month and accounted for about half the increase. FRED’s index levels confirm the moves: headline CPI 336.298 against 334.131 in August, core 338.896 against 337.765.

Today at 8:30 the BLS publishes September producer prices, with forecasters expecting +0.5% headline and +0.3% core after +0.4% and +0.2% (Trading Economics, ActionForex). The Census Bureau’s September retail sales are expected up 0.3%, and 0.5% excluding autos, after an outsized 1.2% in August, which was partly higher gasoline prices. Jobless claims are seen at 195,000–200,000, after 197,000; claims have stayed below 200,000 for four straight weeks on FRED’s series even as payrolls have flattened (159.04 million in September, up only 152,000 since June). The New York and Philadelphia Fed factory surveys come out at the same time. Philadelphia is expected to cool to 26–28 from 37.8.

The Fed raised its target range to 3.75–4.00% in September and meets Oct. 27–28. Futures-implied odds of another hike vary widely depending on when each outlet checked, from about 32% to 52%, against about 22% before CPI. Reuters calls it “a coin toss.” New York Fed President John Williams said on Oct. 13 that one more increase this year is “appropriate,” with no rush. Richmond’s Tom Barkin speaks at 1:30 and Chicago’s Austan Goolsbee at 3:00. Chair Kevin Warsh speaks Friday at the IMF and World Bank meetings in Bangkok, his last scheduled appearance before the blackout, Bloomberg reports.

Why it matters A 0.3%-plus core PPI would add to the evidence that the energy shock is spreading into wider business costs, which is the hike case. A soft retail number would cut the other way. Exposure, not direction: 2-year Treasuries, the dollar, rate-sensitive groups (regional banks KRE, homebuilders, utilities), and long-duration growth stocks.

Bonds and the dollar give back their CPI moves: the 10-year is reported near 5.10%

After the official curve flattened on Wednesday, Treasuries rallied overnight. One explanation in circulation, about Chinese holdings, is unconfirmed.

Wednesday’s official Treasury curve flattened: the 3-month bill rose 2 basis points to 4.28% and the 2-year 3 to 4.82%, while the 10-year fell 2 to 5.19% and the 30-year 4 to 5.52% (Treasury). That is the pattern of a market pricing a nearer hike that it expects to slow growth. The 2-to-10-year spread narrowed to 37 basis points from 42. Overnight, FXStreet and Trading Economics put the 10-year near 5.10%, about a three-week low, and the dollar index fell 0.4% to 102.10 (Yahoo, 6:23), erasing Wednesday’s rise to 102.95, its highest since April 2025.

FXStreet and investingLive tie part of the move to a Bloomberg report that “talk grows” that China is holding Treasuries through Canadian custodians. That is market chatter relayed through one report, with no official confirmation, and it does not obviously explain a rally. Lower oil (Brent −1.8% to $104.12) and the flight out of equities in Asia are more ordinary contributors. Gold rose 1.6% to $4,175, ending a three-day losing streak.

Single-source The 5.10% overnight level comes from two market-data outlets; the official curve publishes only end-of-day yields, and Yahoo’s 10-year quote was not updating before the cash session.

Why it matters Most of the 10-year’s level is real yield: the 10-year TIPS yield was 2.88% and the 10-year breakeven 2.35% (FRED). A rally led by real yields while the Fed debates a hike usually means markets expect growth, not inflation, to give way first. If equities’ problem is rates, a 9-basis-point fall should help; it has not helped Nasdaq futures so far.

BNY beats as the regional banks report: PNC, U.S. Bancorp and Schwab are due before the open

Every one of the six biggest US banks fell on its results day. The question this morning is whether the regionals, more exposed to loan demand and deposit costs, fare any better.

Bank of New York Mellon reported third-quarter earnings of $2.34 a share, up 24% from a year earlier, against analyst forecasts of about $2.24–2.26 (company 8-K filed at 6:30; Zacks, TipRanks). Revenue rose 12% to $5.68 billion, with fee revenue up 12% and net interest income up 15% on the year but down 2% from the second quarter. The custodian now holds $63.4 trillion in assets under custody or administration. Chief executive Robin Vince called the environment “constructive but uneven.”

Pittsburgh-based PNC, U.S. Bancorp and Charles Schwab report before the bell; none had filed when this edition went to press. Consensus is about $4.89–4.97 a share for PNC (against $4.35 a year ago) on revenue of about $6.6 billion, about $1.32 for U.S. Bancorp and $1.66–1.67 for Schwab. PNC told investors in July to expect net interest income up 3–3.5% from the second quarter and fee income down 5–5.5%. Progressive reported Wednesday afternoon: third-quarter profit of $4.37 a share, a combined ratio of 88.7 and premiums up 4%; its shares fell 4.2%.

Why it matters Wednesday’s inflation report raised the odds of a nearer hike while flattening the curve, which is mixed for bank margins: higher short rates lift what banks earn on cash, a flatter curve squeezes lending spreads. Exposure, not direction: KRE (−0.75% Wednesday), XLF, PNC, USB, SCHW, BK.

A second container ship is confirmed sunk near Hormuz, and an Iranian officer says the strait is “closed”

Oil fell anyway: Brent is $104, down 1.8%, on a report that Washington is considering easing sanctions on some tankers.

Singapore’s maritime authority confirmed that the Singapore-flagged container ship GFS Pride sank on Wednesday after being hit on Tuesday by what it called an unidentified projectile. All 21 crew were evacuated. A sister ship, GFS Giselle, was damaged and reached anchorage under its own power (Reuters via US News, The Loadstar, The National). Iran’s Revolutionary Guard navy says GFS Pride hit a mine after taking an “unauthorized route”; a US official told the AP it was more likely a missile. The cause is not established. Reuters, using IMO data, counts it as the third ship sunk since the war began on Feb. 28. Britain’s UKMTO has recorded 20 attacks on shipping since Oct. 1, the AP reports, and an Iranian naval officer said on Thursday that the strait is closed.

The Houthis, meanwhile, fired a ballistic missile at Riyadh on Wednesday that was intercepted, and their spokesman promised more attacks on Saudi airports and oil sites, Euronews and CBS report. Asked whether the US would join Saudi strikes, President Trump said, “We may. We’re going to look at it.” The White House has denied an Axios report that he and Prime Minister Netanyahu discussed a joint strike on Iran before Israel’s Oct. 27 election.

Brent was $104.12 at about 6:23, down 1.8% from Wednesday’s settlement of $106.06, and WTI $93.65 (−1.5%). A Financefeeds report, citing an unnamed US official, says the administration is weighing easing sanctions on some shadow-fleet tankers; we have not seen it confirmed elsewhere.

Why it matters Shipping through the strait is close to zero on the trackers, and still none of the attacks has hit a Gulf export terminal. Oil around $104 is pricing a long disruption, not a total loss. OPEC this week cut its 2026 demand-growth forecast for a sixth time, to about 0.2 million barrels a day. Exposure, not direction: Brent, XLE, airlines (United −4.1%, Delta −3.7% on Wednesday), tanker and insurance names.

Ukraine says it is resuming strikes on Russian refineries, and a Rosneft plant in Ryazan is burning

The energy truce President Trump says is holding looks close to collapse after Russia’s attack on Ukraine’s grid left 600,000 people without power.

President Zelensky told the Financial Times that Ukraine will resume strikes on Russian oil refineries: “The block is out. We’re done” (Bloomberg, Newsweek). Overnight, Ryazan’s governor reported a drone-caused fire at the Rosneft refinery there (Moscow Times). Kyiv has not commented. The decision follows Wednesday’s Russian barrage of more than 120 drones and 39 ballistic missiles on Ukraine’s power system, aimed mainly at Kyiv’s CHPP-4 plant. Zelensky said at least 600,000 people lost power. Death tolls differ: Kyiv’s prosecutor reports four dead in the city, and national counts run from six to nine.

Zelensky says US officials warned that intelligence sharing could be cut if Ukraine kept striking energy targets. He wants more Patriot systems as the price of a halt. On Wednesday, SpaceX capped Starlink terminals moving faster than about 160 km/h, disrupting Ukrainian drone operations, according to Ukrainian officials and the Washington Post. Ukrainian officials link the cap to US pressure; that link is their claim and is not established. Trump says the energy truce holds; the Kremlin says its details are still being worked out.

Why it matters Russian refinery outages tighten diesel, the product the IEA calls the tightest in the market, at the same time as the Gulf disruption. It also tests how far Washington will lean on Kyiv before the US vote.

Rocket Lab pushes Neutron’s first launch into 2027; space stocks slide

The company disclosed the delay in a filing after Wednesday’s close, at the end of a day when its shares had already fallen 7.9%.

Rocket Lab said in an 8-K filed at 4:41 p.m. Wednesday that the inaugural launch of Neutron, its reusable medium-lift rocket, is now expected in 2027 (SEC EDGAR). Its last public target was this year. The release gives no reason in its text and points to an investor presentation. Rocket Lab shares closed Wednesday at $59.18, down 7.85%, and AST SpaceMobile fell 10.5% to $42.14, just above its 52-week low (Yahoo Finance). Benzinga lists Rocket Lab among this morning’s pre-market movers.

Why it matters Neutron is how Rocket Lab plans to compete with SpaceX for constellation launches and national-security missions; a slip delays that revenue. Exposure, not direction: RKLB, the small-cap space names, and SpaceX (SPCX, $152.16, down 2.4% from its 20-day average).

The Justice Department’s 48-hour deadline on California’s voter rolls runs out today

DOJ wants mail ballots from people whose citizenship cannot be confirmed set aside, not cancelled. California officials call the deadline unreasonable.

In an Oct. 13 letter, Acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter gave California election officials 48 hours to check the citizenship of people on a list DOJ supplied on a password-protected thumb drive, and to “segregate — not cancel” the mail ballots of those they cannot confirm. Otherwise, the letter says, DOJ will “seek emergency relief” (CBS, CNN, PBS). The president of the state’s clerks’ association, Santa Cruz County Clerk Tricia Webber, called the deadline “completely unreasonable”; Los Angeles County is reviewing the letter with the Secretary of State and Attorney General. A voting-rights group has threatened to sue. DOJ has sued 30 states and DC for voter data and has so far lost those cases, the AP reports. No DOJ court filing had been reported by this morning. The election is Nov. 3.

Before the Open

What futures price. At about 6:23, S&P 500 futures were near 7,746, down 0.32% from Wednesday’s settlement; Nasdaq 100 futures near 29,841 (−1.13%); Dow futures near 51,986 (+0.25%); Russell 2000 futures near 2,754 (+0.31%). That is a rotation, not a rout: futures price more technology selling and some buying of everything else. Futures are not a guaranteed open, and three 8:30 releases arrive before the bell.

The curve. At Wednesday’s official close: 3-month 4.28%, 2-year 4.82%, 5-year 5.00%, 10-year 5.19%, 30-year 5.52%. The curve flattened: 2s10s narrowed to 37 basis points, 3m10y to 91 and 5s30s to 52. The 10-year is reported near 5.10% overnight, which would be its lowest in about three weeks.

Dollar, volatility, credit. The dollar index is 102.10 after touching 102.95 on Wednesday. The VIX closed Wednesday at 16.87, up from 15.27, with nine-day volatility jumping to 14.59 and three-month at 18.79; it traded at 17.51 overnight. The curve is still in contango, so the market is paying up for near-term protection without signalling panic. High-yield spreads were 3.16% on Oct. 13, up from 3.03% a week earlier, the widest since April (FRED, CNBC).

Commodities and crypto. Oil down, gold up, copper down: a mild risk-off move with lower yields, not a war spike. Bitcoin is near $81,000, down about 3% from this time Wednesday.

Positioning. FINRA’s daily short-volume files for Wednesday show 68% of SPY volume and 71% of QQQ volume marked short, against 50% across the market. Those numbers include market-maker hedging and are not short interest, which is settlement-dated and two to three weeks stale. Taken together with nine-day volatility jumping, they point to hedging demand, not a capitulation.

The most fragile assumption. That this is a technology problem, not a market problem. The tape is pricing a rotation: Dow and small-cap futures up, defensives bid, credit spreads still near 3%. What would break it: high-yield spreads moving decisively above 3.25%, the equal-weight S&P falling with the cap-weighted index instead of holding up (RSP was −0.6% Wednesday against SPY −1.1%), or a hot core PPI that pushes the 2-year up and takes the defensive bid away.

The Economy

Inflation. September CPI showed the oil shock fully in the headline (+0.6% m/m, 3.7% y/y) and now visibly in the core (+0.3%, 2.6%). Both are rates of change; the annual core rate rose from 2.4%. Market inflation expectations barely moved: the 10-year breakeven was 2.35% on Wednesday, against 2.33% on Tuesday (FRED). Today’s PPI covers the pipeline. China’s September producer prices, released Wednesday, rose 5.0% on the year, the fastest in more than four years, on fuel and metals (CNBC). That feeds through to import prices, which are out Friday.

Labor. Unemployment was 4.2% in September, up from 4.1%, and payrolls rose just 29,000 (FRED, preliminary and subject to revision). Initial claims were 197,000 in the week to Oct. 3. Low layoffs, low hiring: a labour market that has stalled without cracking.

The Fed. Target range 3.75–4.00% since September. The September projections showed one more hike this year. Williams (New York) says one more is appropriate, without urgency. Governor Bowman sees no urgent need; St. Louis’s Musalem has said AI investment accounts for about half of current inflation (summaries via investingLive and Newsquawk; dates approximate). Reuters reports that Warsh’s push for a “quieter Fed” with less forward guidance is facing pushback inside the committee, with Governor Waller warning that giving no signal risks volatility. The effective funds rate was 3.89% (SOFR, Oct. 13).

Treasury. The 4- and 8-week bills auction today ($110 billion and $105 billion offered, TreasuryDirect). The 13- and 26-week bills, a 6-week bill, a 20-year bond reopening (Oct. 21) and a 5-year note (Oct. 22) are announced today. Last week’s 30-year auction cleared at 5.618%, the highest since August 2000. The Treasury’s cash account was $920 billion on Oct. 13 (Daily Treasury Statement). September’s Monthly Treasury Statement, which closes fiscal 2026, is not out yet; the Congressional Budget Office estimates the year’s deficit at about $2.0 trillion, $218 billion more than fiscal 2025. At the IMF meetings, Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva said Treasury Secretary Bessent’s growth-led plan was not enough to bring down the debt, which the IMF projects at 142% of GDP by 2031 (CNBC).

Global central banks. ECB Governing Council member Boštjan Dolenc said tighter financial conditions mean the ECB “maybe” does not have to do as much; most economists expect a hold this month and a December hike to 2.75% (Reuters via Mitrade). Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey said it is getting harder for Britain to avoid the inflation hit from energy, and markets price a November hike (single report, FXStreet). The Bank of Japan, at 1.25% after a September hike, is reported to be leaning against moving again this month. China’s central bank set the yuan fixing weaker than expected, at 6.7253.

The United States

Press access. U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly issued a preliminary injunction late Tuesday letting CNN, MS NOW and Politico keep their White House credentials while their suit continues. He found the badge revocations lacked “constitutionally adequate due process” and likely violated the First Amendment, citing a 1977 D.C. Circuit precedent he said bound him. The order does not guarantee Oval Office or Air Force One access. A White House official told Fox News the administration will appeal; the administration cites security, the outlets call the ban viewpoint discrimination (Axios, NPR, Fox News).

Supreme Court. In an unsigned order Wednesday in FCC v. Brown, the Court paused a Fourth Circuit order that had told the FCC to rule quickly on ad rates for coordinated campaign spending, saying the appeals court acted inappropriately in setting the schedule. The effect is that the current FCC rules stay in place for this election (Supreme Court; Washington Times). The Court also heard argument in Salazar v. Paramount Global, a video-privacy case, and agreed to hear cases on DOGE’s access to government data and on what counts as “material support” for terrorism (SCOTUSblog).

Rep. Ilhan Omar. The Justice Department has confirmed only that Monday’s search of her Washington-area home was a court-authorized warrant in an ongoing criminal investigation, carried out by Homeland Security Investigations. The New York Times reports the warrant lists possible immigration-related offences, including false statements; that is one outlet’s reporting. No charges have been filed. Omar calls the search the weaponisation of law enforcement; President Trump says it “has nothing to do with me” (NBC, PBS/AP, CNN).

Oil-trading inquiry. Bloomberg reported that an adviser to CFTC Chair Michael Selig had staff withdraw an insider-trading referral concerning large oil-futures bets placed minutes before a March 23 presidential post about Iran. A source familiar with the matter called the report “false and malicious” and said the inquiry continues. Sen. Elizabeth Warren wants a briefing. No trader has been identified, and unusual trading is not proof of wrongdoing (Bloomberg via Daily Caller and Financefeeds).

The campaign. Nineteen days out, Democrats lead the generic congressional ballot by about 8–9 points in the averages: D+8.9 in Silver Bulletin (Oct. 9), D+8.3 in RealClearPolling (Sept. 21–Oct. 11), D+8 in FiftyPlusOne (Oct. 14). Averages are not forecasts, and House seats turn on individual districts. Government funding runs through Dec. 11 under the September stopgap; Congress is out until after the election.

AI & Technology

Scientists call for locking down hacking-capable AI. About 400 researchers from 34 countries, including Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, published an open letter on Tuesday calling for “high-assurance containment” of AI models capable of serious cyberattacks, including air-gapping, and an international body with an incident registry (Canadian Press via CP24, Global News).

OpenAI’s price tag. OpenAI is reported to be in early talks to raise at least $30 billion at about a $1.4 trillion valuation before new money; nothing is priced. Its last confirmed round, in March, valued it at $852 billion after the investment (secondary reports). The OpenAI engineer charged with planting a recording device in an executive conference room appeared in court Tuesday and remains in custody; he is charged, not convicted (SF Standard, AP).

Custom chips. Microsoft’s Maia 300 inference chip, announced Monday for 2027, adds to the list of cloud companies designing their own accelerators. Microsoft claims 40% more output per megawatt than unnamed leading GPU systems; there are no independent benchmarks (Redmond Magazine, Data Center Knowledge). Read-through: positive for TSMC, a marginal negative for Nvidia and AMD’s share at Microsoft.

Apple in China. Apple plans to launch Apple Intelligence in China in December, Bloomberg reports, citing people familiar with the plans. Apple was one of the few large technology names up on Wednesday (+0.5%).

Science

How Earth kept its oxygen. A peer-reviewed study in Nature Communications, co-authored at UC Riverside, uses South African rocks to argue that phosphorus recycling in the oceans sustained oxygen levels after the Great Oxidation Event about 2.3 billion years ago, rather than letting them collapse (UC Riverside via ScienceDaily).

Steering chemistry with pH. Seoul National University researchers report raising an electrochemical reaction’s selectivity from 16% to 97% by controlling the acidity right next to the electrode, an approach relevant to making fuels and chemicals with electricity (institution release; journal not stated in the summaries we saw).

Cargo to the station. SpaceX’s CRS-35 Dragon, launched Tuesday, is due to dock with the International Space Station this morning, carrying the last pair of roll-out solar arrays and biology experiments (Space.com). A Falcon 9 is scheduled to launch the USSF-481 national-security mission at about 7:44 p.m. ET tonight (Launch Library). Rocket Lab’s Neutron delay is in Top Stories.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

County Council’s final vote on 12 weeks of paid parental leave is expected Tuesday

A council committee voted 5–1 to recommend the bill, which would require employers with 15 or more workers in the county to give 12 weeks of paid leave after a birth, adoption or stillbirth, for employees with about six months on the job (WESA). Under the county’s rules for this kind of bill, council must vote it as written, without amendments, and it needs eight votes; if County Executive Sara Innamorato signs, it takes effect a year later (Pennsylvania Municipal League). The Allegheny Conference on Community Development opposes it. The Oct. 20 date comes from an advocacy group’s schedule and the council agenda had not been published when we checked.

Pennsylvania

Day two of the skill-game ban: police can seize machines, and unplugging one is not enough

State Police began enforcement Wednesday after the 120-day grace period ended. Businesses still holding the machines face seizure, criminal charges and possible forfeiture of cash, and police say switching a machine off does not count as compliance (Inquirer, NBC10). Estimates put 50,000 to 70,000 or more machines in bars, clubs and convenience stores. Six operators and distributors have signed compliance agreements, and many VFW and Legion posts removed machines early. No first-day seizure counts had been reported. Lawmakers return Monday with the tax fight unresolved: Gov. Shapiro wants a 52% rate, Senate Republicans about 35% (City & State PA).

Pennsylvania

State Supreme Court rejects a “co-user” defence in fatal-overdose prosecutions

The court upheld drug-delivery-resulting-in-death convictions and rejected the argument that someone who bought and used drugs with the person who died cannot be charged as a dealer, Spotlight PA reports. The ruling applies statewide and shapes how county prosecutors, including Allegheny County’s, charge overdose deaths.
