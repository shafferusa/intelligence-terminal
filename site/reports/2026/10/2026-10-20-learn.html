The CO2 Over Mauna Loa Falls Every Summer. Charles Keeling’s Curve Is Still Getting Steeper.

Tuesday, October 20, 2026 · 13 min read

The derivative is the instantaneous rate a quantity is changing right now, and the second derivative is whether that rate is itself speeding up — the one distinction that lets a curve that falls every single summer still be the clearest record we have of an accelerating planet.

Mathematics

Day 4 of 293 · Mathematics 4 of 24 · The derivative: rates of change

A young chemist’s instrument on a Hawaiian volcano

In March 1958, Charles David Keeling, a 29-year-old geochemist at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, switched on a new infrared gas analyzer at a freshly built observatory nearly 11,000 feet up the slope of Mauna Loa — a still-active Hawaiian volcano, chosen because it sat far from the cities and forests whose exhaust or photosynthesis could skew a reading. On its first day of operation the instrument recorded 313 parts per million of carbon dioxide, the figure Scripps’ own history of the program still cites today, though some later retellings round it to 315, and others confuse it with 316 ppm — which was actually the following year’s annual average, not Keeling’s opening reading.

Within a couple of years the record showed something odd. Every year CO2 climbed through the northern winter, peaked around May, then fell for months as the Northern Hemisphere’s plants leafed out and inhaled it — a swing of several parts per million, repeating like clockwork. Funding was never secure; a 1964 budget cut interrupted the measurements for several months. But underneath that yearly sawtooth, the line kept climbing, and not at a constant pace: by Scripps’ and NOAA’s own published figures, the underlying trend rose by roughly 0.9 ppm a year through the 1960s and by roughly 2.4 ppm a year through the 2010s, a rate that has more than doubled (these are Mauna Loa, not global, figures, and the exact number shifts a little depending on which years are averaged). In May 2024, Scripps reported that March’s reading had jumped 4.7 ppm over the previous March — the largest twelve-month gain ever recorded at the station, well above the previous record of 4.1 ppm set in 2015–16.

If the reading falls every single summer, in what sense is carbon dioxide “rising”? And if the line never stops wiggling, how can anyone say, with a straight face, that the rise itself is speeding up?

The Big Idea

Today’s concept is the derivative: the instantaneous rate at which a quantity is changing at one particular moment, found by looking at how much it changes over an interval and asking what that rate settles toward as the interval shrinks to nothing. Graphically, it is the slope of the tangent line touching a curve at a single point, not the slope of a line drawn between two points some distance apart.

A derivative answers only one question: is the quantity rising or falling right now, and how fast? To ask whether that speed is itself changing — whether a rise is accelerating or running out of steam — you need a second derivative: the derivative of the derivative, the rate of change of the rate of change. A car’s speed is a derivative of its position; whether that speed is climbing or easing off is a second derivative, the quantity ordinarily called acceleration. The units always carry the whole idea even before the arithmetic does: a derivative’s units are always the original quantity’s units divided by time’s — parts per million per year, meters per second, dollars per quarter — and a second derivative’s units divide by time again, which is exactly why acceleration is measured in meters per second per second.

How It Works

Start with something easier to measure than an instantaneous rate: an average rate over an interval. If a quantity f changes from f(t) to f(t+h) over a stretch of time h, its average rate of change across that stretch is [f(t+h) − f(t)] / h — the familiar “rise over run,” the slope of the straight line (a secant line) connecting the two points on f’s graph. That number depends entirely on how wide h is: average a car’s speed over a whole hour and a dramatic braking-and-accelerating episode in the middle gets smeared into a bland, uninformative number.

The derivative asks what that average rate settles toward as h is made smaller and smaller, approaching — but never quite reaching — zero. Mathematicians call that settling-down value a limit, and write the derivative as f′(t) = limh→0 [f(t+h) − f(t)] / h. Graphically, as h shrinks, the secant line connecting two nearby points on the curve swings around and approaches the tangent line, the single straight line that just grazes the curve at t without crossing it nearby. The derivative is that tangent line’s slope: the rate of change at the instant t itself, not averaged over any surrounding stretch. Nothing about this requires an exact algebraic formula for f: Keeling had no equation for atmospheric CO2, only a sequence of measured readings, and the same shrinking-interval logic applies whether the underlying quantity is a smooth mathematical curve or a monthly average bolted together from real instrument readings.

Nothing in that definition says the rate has to stay positive, or stay the same from one moment to the next. A quantity can rise, level off, then fall, and its derivative tracks exactly that: positive, then zero, then negative. This is precisely the distinction the Keeling curve forces on a careful reader. At almost any point in the Northern Hemisphere’s late spring and summer, carbon dioxide’s instantaneous derivative — the slope of the curve right then — is negative: a hemisphere’s worth of plants is photosynthesizing, pulling carbon out of the air faster than it is being added. That is a true, measured fact about those months, and on its own it says nothing about whether CO2 is “rising” in any longer-run sense.

To see the longer-run trend you have to change what interval you average over. Instead of comparing this month with last month — which mixes the real trend together with wherever in the seasonal cycle you happen to be standing — compare each month with the same month a year earlier: [C(t + 1 yr) − C(t)] / 1 yr. A full year spans exactly one complete seasonal cycle, so that cycle cancels out of the subtraction, leaving a cleaner estimate of how much the underlying level has actually moved. This is not a different kind of mathematics from the plain derivative above — it is the same average-rate-of-change idea, deliberately computed over a wider, carefully chosen interval instead of letting h shrink to an instant, precisely because the instant is dominated by a cycle you want to see past.

That year-over-year rate is itself a quantity you can track through time, and asking whether it is rising, flat, or falling is asking for a second derivative of the original CO2 record. Scripps’ and NOAA’s own growth-rate figures show that rate climbing: roughly 0.9 ppm a year across the 1960s to roughly 2.4 ppm a year across the 2010s. A rate that is itself increasing means the underlying curve is not just rising, it is bending upward — the second derivative is positive. Read any single year’s jump with care, though: the 4.7 ppm gain recorded between March 2023 and March 2024 was amplified by that year’s El Niño, which temporarily weakens how much carbon land ecosystems absorb, so judging acceleration honestly means looking at multi-year averages rather than any one striking year.

Back to the story

The derivative separates exactly the two questions the puzzle poses. Each summer’s instantaneous rate really is negative — plants are winning, for a few months, against emissions. But the year-over-year rate, Keeling’s own trend measure, has been positive in every year since 1958, straight through every seasonal dip. And that trend rate has itself climbed, from under one part per million a year in the program’s first decade to well over two today — a positive second derivative, carried by a record now in its seventh decade. “Rising” and “accelerating” are two different, separately measurable claims, and one wiggling line can make both of them true at once.

Key Concepts

Average rate of change
How much a quantity changes divided by how long that took, measured between two specific points — the slope of the straight line (secant line) connecting them on a graph.
Derivative
The instantaneous rate of change: what the average rate of change settles toward as the interval between the two points shrinks to zero. Graphically, the slope of the tangent line touching the curve at a single point.
Limit
The value an expression approaches as one of its inputs gets arbitrarily close to some point, without necessarily ever reaching it — the operation that turns an average rate over a shrinking interval into an instantaneous one.
Second derivative
The derivative of the derivative: the rate of change of the rate of change. Positive means the original rate is itself increasing, so the quantity’s rise is accelerating; negative means the rate is easing off.
Trend versus seasonal cycle
A trend is the longer-run direction a quantity is heading; a seasonal cycle is a repeating swing layered on top of it. Comparing a reading only with the same point a year earlier cancels a full seasonal cycle and isolates the trend’s own rate.

Example: An Airbag That Reads a Derivative in Milliseconds

An airbag’s trigger sits at the car chain’s engineering link, translating a physical threat — a sudden crash — into a controlled, split-second action. What it actually measures is a derivative: not how fast the car is going, but how fast that speed is changing, moment to moment. Analog Devices announced the ADXL50 in 1991, among the first mass-produced micromachined accelerometers: a single silicon chip, built with semiconductor manufacturing techniques, carrying a free-floating microscopic beam whose deflection under acceleration an onboard circuit converts directly into a voltage. It replaced older mechanical “ball-in-tube” crash sensors and became a workhorse of automotive airbag deployment through the 1990s.

1. Convert the impact speed. 30 mph ≈ 13.4 m/s.

2. Find the decision window. Engineering accounts of airbag timing put the bag’s own inflation at roughly 30 milliseconds once triggered, so for it to be in place before an unrestrained occupant would otherwise reach the wheel or dashboard, the controller has to decide to fire within roughly the crash’s first 20 milliseconds, not the whole fraction-of-a-second collision — typical figures for this kind of system, not a fixed standard.

3. See what the chip is actually computing. In that 20-millisecond window it is not reporting one number: it is sampling acceleration — essentially dv/dt — many times, repeatedly asking whether velocity is falling fast and staying that way.

4. Compare two kinds of signal. A pothole or a hard expansion joint can produce one sharp spike of deceleration lasting barely a millisecond before the reading snaps back toward zero; a real crash pulse keeps dv/dt large and negative for the rest of the window.

5. See the connection to today’s lesson. The controller is, in effect, testing the derivative’s behavior across the whole decision window rather than reacting to any single instant — the same average-versus-instantaneous distinction How It Works just walked through, applied by a chip instead of a mathematician.

This is why a single-axis accelerometer alone is not a safety system: a chip that fired on any one large reading would deploy on every speed bump and curb strike a car ever took. What makes it useful is exactly the discipline this lesson has been building — distinguishing a rate measured at one instant from a rate that holds up over a meaningful stretch of time, then deciding, in milliseconds, which kind of signal it is actually looking at.

Why It Matters

Once you can tell an instantaneous rate from a second derivative, a chart that is falling this month and setting a record this decade stops looking like a contradiction. The same shape shows up anywhere a real trend rides on top of a repeating cycle: tax receipts dip every April after refunds and still trend upward for a decade; a retailer’s revenue falls every January after the holidays and still grows year over year; a region’s electricity demand sags every spring and autumn between heating and cooling seasons while still climbing on a multi-year basis as the economy grows. In every one of these the honest answer to “is it rising?” depends entirely on which interval you choose to average over, and a chart read at the wrong interval can make a steady decline look alarming, or a genuine emergency look calm.

It also points you toward where danger actually hides in a system, which is rarely the current level. A quantity that is high but steady is a known, manageable problem: you can plan around a constant. A quantity whose own rate of change is climbing — a positive second derivative — is a system whose demands on you are growing faster than you are used to planning for, so whatever response matched last decade’s pace will already be behind by the time it is built. That is exactly the shape of warning the Keeling curve’s trend has carried for six decades: not a fixed problem to solve once, but a moving target whose own speed keeps increasing.

Settled. Direct measurement shows carbon dioxide rising at Mauna Loa and globally every year since 1958, and that annual rise itself growing larger; isotopic and carbon-budget evidence places the dominant cause in fossil-fuel combustion (IPCC AR6).

Debated. Exactly how much of any single year’s jump — 2023–24’s included — to attribute to a specific El Niño episode versus the underlying trend is still refined by ongoing research, which is why acceleration is judged on multi-year averages rather than any one year’s reading.

Value judgment. How much to spend now, guarding against a rate of change not yet visible in this year’s reading rather than against a level everyone can already see, is a judgment about who bears the cost of waiting — the question Apply It puts to a coastal planner below.

Connections

Back. Day 2 defined growth as a constant percentage change per period — itself a rate of change relative to the current amount, already a derivative in disguise, just not named as one. Today names that idea properly and takes the one step Day 2’s single log scale could not: asking whether the rate itself is changing.

Across. Engineering builds control out of the same quantity. In 1922 the Russian-born American engineer Nicolas Minorsky published “Directional Stability of Automatically Steered Bodies,” drawn from his work on automatic steering for U.S. Navy ships, giving the first rigorous mathematical treatment of what is now called a PID (proportional-integral-derivative) controller. A purely proportional controller reacts only to how large an error currently is, which tends to overshoot and oscillate around the target; Minorsky’s derivative term instead reacts to how fast that error — a ship’s heading, a thermostat’s temperature, a drone’s altitude — is changing right now, damping the overshoot by anticipating where the error is headed rather than reacting only to where it currently sits. The same three-term logic now runs inside almost every industrial control loop, from cruise control to a chemical plant’s temperature regulators.

Remember These
  • The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change: what an average rate over an interval settles toward as that interval shrinks to zero — the tangent line’s slope.
  • The second derivative is the rate of change of the rate: positive means a rise is accelerating, negative means it is easing off.
  • A quantity can be falling right now and still be rising on trend — pick the right interval before calling either one true.
  • The Keeling curve’s year-over-year rate has itself climbed since 1958: not just rising, but rising faster.
  • Engineers read the same derivative directly — an airbag’s accelerometer and a PID controller’s derivative term both act on how fast something is changing, not just how much.
