In 1920, Two Scientists Fit a Near-Perfect Curve to America’s Growth and Capped It at 197 Million. The Country Blew Past It by 1967.

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 13 min read

Growth that runs up against a ceiling traces a predictable S-shaped curve — but the curve’s shape and the location of its ceiling are two different questions, and the data cannot answer the second one until growth has already passed its fastest point.

Mathematics

Day 7 of 293 · Mathematics 7 of 24 · Logistic growth and S-curves

A near-perfect fit to 120 years of census data

In June 1920, two scientists at Johns Hopkins — the biologist Raymond Pearl and the statistician Lowell Reed — published a short paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. They had taken every US census count from 1790 to 1910 and fit it to a curve the Belgian mathematician Pierre-François Verhulst had proposed for populations back in 1838, then largely forgotten for decades: the logistic. The fit was remarkable — smooth, close, and consistent across 120 years of a young, fast-growing country. Pearl and Reed’s curve said American growth had already passed its fastest point, around 1914, and from there would bend over and level off at a ceiling of about 197,273,000 people.

Pearl did not stop at the census. Convinced he had found something closer to a law of nature than a convenient fit, he went on to grow fruit flies in bottles and yeast in flasks, and found the same S-shaped curve there too. If one equation could describe flies, yeast and the population of the United States, Pearl argued, it deserved to be taken seriously as a general law of growth.

On November 20, 1967, a population clock in the Commerce Department’s lobby in Washington ticked past 200 million — already more than two million above Pearl and Reed’s supposed ceiling, with the country still growing. By the 2020 Census the count stood at roughly 331 million, nearly 134 million above the number two careful scientists had fit to twelve decades of real data. If the curve matched the past so well, how could its own ceiling be off by more than a hundred million people?

The Big Idea

When a growing quantity is limited by some ceiling, it tends to trace a specific S-shaped curve — slow at first, then sharply faster, then slower again as it closes in on the limit — and that shape is a real, recurring pattern in bounded growth, not a coincidence. But the curve’s shape and the height of its ceiling are two separate facts, and fitting the shape well does not automatically tell you the height.

Specifically: while a population, a market or a disease outbreak is still on the accelerating part of the curve, the data in hand can be fit almost equally well by curves heading toward very different ceilings. Only once growth has visibly passed its fastest point and begun to bend over does the data start to pin the ceiling down with any confidence. Pearl and Reed had excellent data and did the arithmetic honestly — their mistake was trusting a ceiling their own data had not yet earned the right to reveal.

How It Works

Start from Day 2’s exponential growth: a quantity that grows by a constant percentage of itself per period, compounding without limit. Real growth is rarely that generous forever. Logistic growth keeps the same instinct — the rate of change still depends on how much currently exists — but adds a second factor: how much room is left beneath a ceiling. Day 6 wrote a differential equation as a rule connecting a quantity to its own rate of change; the logistic equation is exactly that kind of rule, with a braking term added: dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K). Here N is the size of the population (or market, or outbreak) at time t, r is the unconstrained growth rate it would have with no ceiling at all, and K is the ceiling itself, the carrying capacity.

Read the braking term on its own: (1 − N/K). When N is small compared with K, that term sits close to 1, so dN/dt ≈ rN — this is Day 2’s unconstrained exponential growth again, because when the ceiling is far away it barely matters. As N climbs toward K, N/K climbs toward 1, so (1 − N/K) shrinks toward zero, and the growth rate shrinks with it — not because anything is being actively suppressed, but because there is simply less room left to grow into. That single multiplicative term is the whole mechanism: the same exponential impulse, discounted by how much of the ceiling has already been used up.

Solved, the equation gives the logistic function itself: N(t) = K / (1 + e−r(t − t0)), an S-shaped curve that starts near zero, rises, and flattens toward K as t grows large. The constant t0 marks the curve’s midpoint in time — and that midpoint is not arbitrary. Treat the growth rate itself, R(N) = rN(1 − N/K) = rN − rN²/K, as a function of N rather than of time, and ask where it is largest. Its derivative with respect to N is dR/dN = r − 2rN/K; setting that to zero and solving gives N = K/2. The growth rate is highest exactly when the quantity has reached half its ultimate ceiling — the inflection point. Before that point the curve is accelerating (concave up, looking almost exponential); after it, the curve is decelerating (concave down, bending toward the ceiling) even though N is still rising.

That inflection point is also where the trouble starts for anyone trying to fit the curve to real data in real time. Before a growth process has passed its inflection, the observed data looks almost indistinguishable from several different logistic curves with quite different values of K: a curve heading toward a ceiling of 200 million and a curve heading toward a ceiling of 400 million can produce nearly the same numbers for the first several decades, because both are still in their early, near-exponential phase, where the braking term (1 − N/K) has barely begun to bite. Statisticians call this a problem of identifiability: the data, as given, simply does not contain enough information to distinguish between those competing ceilings, no matter how well any one of them happens to fit. Only once growth has visibly bent over — once you can see the deceleration with your own eyes in the data — does the arithmetic start to narrow K down with real confidence.

Pearl and Reed’s own fitted curve placed the United States’ inflection point around 1914, just six years before they published. Their 1790–1910 data had barely begun to show the bend their model assumed was already underway, which meant the ceiling their fit produced was far closer to an extrapolation of an assumed shape than a conclusion the data had actually delivered. And the real world did not hold still to let the extrapolation stand: large-scale immigration before restrictive 1920s quota laws, the postwar Baby Boom, rising life expectancy, and twentieth-century gains in agricultural productivity all moved the country’s real capacity for population in ways no curve fit to 1790–1910 data could have anticipated. Pearl’s further move — generalizing from fruit flies and yeast to a universal “law of population growth” — compounded the error: a flask of yeast has a fixed, physically enforced ceiling (the sugar it can ferment before the alcohol it produces kills it), while a national population’s ceiling is not fixed by anything remotely so simple, and later demographers did not accept Pearl’s claim to have found a general law.

Back to the story

That is the answer to the puzzle. The S-shape itself was not wrong — bounded growth genuinely does tend to look logistic, and Pearl and Reed’s equation fit 120 years of real census counts about as well as a simple model ever fits history. What failed was the leap from a good fit on the accelerating half of the curve to a confident number for where the curve would eventually flatten. In 1920 the US was still too close to its own inflection point for the data to have earned that number, and the following decades changed the real constraints on American population growth in ways a backward-looking curve could not see coming. The curve described the shape of the past faithfully; it was never equipped to measure a ceiling the future itself had not yet fixed.

Key Concepts

Logistic growth
Growth whose rate depends on both how much of a quantity already exists and how much room remains beneath a ceiling; the exponential growth of Day 2, discounted as that room runs out.
Carrying capacity (K)
The ceiling a logistic quantity approaches but, in the idealized model, never exceeds — an assumption built into the model, not something early data can measure directly.
Inflection point
The point, at N = K/2, where the growth rate itself is largest; growth accelerates before it and decelerates after it, even while still rising.
S-curve
The sigmoid shape traced by logistic growth: slow, then fast, then slow again as the ceiling nears.
Identifiability
Whether a given set of data actually contains enough information to pin down a model’s parameters; logistic curves are poorly identified — many different ceilings fit almost equally well — before the inflection point.

Example: Reading Where a Technology Sits on Its S

Technology adoption inside a population traces the same shape, which is how to read the adoption link of Logan’s car chain for any technology, not just the automobile. Diego Comin and Bart Hobijn’s cross-country dataset (“An Exploration of Technology Diffusion,” American Economic Review, 2010) traces adoption curves for technologies from electricity to the internet across dozens of countries and finds adoption lags between countries that stretch across decades — some countries hit their own inflection point for a given technology generations after others. The practical trick for reading where any single technology sits on its S, without knowing the final ceiling in advance, is to track the annual point gain rather than the level itself: that gain rises while the technology is still on the accelerating half of its curve, peaks near the inflection, and shrinks as the ceiling approaches — the same arithmetic that made Pearl and Reed’s growth rate fastest at N = K/2.

1. Read the early, accelerating phase. Pew Research Center’s surveys put US adult smartphone ownership at 35% in 2011 and 56% in 2013 — a gain of about 21 points in two years, roughly 10–11 points a year: the steep early rise of an S-curve still well below any plausible ceiling.

2. Watch the annual gain, not just the level. By 2016 ownership reached 77% — past the halfway mark between 0% and a near-100% ceiling, with the pace of gain already smaller than the 2011–2013 stretch, consistent with having crossed the inflection.

3. Watch it slow further. By 2024–2025, ownership had reached 91% and sat flat there across both years — a gain of only about 14 points over the eight years since 2016, versus 42 points in the five years before it: the deceleration that marks the curve’s upper bend.

4. Guess the ceiling, cautiously. A plateau at 91% rather than 100% suggests the real ceiling for smartphone ownership sits below full saturation — some adults, by choice, cost or access, may never adopt one.

5. Apply the Story’s caution to the guess itself. That ceiling read from two flat years is still an inference, not a measurement with the error bars of a physical constant — exactly the gap that caught Pearl and Reed, just with a much shorter and much more recent run of data.

Equation / Model

dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K)  ⇒  N(t) = K / (1 + e−r(t − t0))

The rate of change of N with respect to t equals r times N times the quantity 1 minus N over K. This implies N of t equals K divided by the quantity 1 plus e to the negative r times the quantity t minus t-naught.

N
the size of the growing quantity (a population, an adopting market, an outbreak) at time t
r
the unconstrained growth rate — how fast N would grow with no ceiling at all
K
the carrying capacity: the ceiling N approaches but, in the idealized model, never exceeds
t0
the time at which N reaches the inflection point, K/2, where growth is fastest

The decisive step is finding where the growth rate itself peaks, treating it as a function of N:

R(N) = rN − rN²/K  ⇒  dR/dN = r − 2rN/K = 0  ⇒  N = K/2

The growth rate R of N equals r N minus r N squared over K. Its derivative with respect to N equals r minus 2 r N over K. Setting that to zero and solving gives N equals K over 2.

R(N)
the instantaneous growth rate, dN/dt, written as a function of the current size N rather than of time
N = K/2
the inflection point: the one value of N at which the growth rate is at its maximum

Why It Matters

Once you can recognize the logistic S-curve, you gain something sharper than “growth eventually slows down” — you gain a specific reason to distrust any forecast of where a still-accelerating process will level off, no matter how beautifully its curve fits the past. That caution applies far beyond population counts: it applies to a company projecting a market’s saturation point from its first few years of sales, a public-health forecast of how large an outbreak will ultimately get while cases are still climbing, or an energy analyst projecting how much of the grid a new technology will eventually capture. The shape being right is not the same claim as the ceiling being known, and the gap between those two claims is widest exactly when a forecast is most tempting to make.

Connections

Back. Day 6’s differential equation, dN/dt = −kN, is a decay rule with no braking term at all; today’s equation adds exactly one, (1 − N/K), which slows growth as the ceiling nears rather than letting it run forever. And the logistic’s early phase, while N is still small relative to K, is Day 2’s exponential growth outright — which is why an S-curve looks exponential right up until the point it visibly doesn’t, and why a logarithmic plot of the data shows the coming bend before a plain plot does.

Across. William Kermack and Anderson McKendrick’s 1927 epidemic model, a foundation of mathematical epidemiology, produces its own S-shaped cumulative-case curve — but its ceiling, the epidemic’s final size, is not fixed by the size of the population the way K is a free parameter in Pearl and Reed’s fit. It is an output of the mechanism itself: transmissibility and the fraction of people who become immune, through the model’s own final-size relation connecting the reproduction number to how many people are ultimately infected. An epidemic can burn out and stop while most of the population is still susceptible, which is the opposite lesson from Pearl and Reed’s: there, the ceiling was an unmeasured assumption fed into the curve; here, the ceiling falls out of the biology once you know the mechanism driving it.
