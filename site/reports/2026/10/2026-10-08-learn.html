How to Get Infinitely Close to a Number and Still Call It Exact

Thursday, October 8, 2026 · 13 min read

For roughly a century and a half, calculus produced correct answers by leaning on quantities nobody could define without contradiction; the limit, finished off by Karl Weierstrass in 1861, replaced them with a tolerance test anyone can check, and it is the foundation every calculus idea in this subject now stands on.

Mathematics

Day 9 of 300 · Mathematics 9 of 20 · The limit: approaching forever without arriving

The Big Idea

Some quantities refuse to hand themselves over by direct calculation. Ask for the slope of a curving line at one single instant, and you run into a wall immediately: slope, by its ordinary definition, needs two points to compare — rise over run — and a single instant is only one point. Ask what 1 divided by a number equals as that number grows without any bound, and you run into a different wall: the quotient keeps shrinking, gets as close to zero as you like, and yet, for any actual number you could name, never quite touches it.

Both walls dissolve once you stop asking “what is the value, exactly, right there,” and start asking a more patient question instead: what value does this quantity keep closing in on, as you push closer and closer to the point that is causing trouble — whether or not you ever actually arrive? That closing-in value is called a limit, and it is one of the two or three ideas the rest of this subject’s calculus block is built entirely out of.

The idea sounds almost too simple to need a whole lesson, and for about a hundred and fifty years mathematicians treated it that way — as an intuition, not a definition, and calculus ran into real trouble because of it. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz, working independently in the 1660s and 1670s, built the whole machinery of calculus on something that acted like a limit but was never pinned down: an “infinitesimal,” a quantity small enough to vanish from an equation when it was inconvenient, yet large enough to divide by when it wasn’t. In 1734 the philosopher George Berkeley attacked exactly that sleight of hand in a pamphlet called The Analyst, asking what these vanishing quantities actually were and answering, memorably, that they could only be “the ghosts of departed quantities” — something that had to be nonzero one line and zero the next. Calculus kept giving correct answers regardless, which is the only reason nobody abandoned it. But it took until the 19th century for mathematicians to build a definition rigorous enough to answer Berkeley on his own terms. That definition is today’s lesson.

How It Works

Start with a concrete case. Define a function f(x) = (x² − 4) / (x − 2), and ask for its value at x = 2. Plug in directly and you get 0/0 — undefined, a dead end. But instead of insisting on x = 2 itself, watch what f(x) does as x creeps toward 2 without ever landing on it, from both directions:

From either side, f(x) closes in on 4 — and you can check algebraically why: for any x other than 2, (x² − 4)/(x − 2) factors to (x − 2)(x + 2)/(x − 2), and the (x − 2) terms cancel legitimately (they’re nonzero, since x isn’t 2 yet), leaving plain x + 2, which equals 4 exactly when x = 2. So you can say, with total confidence, that the limit of f(x) as x approaches 2 is 4 — written limx→2 f(x) = 4 — even though f(2) itself is not defined at all. That gap between what a function does near a point and what it equals at that point is the first thing to hold onto: a limit is a statement about approaches vs. equals, and the two can genuinely disagree.

A pattern in a table of six numbers, though, is evidence, not proof — it tells you what seems to be happening, not what must happen in every case, however small the gap gets. For most of calculus’s history, mathematicians didn’t have anything better: the standard language was that x “moves toward” 2 and f(x) “moves toward” 4, as if a point were physically traveling along the number line. That phrasing gets the right answer most of the time, but it smuggles in a notion of motion and time that has no proper place in pure logic, and worse, it never says how close is close enough to count as proof. The Czech mathematician Bernard Bolzano sketched a more careful version as early as 1817; the French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy introduced the Greek letter used for it today in his 1821 textbook; but it was the German mathematician Karl Weierstrass, in lectures delivered in Berlin and fully worked out by 1861, who finished the job and gave the limit the exact form every textbook has used since.

That form reframes “gets arbitrarily close” as a challenge-and-response game about tolerances, with no motion anywhere in it. Call the tolerance you’re willing to accept on the output side epsilon (ε, the conventional Greek letter for it) — however small you demand it be. The claim limx→c f(x) = L says: whatever epsilon you throw at it, no matter how tiny, a matching tolerance on the input side, called delta (δ), can always be produced, with the guarantee that every x within delta of c (other than c itself) lands f(x) within epsilon of L. If even one impossibly small epsilon exists for which no delta can be found, the limit isn’t L — or doesn’t exist at all. The exact mechanics, worked through on a real example, are in the Equation / Model section below; the concept to take from this paragraph is the shape of the deal: a limit is not “it looks like it’s heading there,” it is “for every possible tolerance you name, here is the window that guarantees it,” produced on demand.

That same machinery gives you continuity almost for free. A function is continuous at a point c if three things line up: the limit as x approaches c exists, f(c) is actually defined, and the two are equal — no jump, no hole, no break in the graph at that point. The example above fails this test at x = 2: the limit exists and equals 4, but f(2) itself is undefined, so f has what’s called a removable discontinuity there — a single-point hole you could patch by simply declaring f(2) = 4 and nothing else about the function would change. A genuine jump discontinuity is different in kind: a tax bracket or a shipping-rate schedule that charges a flat fee up to a weight threshold and a different flat fee the instant you cross it has no single value the function is closing in on from both sides at that threshold — approaching from below gives one number, approaching from above gives another, so no limit exists there at all, let alone a continuous one.

The geometric picture of a limit is an asymptote: a line a curve gets arbitrarily close to but never reaches. Graph y = 1/x. As x grows without any bound in the positive direction, y shrinks toward 0 but never touches it — a horizontal asymptote. As x itself shrinks toward 0 from the positive side, y grows without any bound — a vertical asymptote at x = 0, the one point where the function has no value to give at all. Every asymptote on a graph is a limit statement drawn as a picture: “this curve approaches this line forever, and the gap between them can be made smaller than any tolerance you name, without the gap ever reaching zero.”

That phrase — approaching forever without arriving — should sound familiar from two lessons ago. Day 8’s infinite series, the one that resolved Zeno’s paradox of Achilles and the tortoise, worked by adding infinitely many shrinking pieces and watching the running total close in on a fixed number without any single partial sum ever reaching it. That was a limit the whole time, described informally. Today supplies the machinery that makes a claim like “this infinite sum equals exactly 2, not approximately 2” a provable statement rather than a plausible one.

Key Concepts

Limit
The value a function (or sequence) gets arbitrarily close to as its input approaches some point, regardless of whether the function is even defined at that exact point.
Approaches vs. equals
What a function does in the immediate neighborhood of a point (what it approaches) can differ from, or simply not exist at, what the function is defined to equal at that exact point.
Continuity
A function is continuous at a point when the limit there exists, the function is actually defined there, and the two values are equal — no jump and no hole in the graph.
Asymptote
A line that a curve gets arbitrarily close to without ever touching — the geometric picture of a limit, horizontal (as the input grows without bound) or vertical (near a point the function blows up at).

Example / Application

Here is where the limit earns its keep outside pure algebra: measuring a rate of change at a single instant, which is exactly the kind of question that opened this lesson.

Suppose a company’s quarterly revenue, in thousands of dollars, follows R(t) = 3t², with t measured in years since launch. The average growth rate over any stretch of time is easy: change in revenue divided by change in time. Over year 4 to year 5, that’s [R(5) − R(4)] / (5 − 4) = (75 − 48) / 1 = 27 — revenue grew at an average rate of $27,000 per year over that stretch. But what is the growth rate at the single instant t = 4, not averaged over any stretch at all? Shrink the stretch and watch the average rate as the gap, h, shrinks toward zero:

The pattern closes in on 24, and algebra confirms it’s exact, not just a trend in the table: [R(4+h) − R(4)]/h = [3(4+h)² − 3(4)²]/h = [3(16 + 8h + h²) − 48]/h = (24h + 3h²)/h = 24 + 3h, for any h other than zero. As h shrinks toward 0 — without ever equaling it, since dividing by h = 0 is exactly the dead end this lesson started with — the expression 24 + 3h closes in on 24 with no ambiguity at all. The instantaneous growth rate at year 4 is $24,000 per year, obtained entirely by taking a limit of an average.

Hold onto that calculation. “The limit of an average rate of change, as the interval it’s averaged over shrinks to nothing” is not just a trick for this one example — it is, word for word, the definition of the derivative that tomorrow’s lesson builds in general.

Equation / Model

Here is the definition itself — the one Weierstrass finished in 1861, and the one every later use of the word “limit” in this subject means precisely.

limx→c f(x) = L  ⇔  ∀ε>0, ∃δ>0 : 0<|x−c|<δ ⇒ |f(x)−L|<ε

The limit of f of x, as x approaches c, equals L, if and only if: for every epsilon greater than zero, there exists a delta greater than zero, such that for every x, if the distance between x and c is greater than zero and less than delta, then the distance between f of x and L is less than epsilon.

x
the input to the function, the variable being moved toward c
c
the point x is approaching (the function need not be defined at c itself)
f(x)
the function, or quantity, whose behavior near c is being measured
L
the proposed limit — the value being claimed as what f(x) approaches
ε (epsilon)
the tolerance demanded on the output: how close to L the claim requires f(x) to land, however small a number is chosen
δ (delta)
the matching tolerance on the input: a window around c, dependent on the chosen epsilon, guaranteeing the output lands within it
0<|x−c|<δ
x is within delta of c, but is explicitly not equal to c — the definition never requires, or even looks at, the value of f at c itself

That reads as pure logic, so it’s worth seeing it actually used once, on the simplest case there is: f(x) = 3x + 1, c = 2, and the claim is limx→2 f(x) = 7 (plain substitution gives the same answer here, since this function has no hole to worry about; the point is to show the machinery working, not to solve a hard case). Take the quantity the definition needs to control, |f(x) − L|, and simplify it:

|f(x) − 7| = |(3x + 1) − 7| = |3x − 6| = 3|x − 2|

The absolute value of f of x minus 7 equals the absolute value of 3x plus 1 minus 7, which equals the absolute value of 3x minus 6, which equals 3 times the absolute value of x minus 2.

That one algebraic line is the whole proof, because it hands you the matching delta directly: you need 3|x − 2| < ε, which is exactly the same demand as |x − 2| < ε/3. So for any epsilon at all, choose δ = ε/3. Whenever 0 < |x − 2| < δ, you get |f(x) − 7| = 3|x − 2| < 3·(ε/3) = ε, exactly as required. Because that delta exists for every epsilon you could ever name — not just the ones checked in a table — the limit is proven, not merely observed.

Why It Matters

For about a century and a half after Newton and Leibniz, calculus was in an odd position: it worked. Planetary orbits, bridges, the motion of projectiles — it predicted all of them correctly, using a method nobody could fully justify. The flaw wasn’t in the arithmetic; it was in the foundation underneath it, the infinitesimal that Berkeley had correctly identified as needing to be both something and nothing. The limit, in its finished epsilon-delta form, is the actual answer to Berkeley’s challenge: it lets every calculus statement that ever said “as x gets infinitely close to c” be rewritten using nothing but ordinary numbers, ordinary subtraction, and ordinary inequalities — no mysterious vanishing quantity required anywhere in the argument.

That rewrite is also, very concretely, your onramp into the rest of this block. Tomorrow’s lesson defines the derivative — the slope of a curve at a single point, the rate of change of one quantity with respect to another — as exactly the calculation you just did with the revenue example: the limit of an average rate of change, as the interval it’s measured over shrinks to nothing. Two lessons after that, the integral is built the same way, as the limit of a sum of infinitely many infinitesimally thin slices. Neither idea needs a single new logical tool beyond what sits in today’s formula plate — they need you to trust that “approaching forever without arriving” can mean something exact. That trust is what today bought.

Remember These
  • A limit describes what a quantity approaches as its input approaches some value — it says nothing about, and doesn’t require, what the quantity equals at that value itself.
  • Calculus ran for roughly 150 years on undefined “infinitesimals” before the limit, finished by Weierstrass in 1861, gave it a logically solid foundation.
  • The epsilon-delta definition turns “gets arbitrarily close” into a provable guarantee: for every output tolerance (epsilon) demanded, a matching input window (delta) can always be produced.
  • Continuity means the limit at a point equals the function’s actual value there — no jump, no hole.
  • An asymptote is a limit drawn as a picture: a line a curve approaches forever and never touches.
Think About It

The epsilon-delta definition explicitly excludes x = c — the condition is 0 < |x − c| < δ, never x = c itself. That’s exactly why the limit of (x² − 4)/(x − 2) at x = 2 can equal 4 even though the function has no value at x = 2 at all. Given that the definition never looks at f(c), what is a limit actually measuring, if it isn’t “what the function does at that point”? What does that tell you about why continuity — the case where the limit and the function’s actual value happen to agree — needs to be stated as a separate, extra condition rather than being automatic?
