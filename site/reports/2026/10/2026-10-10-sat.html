Today’s News

Isaias comes ashore near Destin as a Category 2, weaker than forecast, and nearly 900,000 lose power

The storm lost strength in its final hours and landed about 40–50 miles east of Pensacola. It is now a post-tropical rainstorm over Alabama, with flooding the main threat left.

The National Hurricane Center says Isaias made landfall near Destin, in Florida’s Okaloosa County, at about 8:30 PM CDT Friday (9:30 PM ET). At landfall it had 105 mph winds and an estimated central pressure of 971 mb. That makes it a strong Category 2. On Friday morning it had been a Category 3 at 120 mph and 959 mb, forecast to come ashore near Pensacola. One CBS item puts landfall closer to 8 PM ET; most accounts citing NHC say 9:30.

Overnight, NHC corrected an inland wind estimate from 85 to 100 mph, then the storm weakened to Category 1 and became post-tropical. Advisory 15, at 4 AM CDT, put the remnant about 25 miles south-southwest of Montgomery, Alabama, with 35 mph winds. It is forecast to dissipate Sunday. All surge and tropical-storm warnings have been dropped. What remains is rain: 4–8 inches across southern Alabama, the Florida Panhandle and southwest Georgia, locally up to 15.

The damage so far.

  • Power. About 874,000–900,000 customers were out this morning, depending on the outlet and the hour. PowerOutage figures cited by CBS: Florida 430,443, Alabama 326,057, Georgia 127,905.
  • Casualties. Unconfirmed by the state CNN, citing an Escambia County spokesperson, reports one death from a falling tree. Gov. DeSantis said the state had confirmed no deaths.
  • Wind and water. Part of a Pensacola motel roof was torn off, and trees and streetlights are down downtown. Reported gusts run from about 86 mph at Pensacola’s airport to 102 mph elsewhere in the city. Surge overtopped beach walkways in Destin. CNN reports about five feet of surge; NHC had forecast 2–4 feet on that stretch, and the five-foot figure is single-sourced.
  • Declarations. Florida, Alabama and Georgia declared states of emergency before landfall. This edition found no confirmed federal declaration.
Why it matters Oil producers had shut in 71.5% of Gulf of Mexico output and evacuated 129 of 371 staffed platforms by Friday. The storm’s track, east of Mobile Bay, kept its strongest winds away from the Louisiana and Texas refining belt and the Pascagoula refinery. Oil barely moved on the shut-ins all week, which says traders expected a quick restart. Restart figures and refinery checks are not yet published, so the energy damage is unknown this morning.
What’s next The offshore regulator’s daily shut-in reports from Monday will show how fast output returns. The remnants reach western Pennsylvania late tonight (see Local). Isaias was also the latest first Atlantic hurricane in more than a century, according to AccuWeather.

The Kremlin says Putin passed Iran’s terms to Trump, as Washington licenses Russian diesel

The diesel deal is the clearer fact: Treasury has issued a license easing sanctions on Russian diesel through April 2027. The Iran channel rests on Moscow’s word.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said on Saturday that Vladimir Putin conveyed Tehran’s position on ending the US–Iran war during Friday’s phone call with President Trump, “in coordination with” Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. He gave no details. The Kremlin also says Trump welcomed Russia’s role. That account is Moscow’s alone, and the White House has not described the call’s Iran portion. On Thursday Pezeshkian told Putin that Iran had “revised” its proposals and would finalize them through mediators. Trump has said the US will not strike Iran before the Nov. 3 elections, but that the naval blockade stays.

The diesel part is on the record. Trump said Russia will supply about 300,000 tons immediately, 500,000 in November and 1 million tons after that, to ease fuel prices. Treasury issued a temporary general license allowing it. Several outlets note that this cuts against the Russia sanctions law signed last month. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said Putin played down any quick return to Ukraine peace talks.

Ukraine’s President Zelensky called the license “a weak decision … by strong partners” that “plays into Russia’s hands.” He offered to stop Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries if Russia stops hitting Ukraine’s power grid.

Why it matters Diesel is the fuel in Delta’s cost warning and in this week’s rise in inflation expectations. The volumes are small against a world diesel market of roughly 30 million barrels a day; one million tons is about 7.5 million barrels. Analysts quoted by CNBC doubt it moves prices much. The bigger change is the signal: Washington is trading Russia sanctions relief for fuel ahead of the midterms, and Moscow now presents itself as the go-between with Tehran.

The Week’s Ten Stories

1 · The long bond hits a 24-year high, then $119 billion of Treasuries sells cleanly

The question that hung over the market for a month was whether anyone would buy long Treasuries at these yields. This week buyers turned up, but only at about 5.30% and 5.62%.

How it started. The 10-year ended last week at 5.28% and the 30-year at 5.63%, both higher even though the September jobs report was weak. September’s note auctions had gone badly. Gilts above 6% and record Japanese yields showed the selling was global.

How it developed.

  • Monday: the 10-year closed at 5.31%, its highest close of the cycle, after touching about 5.347% intraday.
  • Tuesday: the $58 billion 3-year sold at 4.932%, 0.2 basis points through its pre-auction level.
  • Wednesday: the 10-year touched about 5.365% in the morning, its highest since 2002. Then the $39 billion 10-year reopening sold at 5.300%. That is about 1.7 basis points through the pre-auction level, with foreign and institutional buyers taking about 80% and dealers left only 2.5%.
  • Thursday: the $22 billion 30-year sold at 5.618%, the highest 30-year auction yield since 2000. It tailed by 0.1 basis point, essentially on the screws. Bonds rallied anyway after the president’s Iran pledge. The 10-year fell 6 basis points to 5.22%.
  • Friday: the 2-year rose 5 basis points on Fed talk and the consumer survey. The 10-year finished at 5.24%.

How it ended. For the week, the 10-year fell 4 basis points, the 30-year 3 and the 2-year 3. The 10-year’s real yield eased to 2.87% while break-even inflation was steady near 2.35%, so the move came from real yields, not from inflation fears.

What was misunderstood. Much commentary treated the morning high on Wednesday as the start of a break-out. The auction showed a deep buyer base at 5.30%. That level now looks like a working ceiling, but it is a demand ceiling. Nothing in this week’s data lowers the supply that pushed yields there.

Unresolved. No coupon auctions until late October. CPI on Wednesday is the next test, and the Treasury’s quarterly refunding comes after the Fed meeting. Keep on the watchlist.

2 · The Fed’s minutes point to one more hike, but not necessarily this month

The record of September’s meeting was hawkish on the destination and silent on timing. Waller then filled in the timing: hikes “do not have to” come at consecutive meetings.

The minutes of the Sept. 15–16 meeting, at which the Fed raised its target range to 3.75–4.00%, came out Wednesday. Their key passages:

  • The next step. “Most participants” said another increase “would likely be appropriate by year end.”
  • The risks. “Almost all” saw inflation risks tilted up. Labor-market risks “had diminished and were now broadly balanced.”
  • Energy. “Many” warned that the longer energy prices stay high, the greater the risk of broader price pressure. The minutes also name AI-related borrowing as a source of upward pressure on long-term yields.

Governor Christopher Waller said Thursday that he expects “further rate hikes will be needed” if data meet expectations, but “we do not have to hike at consecutive meetings.” St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem said “more monetary policy firming will be required.” San Francisco’s Mary Daly said that if this year’s shocks prove ordinary, “we may not need more.”

Friday’s University of Michigan survey was reported at 46.3, near its record low of 44.8. Households’ year-ahead inflation expectations rose to 4.7% and five-to-ten-year expectations to 3.5%. That longer-run number is the one Fed officials quote most.

Where it ended. CME FedWatch put an October hike at about 18% and at least one hike by December at about 85% (as relayed by FXStreet). What was misunderstood: markets often read “most participants” as a vote count for October. It is a statement about the year. Unresolved: September CPI on Wednesday is the last inflation report before the Oct. 27–28 meeting. Keep on the watchlist.

3 · SpaceX buys nationwide airwaves, and the phone companies lose a tenth of their value

In four days SpaceX won FCC approval for its own phone-to-satellite constellation, arranged about $40 billion of debt for chips, and agreed to buy low-band spectrum for a standalone carrier.

The build-up. SpaceX (SPCX) jumped 7.6% on Monday to $171.09 as Morgan Stanley repeated a $300 target. On Wednesday Bloomberg reported that SpaceX was arranging about $40 billion of debt, mostly investment-grade bonds, to buy Nvidia chips. Its credit-default swaps hit a record. On Thursday the FCC reportedly authorized a direct-to-cell constellation of about 15,000 satellites and waived the requirement to sell through a carrier. AST SpaceMobile fell 6.1% that day.

The deal. After Thursday’s close SpaceX agreed to buy Grain Management’s nationwide 800 MHz licences: up to 14 MHz of paired low-band spectrum in 3,224 counties, for its Starlink Mobile service. The companies gave no price; the Wall Street Journal and Reuters report about $8 billion in cash. Grain bought the licences from T-Mobile in August. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr praised the deal, which still needs his agency’s approval. Musk called it “the last critical piece.”

The reaction. On Friday T-Mobile fell 13.3%, AT&T 10.8% and Verizon 10.1%, on three to four times normal volume. CNBC called it Verizon’s worst day since 2002. Tower owners rose: Crown Castle +15.6%, American Tower +9.3%. We still have no confirmed explanation for the tower rally. SpaceX’s first post-IPO lockup ended Friday; the stock rose 1.3% to $162.57 on normal volume, up 2.3% for the week. AST SpaceMobile lost 12.8% on the week.

What was misunderstood A Verizon spokesman said spectrum is “just empty airwaves” without a network. The carriers’ point has merit: a satellite cell is huge, so capacity per user in a city is a fraction of a tower’s. But the market was not pricing city traffic. It was pricing the loss of exclusivity: someone other than three companies can now sell a phone plan that works everywhere. Roughly $60–70 billion of carrier value came off in a day, by this paper’s arithmetic.

4 · An OpenAI revenue report splits the AI trade between the chipmakers and their customers

On Thursday the Financial Times reported that OpenAI told investors it was running at about $50 billion a year, not the roughly $68–70 billion that had circulated. Chip stocks lost 4.3% on the week.

CNBC confirmed the FT figure. Its source said the bigger number had counted cloud partners’ gross sales, while OpenAI books only its net share. On Friday Bloomberg reported that OpenAI expects at least $70 billion annualized by the end of 2026, and that its investor materials cite 77% run-rate growth in the quarter. None of this is audited, and OpenAI has not commented on the record.

On Thursday the semiconductor fund SMH fell 2.8%. Micron fell 4.8%, Broadcom 4.4%, Nvidia 2.9% and Oracle 5.5%, and the Nasdaq lost 1.25%. Yet eight of eleven sectors rose and the equal-weight S&P gained 0.6%. Money moved within the market rather than leaving it. By Friday the split was clear: for the week Amazon gained 4.3% and Microsoft 3.4%, while Nvidia lost 2.0%, AMD 4.1% and Micron 4.3%. The buyers of AI chips did better than their sellers.

What was misunderstood. The “$20 billion miss” is mostly a definition. OpenAI’s own number grew; the circulating number was never OpenAI’s. The real lesson is that AI-lab “run-rates” are not comparable across companies. Anthropic, which reportedly counts some partner revenue gross, is preparing an IPO, and a public filing would be the first audited figures from any frontier lab. Unresolved: TSMC reports Thursday. Keep on the watchlist.

5 · Oil holds above $100 as the Gulf war spreads and Washington rules out strikes until November

Brent rose 2.4% on the week to $104.72. The two big events cancelled out: a report of pre-election strike planning, then the president’s pledge of no strike before Nov. 3.

Early in the week Brent drifted down to just above $100. The G7’s 100-million-barrel stock release was arriving and Middle East exports via routes outside the strait were rising. On Monday the Houthis claimed missile strikes on Aramco near Riyadh and at Khurais; the Saudi-led coalition called that “misleading,” and Brent settled at $100.32.

Thursday changed the tone. The Atlantic reported that the White House had asked for Iran strike options usable before the midterms. A tanker was hit off northern Qatar, the first attack in the western Gulf in more than six months, and a second off Fujairah. Brent traded above $105. That afternoon President Trump posted that the US would not attack Iran “at any time prior to the Midterm Elections,” while keeping the blockade. Brent still settled up 4.1% at $104.28, and $104.72 on Friday.

The attacks kept spreading. UKMTO counts 14 tanker attacks since Sept. 30, and the IMO’s count for the week to Oct. 4 was a record. Only seven tankers transited Hormuz on Tuesday, a two-month low (Kpler). The IRGC claimed a strike on an LPG carrier south of the strait, which its owner denies, and threatened ships anywhere in the region. On Thursday a Houthi attack on Riyadh’s main airport killed three Saudis, including a Saudia pilot. Five Western governments now advise against travel to Saudi Arabia.

Unresolved. Iran says it will answer Washington’s response to its seven-day plan through mediators, and Moscow now says it is carrying messages. Nobody has confirmed who is firing at the tankers. Keep on the watchlist.

6 · The fuel shock reaches earnings: Delta cuts its year, PepsiCo cuts its outlook, and investors shrug

The first big reports of the quarter showed what $100 oil costs companies. Both stocks did better than their numbers.

Delta earned an adjusted $1.72 a share in the third quarter, below the $2.00–2.50 it guided in July and below every consensus figure we saw. Fuel expense rose 62% from a year ago, to about $4.1 billion, and the airline expects about $6 billion of extra fuel cost this year. It cut full-year EPS to $5.10–5.60 from $6.50–7.50 and assumes about $4.25 a gallon in the fourth quarter, against $3.15 in July. Revenue was a record $17.6 billion, up 16%. The stock fell 4–5% before the open and closed up 3 cents. Chief executive Ed Bastian said demand is strong.

PepsiCo beat on sales ($25.27 billion against $24.96 billion) and core EPS ($2.34 against about $2.29). It cut its full-year core EPS growth to 2.5–3.5% from the low end of 5–7%, citing rising input costs. The stock rose 3.7% from a 52-week low.

The pattern: costs are rising, demand is holding, and investors are treating the fuel bill as temporary. The administration’s diesel tax order and the Russian diesel license are aimed at the same pressure. Bank earnings on Tuesday will show whether consumers are still spending through it.

What Changed in the World

The long end found its price. For a month the bond market’s question was who buys at these yields. This week it got an answer: foreign and institutional buyers at 5.30% on the 10-year and about 5.62% on the 30-year. That is a ceiling set by demand, not by the Fed or by falling inflation, so it holds only while those buyers keep coming. But it replaced a market in free fall with a market that has a level.

Satellites became a phone company. Until Thursday, satellite-to-phone service was something the carriers rented out. SpaceX now has its own nationwide low-band spectrum, its own constellation licence, and permission to sell direct. The carriers lost about a tenth of their value in a day. Two questions now decide whether that was right: how the FCC rules on the Grain deal, and what Starlink Mobile charges.

The AI trade grew a fault line. One unaudited revenue figure took 4% off the chipmakers in a week, while Amazon and Microsoft rose. Investors now distinguish between those who sell AI capacity and those who buy it. The buyers are funding more of their spending with debt: SpaceX’s $40 billion, Broadcom’s reported private credit, and the AI borrowing the Fed’s minutes named.

The Iran war has a pause, not an end. The president’s pre-election no-strike pledge, a blockade that stays, attacks that have spread to Qatar’s and the UAE’s waters, and a Russian go-between. Oil priced all of it at about $104, roughly unchanged from where it began.

The Economy & Central Banks

The week’s data, actual against consensus:

  • Consumer credit (Aug.): +1.9% annualized. Revolving credit, mostly credit cards, shrank at a 4.2% rate.

Scorecard

Forty-seven forecasts came due: 21 correct, 26 wrong, none partial. A forecast counts as correct when what it said would happen did happen, whatever probability it carried. Many “wrong” grades are low-probability calls that did not happen and were sensibly sized; the lessons separate those from real misses. One more call, on Missouri’s secretary of state, comes due Sunday and is graded next week.

Last Sunday’s outlook: 8 of 21 correct.

  • The base case held. The weekday calls.

  • The rates line kept missing just above the move. Three versions of “the 10-year closes at 5.35% or higher” (40–55%, 30–45%, 20–35%) all missed: the highest close was 5.31%.   • Friday’s Brent call was wrong. We expected a settlement below Thursday’s $104.28 (65–80%) after the no-strike pledge pushed Brent down 1.3% at the open. It recovered and settled at $104.72. A morning move is not a settlement.
  • Correct: the minutes showing a majority for another hike (65–80%); Michigan sentiment below 48.1 (55–65%); the S&P above 7,800 on Friday (50–60%); Constellation keeping half its Google-deal gain (60–70%; it closed at $298.07); Brent above $100 on Friday (60–75%); the 10-year at 5.20% or higher on Friday (70–80%); a Duquesne Light strike beginning (10–20%, too low).

The pattern. This paper’s market-level calls were better than its threshold calls. Where a forecast named a direction or a range, it was usually right. Where it named a line just past the recent extreme, it usually missed. Sunday’s edition will set lines with more room.

Overhyped: the “$20 billion OpenAI miss.” It moved the Nasdaq more than any economic report this week, and it was mostly a gap between two ways of counting revenue. Overhyped: Russian diesel as relief at the pump. A first tranche of 300,000 tons is about two million barrels, against a world diesel market of about 30 million barrels a day. The policy change is bigger than the volume: a sanctions waiver through April 2027.
