Top Stories

The S&P 500 closes at a record above 7,800 as Treasury yields ease from their 2002 highs

The 10-year fell 4bp to 5.27% and the 3-year auction went off cleanly. Small caps fell anyway, and the record rests on a narrower base than the headline suggests.

The S&P 500 rose 0.58% to 7,818.93, a record close and its first finish above 7,800. During the session it reached 7,844.52, a new intraday high (Yahoo Finance, TheStreet). The Nasdaq Composite added 0.45% to 27,599.79, a second straight record close, and the Dow rose 0.49%. The Russell 2000 fell 0.59%, and IWM, the small-cap ETF, dropped 0.7% on normal volume.

The bond market gave back some of Monday’s selloff. Treasury’s official closing curve:

  • 3-month bill: 4.21%, down 1bp
  • 2-year: 4.79%, down 5bp
  • 3-year: 4.88%, down 9bp, after the auction
  • 10-year: 5.27%, down 4bp
  • 30-year: 5.64%, down 2bp

Several reports tied the gain to lower oil prices and Treasury yields, along with buying in AI-linked stocks (CNBC, TheStreet). Likely contributor The yield decline lines up in time with the rally. But oil finished slightly higher on the day, so the oil explanation does not hold at the close. The day’s clearest single catalysts were company-specific: Google’s nuclear power agreement with Constellation Energy and Marvell’s investor day.

Breadth was mixed. Equal weight (RSP +0.57%) kept pace with the S&P for a second session. But small caps fell, as did emerging markets (EEM −0.7%) and semiconductors as a group (SMH −0.2%). Seagate, Western Digital and Micron pulled the group down, offsetting gains in AMD and Broadcom. On Monday’s full-market count, about 31% of US stocks with enough history were above their 50-day trend. That measure is an estimate from our own data, which has only just reached 50 sessions.

Why it matters A record with the 10-year still above 5.25% means investors are paying more for earnings even as bonds pay more. That works while profit forecasts keep rising. The S&P is 1.8% above its 50-day average, while IWM is 3.7% below its own. The rally is still a large-cap story.
What’s next Wednesday brings the $39 billion 10-year reopening at 1:00 PM and the minutes of the September Fed meeting at 2:00 PM. Thursday brings the $22 billion 30-year at 1:00 PM and PepsiCo’s earnings.

Sources: Treasury par yield curve (primary, Oct 6); Yahoo Finance; TheStreet; CNBC; Massive grouped-daily bars (Oct 5 breadth).

Google signs a 20-year nuclear power deal with Constellation; utilities jump 3%

Constellation rose 12.3% and Vistra 10.8% as investors repriced what AI data centres will pay for always-on power.

Google agreed to a 20-year power purchase agreement for 890 megawatts of new nuclear capacity from Constellation Energy on the PJM grid, which serves the mid-Atlantic, including Pennsylvania. A separate supply agreement covers about 2,700 megawatts. Reports say the arrangements support more than $4.3 billion of new Constellation investment (CNBC, GuruFocus, 24/7 Wall St.). We could not read the company’s release in full, so the split between new reactors and uprates at existing plants is not yet clear.

The market response:

  • Constellation Energy: +12.3%, to $300.40
  • Vistra, another nuclear-heavy generator: +10.8%, to $160.50
  • Utilities sector (XLU): +3.0%, the best sector by almost two points, and up 3.7% over five days
  • Eaton, which makes electrical equipment: +2.9%

The Motley Fool reported a separate multibillion-dollar Department of Energy nuclear announcement the same day. We could not retrieve its details.

Why it matters A 20-year contract with a creditworthy buyer is the kind of revenue that justifies building new reactors. It also tells investors that AI computing’s power demand is being locked in for decades, not just a few years. For PJM customers, including Duquesne Light’s, more data-centre demand on the same grid has been a source of upward pressure on capacity prices.

Sources: CNBC (midday movers); GuruFocus; 24/7 Wall St.; Motley Fool; Finnhub and Yahoo closing prices.

The trade deficit widens to $105.6 billion in August as imports hit a record

Imports of AI hardware and other goods rose $17 billion in a month, and July was revised wider. Net exports will be a large drag on third-quarter growth.

The Commerce Department’s August trade figures (BEA and Census, released 8:30 AM):

  • Total deficit: $105.6 billion, against about $102 billion expected (Reuters and Bloomberg polls)
  • July: revised to $92.8 billion, from $88.6 billion as first reported
  • Exports: $315.2 billion, up $4.5 billion
  • Imports: $420.8 billion, up $17.2 billion, a record
  • Goods deficit: $136.6 billion. Services surplus: $31.0 billion.

It is the widest deficit since just before the 2025 tariffs took effect (CNBC). Coverage pointed to AI-related goods such as computers, servers and chips as the main source of the import jump. These are nominal dollar figures, not adjusted for prices, and the July revision means the third-quarter deficit is wider than first thought. The Atlanta Fed’s GDPNow model was reported to show net exports subtracting about 2.6 percentage points from third-quarter growth. We saw that figure only in a search summary.

Why it matters Imports for the AI buildout are investment spending: they add to equipment investment in GDP while subtracting through net exports. The headline GDP number will look weaker than the underlying demand. The bond market did not react much. Yields fell across the day.

Sources: BEA, U.S. International Trade in Goods and Services, August 2026 (primary); CNBC; Reuters via BNN Bloomberg; Transport Topics.

The $58 billion 3-year auction clears without a tail before the bigger tests

Demand was adequate rather than strong: foreign buying was light, and domestic buyers filled the gap.

Treasury sold $58 billion of 3-year notes at a high yield of 4.932%, 0.2bp below the 4.934% when-issued level at the 1:00 PM deadline. That is a small “stop-through,” meaning the government borrowed slightly more cheaply than the market expected. The details (MNI, investingLive, Newsquawk):

  • Bid-to-cover: 2.62, below the 2.65 recent average and September’s 2.72
  • Indirect bidders (a proxy for foreign and institutional demand): 57.6%, well below the 65.9% average
  • Direct bidders (domestic funds buying for themselves): 31.7%, against a 21.5% average
  • Dealers, who absorb what is left: 10.7%, against 12.6%

The yield of 4.932% was about 46bp higher than September’s 3-year auction, at 4.474%. The 3-year par yield closed 9bp lower on the day, at 4.88%.

What’s next Wednesday’s $39 billion 10-year reopening comes with the 10-year near its highest level since 2002 and an hour before the Fed minutes. Sunday’s edition put the chance that it tails, meaning prices weaker than expected at the deadline, at about even. Thursday’s $22 billion 30-year follows. Bloomberg reported that BNP Paribas warned that dropping the 20-year bond, an option attributed to Treasury Secretary Bessent, could push yields higher by shifting supply.

Sources: TreasuryDirect auction results via MNI/TradingView and investingLive; Newsquawk; Bloomberg; Treasury par yield curve.

Paramount Skydance closes its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery

The roughly $110 billion deal closed after a judge approved a settlement with 12 states that imposes five years of conditions.

Paramount Skydance completed its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery on Tuesday, with David Ellison as chief executive, according to CNBC and NBC News. The settlement with 12 state attorneys general sets five-year conditions on theatrical releases, production spending, cable carriage negotiations and news operations. Reports name the combined company “Skydance.” We have not seen the closing 8-K, so treat the name and the exact value as reported rather than confirmed.

The financing was arranged last week: about $30 billion of investment-grade bonds, $11.4 billion of second-lien debt plus a euro tranche, and about $9.5 billion of loans. Paramount Skydance shares rose 2.9%.

Why it matters It joins a major Hollywood studio, HBO, CNN and CBS under one owner. That makes it one of the most indebted media companies, so its bonds will be a test of credit markets in the months ahead. The states’ conditions on news operations are unusual for a merger settlement.

Sources: CNBC; NBC News; Washington Times; Finnhub quotes.

Marvell lifts its long-term target and Ciena jumps 14% as AI networking rallies

The chips and gear that connect AI processors outperformed the processors themselves.

At its investor day, Marvell Technology raised its fiscal 2028 revenue target to $20 billion, from $18 billion. Analysts had expected about $18.2 billion, according to FactSet. Marvell also put fiscal 2031 revenue at $70–90 billion and its addressable market at $400 billion by 2030 (GuruFocus, Invezz). The stock rose about 10% early and closed 5.8% higher at $287.01.

Other AI networking and infrastructure names rose with it:

  • Ciena (optical networking): +13.9%
  • Astera Labs (data-centre connectivity chips): +7.6%
  • Nebius (AI cloud): +7.4%
  • Arista Networks: +4.1%
  • Broadcom: +3.7%
  • AMD: +2.8%

Nvidia rose just 0.1%. Reports said AMD set a record high.

Why it matters Investors are spreading their AI bets from the processors to the plumbing: networking, optics, custom chips and power. A long-term target is a forecast, not an order book, and Marvell gave back about half of its early gain by the close.

Sources: GuruFocus; Invezz; CNBC; Yahoo Finance; Finnhub closing quotes.

Ukraine strikes the Moscow region with hundreds of drones and hits a major fuel depot

Moscow’s airports halted flights. Two people were killed, and the depot that supplies the capital’s three main airports was reported burning.

Moscow Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said air defences engaged about 650 drones overnight. Other accounts put the number aimed at Moscow and nearby regions at about 290. Two people were killed and seven to nine injured, according to Russian officials quoted by Al Jazeera and the Kyiv Post. The Volodarskaya oil depot and pumping station, the region’s largest, which supplies jet fuel to Vnukovo, Domodedovo and Sheremetyevo airports, was reported hit and on fire. Ukraine has not detailed the targets.

At sea, a Turkish-owned ship carrying Ukrainian corn sank in Romania’s exclusive economic zone on Monday after a reported drone strike, killing two (Euronews). President Zelensky blamed Russia, and Romania has not attributed it. On Tuesday there were reports of two more merchant ships hit by drones off Bulgaria. We could not confirm those.

Why it matters Strikes on Russian refining and fuel storage reduce Russia’s exports of diesel and jet fuel at a time when the Gulf is already disrupted. Attacks on Black Sea shipping raise war-risk insurance and grain freight costs. Brent settled just above $100.

Sources: Al Jazeera; Kyiv Post; Euromaidan Press; Euronews; CBC. Casualty and drone counts are from Russian officials and differ between reports.

Saudi-backed forces say they retook Mocha; Houthi strikes hit two Saudi airports

The fight for the Red Sea coast near the Bab el-Mandeb strait is escalating while Iran’s Hormuz offer sits unanswered.

Yemen’s Saudi-backed government said its forces retook the port of Mocha, on the Red Sea near the Bab el-Mandeb strait (Bloomberg). The Houthis deny losing ground. Saudi Arabia said Houthi attacks on Monday evening hit airports at Jazan and Najran, wounding three. The coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at Khamis Mushait (Al Jazeera).

In the Gulf, Al Jazeera and CNBC counted nearly 20 commercial ships attacked in the past month, including at least seven tanker incidents in early October. Even so, Gulf exports outside Iran recovered to more than 81% of pre-war levels in September. We found no new confirmed tanker strike on Tuesday. Trump rejected Iran’s offer, conveyed through Qatar, to reopen the strait within seven days in exchange for an end to the US naval blockade and sanctions relief. He told Axios on Sunday that he expected talks to resume this week. We found no confirmation that they have.

Sources: Bloomberg; Al Jazeera; The National; CNBC; PBS NewsHour; Axios (via PBS).

The Economy

Trade. August’s $105.6 billion deficit and the upward revision to July are covered in Top Stories. One more point: the monthly figures are nominal. Some of the rise in imports reflects higher prices for energy and metals, not only higher volumes.

Energy outlook. The Energy Information Administration’s October outlook, published today, raised its fourth-quarter Brent forecast to $105 a barrel, up $14 from last month. It raised its 2026 average to $96 from $91. It assumes Middle East flows stay constrained through the fourth quarter and cites attacks including one on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline (EIA, via OilPrice.com). That is a forecast, not a market price. Brent settled at about $100.58.

The Fed. The target range is 3.75–4.00% after September’s hike. CME FedWatch put the odds of another hike on October 28 at about 22%, down from 64% a week ago (Benzinga). Later meetings showed higher odds of at least one hike by December, but we could not confirm how those figures are defined, so we are not printing them. Governor Bowman spoke on bank regulation. We found no remarks from her on the rate path. Wednesday brings the minutes of the September 15–16 meeting, and Governor Waller speaks on the outlook early Thursday.

Treasury. Beyond the auction, Treasury said its Do Not Pay screening reviewed more than 1.1 billion payments worth about $3.7 trillion in fiscal 2026 and stopped about 13,500 payments, worth $175 million, to people who had died (Treasury via RedState). Separately, an executive order signed Monday evening suspends the 24.4-cent federal excise tax on dyed diesel used on public roads from October 5 through December 31. GasBuddy’s Patrick De Haan said most drivers would see little benefit, because state laws still apply and no new supply is created.

Rates and credit. The par curve flattened slightly at the front: 2s10s 48bp, 5s30s 61bp. The 10-year real yield was 2.95% and the 10-year breakeven 2.36% on Monday’s FRED data. All of the past week’s rise in the 10-year has come from real yields. High-yield spreads were 3.12% on Monday, up 2bp (ICE BofA via FRED). Today HYG rose 0.4% and LQD 0.3%. SOFR was 3.89%.

The United States

Submarine industrial base. The Navy and the defence company Anduril announced a $6.6 billion plan to build Virginia-class submarine components, starting with torpedo tubes, at Sparrows Point near Baltimore. It combines about $3.7 billion of Anduril investment in a 2-million-square-foot plant with a Navy contract worth up to $2.9 billion. The project is expected to bring 3,100 direct jobs. Maryland Governor Wes Moore did not attend the announcement (Washington Times, WMAR). Anduril is private. General Dynamics and Huntington Ingalls are the submarine prime contractors. Huntington Ingalls fell 1.0%.

Trump’s Iran remark. At a Nebraska rally on Monday, the President said of Iran: “Let them take out Los Angeles, let them take out San Diego.” California Governor Gavin Newsom called the remark “deranged and dangerous.” Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass said it could compromise the cities’ safety. White House communications director Steven Cheung said the President meant US cities would have been threatened had a nuclear Iran not been stopped (CNN, Washington Post).

Government ads. Science

Nobel Prize in Physics. Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin–Madison won the prize alone, “for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin” (Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences). IceCube is a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice, threaded with light sensors, at the South Pole. In 2013 it reported neutrinos with energies too high to come from inside the solar system. In 2017 it traced one to a distant galaxy with a jet pointed at Earth, the blazar TXS 0506+056. Neutrinos barely interact with matter, so they travel across the universe in straight lines. That makes them a way to see the most violent places in the cosmos that light cannot reach. Halzen, who was born in Belgium, is IceCube’s principal investigator. The prize is 12 million Swedish kronor. Chemistry is announced on Wednesday.

Antarctic sea ice. A study in Nature reports that six atmospheric rivers, plus marine heatwaves, opened a hole of about 89,000 square kilometres, almost the size of Portugal, in East Antarctica’s winter sea ice in August 2024. It shows how warm air and ocean heat together can open sea ice in midwinter (Nature, ScienceAlert).

About 1,000 IBEW Local 29 workers remain on strike against Duquesne Light, which serves more than 500,000 customers in Allegheny and Beaver counties. City Council takes up a 2027 budget with no tax increase and frozen housing funds

Mayor Corey O’Connor’s preliminary 2027 budget, now before City Council, has no tax increase and no layoffs. It freezes the Stop the Violence Fund and the Housing Opportunity Fund, moving about $20 million to the general fund. It adds money for paving, landslides and stormwater. The administration projects a $30–40 million gap next year and about $250 million over five years. The housing-fund freeze directly affects affordable-housing projects waiting on city money.

Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania’s Meta settlement money sets up a fight over who controls it

Pennsylvania stands to receive $516–729 million over about ten years from Meta’s multistate child-safety settlement. The settlement sends the money to the Attorney General’s office, under Attorney General Dave Sunday, into a dedicated fund. The state fiscal code says such money belongs in the General Fund, where the legislature appropriates it. That points to a possible dispute between the Attorney General and lawmakers. The governor’s debate between Josh Shapiro and Stacy Garrity is Wednesday at 8:00 PM on Nexstar stations. None of them is in the Pittsburgh market, so check streaming.
