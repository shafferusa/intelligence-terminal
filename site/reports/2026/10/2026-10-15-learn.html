Enrico Fermi Guessed an Atomic Bomb’s Size by Dropping Scraps of Paper. He Landed Within a Factor of Two.

Thursday, October 15, 2026 · 16 min read

A method sturdy enough to size a nuclear blast with torn paper and no instruments works the same way on a battery pack: break the unknown into factors you can actually guess, carry the units through every step, and aim to land within a factor of a few rather than demand an exact number.

Mathematics

Day 1 of 293 · Mathematics 1 of 24 · Orders of magnitude: estimating the world in powers of ten

Ten miles from the first atomic bomb, a physicist tears up a sheet of paper

At 5:29 a.m. Mountain War Time on July 16, 1945, the United States detonated the world’s first nuclear weapon atop a 100-foot steel tower at the Trinity test site in the New Mexico desert. Enrico Fermi, one of the physicists who had helped design the device, watched from the base camp roughly 10 miles (16 km) away — close enough to see the fireball, far enough that the shock wave took its time arriving. By his own account, written up within days as a short memo, the air blast reached him about 40 seconds after the flash.

Fermi had come prepared with nothing more sophisticated than a handful of torn paper. As the shock wave approached, he held small scraps about six feet above the ground and let them fall — before the blast arrived, as it passed, and afterward. The pieces released during the blast were carried roughly two and a half meters from where he stood; the ones dropped before and after barely moved. From that single measurement — a distance he could have paced off in four strides — he wrote down an estimate: the device had released energy equivalent to about ten thousand tons of TNT.

He had no seismograph at hand, no blast-pressure gauge, and no result yet from the instrumented stations ringing the site, which took days to read. When those real instruments were analyzed, the 1945 figure came in at 18.6 kilotons. It was later revised to a long-standing official figure of about 21 kilotons, and a 2021 reanalysis of melted, radioactive desert sand from the site (H. D. Selby and colleagues, using mass spectrometry) put it at 24.8 kilotons, give or take 2. Three real answers, refined across 76 years, all in the same neighborhood of twenty-odd kilotons.

Fermi’s paper-scrap guess of ten thousand tons was roughly half that neighborhood — within a factor of about two. He had no formula written down for how a few feet of sideways drift at ten miles translates into a nuclear yield; physicist J. I. Katz, reconstructing the reasoning in 2021, found no record of the calibration step at all, only the observation and the number it produced. How does tearing up a sheet of paper, with no instrument faster than a wristwatch, get you within a factor of two of a number specialists with mass spectrometers are still refining three-quarters of a century later — and what kind of “right” is that?

The Big Idea

You can reach a usable estimate of almost any quantity — even one you could never measure directly — by breaking it into a handful of rough factors you actually can guess, multiplying them while carefully tracking their units and their powers of ten, and aiming to land within a factor of a few of the truth rather than demanding an exact figure. This way of reasoning is named after Fermi himself: a Fermi estimate (or Fermi problem) is any calculation built this way, the classic classroom example being “how many piano tuners work in Chicago?” — a question nobody can look up, built from a chain of smaller ones almost anyone can guess at (how many people live there, how many own pianos, how often a piano needs tuning, how many tunings one tuner can do in a year).

The method works because most of the hard quantities in the world are not single mysteries; they are the product of several much easier ones, chained together. You rarely know a bomb’s yield directly, but a physicist standing in the open with paper and a stopwatch can reason from a handful of things that are each individually guessable. Each factor in the chain carries its own uncertainty — you might be off by a factor of two or three on any one of them — but, for a reason the next section explains, the chain as a whole is often far more reliable than any single piece of it feels like it should be.

The goal is never a decimal point. It is the power of ten: is the answer close to ten, a hundred, a thousand, ten thousand? Getting that right, quickly, using only reasoning, is worth more in practice than it sounds — it is the sanity check that catches a wildly wrong number before it goes into a design, a budget, or a decision.

How It Works

A Fermi estimate begins by rewriting the unknown quantity as a product of factors, each one easier to guess than the original question. Call the quantity you want Q; you write it as Q ≈ f1 × f2 × … × fn, choosing factors you can each put a number on from general knowledge, a quick calculation, or a reasonable guess — rather than factors that would require you to already know the answer. For the piano-tuner question: Chicago’s population, the fraction of households owning a piano, how often each piano gets tuned per year, and how many tunings one full-time tuner can complete in a year. None of those four numbers is exact, and none of them needs to be — you only need each one to be in the right ballpark.

Because the factors can each span many powers of ten, Fermi estimates are almost always carried in scientific notation — a number written as a single leading digit (or one decimal place) times ten raised to a power, such as 3 × 106 for three million. Multiplying numbers written this way is mostly just adding the powers of ten: (3 × 106) × (2 × 103) = 6 × 109. That is why, underneath the multiplication, a Fermi estimate is really an addition: taking the base-ten logarithm of both sides turns the product f1 × f2 × … into the sum log10 f1 + log10 f2 + …, and the whole estimate becomes a running tally of how many powers of ten each factor contributes. This is the same logarithm whose job is to compress enormous ranges onto one line — the Richter scale for earthquakes and the decibel scale for sound both work for exactly this reason, and both are built the same way a Fermi estimate is: as a sum of log-scale contributions rather than a product of raw numbers.

Every factor also carries a unit, and the units have to cancel cleanly on the way to the answer — this bookkeeping is called dimensional analysis. If you are estimating gallons of gasoline burned per year and one of your factors is in miles per gallon, it had better multiply against a quantity in miles, not kilometers or minutes, or the units left over at the end will not be gallons at all. Writing every number down with its unit attached, and checking that the units you expect to cancel actually do, catches a large share of Fermi-estimate errors before you ever get to checking the arithmetic — a wrong unit produces an answer that is off by whatever conversion factor you dropped, often one or more entire powers of ten.

Each factor is rounded, deliberately, to about one significant digit and the nearest sensible power of ten — not because more precision is unavailable, but because more precision in any one factor is wasted effort when the other factors in the chain are themselves only guesses. Having multiplied everything out, the last step is a sanity check: does the answer’s order of magnitude make sense against anything else you already know? An estimate of Chicago’s piano tuners that comes out in the millions, or in the single digits, should send you back to look for a dropped factor of a thousand before you trust it.

Here is the part that makes the method work better than it has any right to. If you had to guess four or five independent factors and each one might be off by a factor of two or three in either direction, it would be reasonable to worry the errors all stack up — that being off by 2× on every factor leaves you off by 24 = 16× or worse on the final answer. In practice this almost never happens, because the errors are independent: there is no reason your guess at piano-ownership rates and your guess at tunings-per-tuner should both happen to be too high, or both too low, at the same time. Some of your errors overshoot, some undershoot, and they partly cancel in the product rather than compounding. The rule of thumb carried forward from this is that n independent errors, each a factor of about k, tend to leave the product off by roughly k raised to the square root of n, not k raised to the full n — four independent factor-of-two errors behave more like a single factor-of-four error than a factor-of-sixteen one. Day 16 derives exactly why a square root shows up here, when it covers how the same kind of averaging-out of independent errors sets the margin of error on an opinion poll.

Back to the story

Fermi was not measuring the bomb directly; nothing he was holding could do that. He was chaining a short, specific series of physical facts. The paper scraps were light enough that their fall was governed by the moving air itself rather than by their own weight — what physicist J. I. Katz, reconstructing the calculation in 2021, called their low “ballistic coefficient” — so their sideways drift traced the speed of the air in the blast wave at the moment it passed him. That air speed relates, through the physics of a shock wave, to the wave’s overpressure; overpressure at a known distance from an explosion relates, through blast scaling worked out well before Trinity on conventional high explosives, to the explosion’s energy. Each link is its own small, estimable quantity — a distance, a time, a known relationship from earlier tests — chained the same way the piano-tuner factors are chained. Fermi’s own memo does not spell out that middle step; by Katz’s account, no written record of it survives, only the observation and the number it produced. What the story asked you to explain is exactly this: a Fermi estimate does not require perfect data, just enough independently-guessable links, each roughly right, to land on the correct power of ten — and landing on 10,000 tons against a true value in the high teens to mid-twenties of thousands is squarely that kind of right. Part of the remaining gap may be physical, not just a reasoning shortfall: a nuclear explosion also sends real energy out as heat and light, which never moved the paper at all, so a method built entirely on the mechanical blast wave was always going to read a little low.

Key Concepts

Order of magnitude
The power of ten closest to a quantity’s size — the question “is it closer to ten, a hundred, or a thousand?” rather than its exact value.
Fermi estimate
An estimate built by decomposing an unknown quantity into a product of smaller, independently guessable factors, rather than trying to know or look up the answer directly.
Scientific notation
Writing a number as a single leading digit times ten raised to a power (3 × 106 for three million), which keeps very large or very small quantities readable and makes multiplication mostly a matter of adding exponents.
Unit bookkeeping (dimensional analysis)
Carrying every factor’s unit through the calculation and checking that the units left over at the end are the ones you actually wanted — a quick check that catches many Fermi-estimate errors before the arithmetic is even reviewed.
Energy density per kilogram
How much usable energy a fuel or battery stores for each kilogram it weighs — the single number that decides how heavy a given amount of stored energy has to be.
Factor-of-a-few accuracy
The standard a Fermi estimate is actually held to: landing within roughly two to five times the true value, not within a percentage point of it.

Example: Why an Electric Car’s Battery Weighs What It Does

Early in the car’s own story — at the physical-constraint link, well before anyone designs a vehicle, finances it, or regulates it — sits a Fermi question an engineer has to size up before going further: roughly how much heavier does a battery pack have to be than a tank of gasoline, to store the same usable energy? You do not need a chemist’s precision for a useful first pass; you need a Fermi estimate, built from a few factors anyone can look up or approximate.

1. Gasoline’s raw energy per kilogram. The EPA’s own conversion factor for a gallon of gasoline is 33.7 kWh (its basis for rating plug-in cars’ “MPGe”). A U.S. gallon of gasoline weighs about 2.8 kg, so gasoline holds roughly 33.7 ÷ 2.8 ≈ 12 kWh per kilogram.

2. A lithium-ion pack’s raw energy per kilogram. A mid-2020s electric-car battery pack — not a bare cell, which does better, but the full pack with its casing, cooling and wiring — stores roughly 0.15 to 0.20 kWh per kilogram.

3. The raw gap. Dividing, 12 ÷ 0.15–0.20 ≈ 60 to 80 times. Gasoline simply packs far more raw energy into each kilogram than a battery pack does — the fact every electric-car designer has to design around.

4. But raw energy is not useful energy. A gasoline engine wastes most of what it burns as heat; the Department of Energy puts a typical gasoline car’s overall tank-to-wheels efficiency at around 30% (older federal estimates range from about 12% in stop-and-go city driving up to 30%). An electric motor and battery system waste far less: fueleconomy.gov puts an EV at roughly 60–73% of its battery energy reaching the wheels before counting regenerative braking (the DOE figure counting regeneration is 87–91%; use the pre-regeneration number here, since gasoline’s 30% gets no braking credit either).

5. The useful-energy gap. Multiply each side by its own efficiency: gasoline’s useful energy per kilogram is roughly 12 × 0.20 ≈ 2.4 kWh/kg (20%, a round middle of the 12–30% range); the battery’s is roughly 0.175 × 0.65 ≈ 0.11 kWh/kg. The ratio lands at roughly twenty times — one clean order of magnitude smaller than the raw 60–80× gap, because the battery system’s efficiency advantage claws back a meaningful chunk of it.

Call it “about twenty times,” not 21.8× — every input above was itself a range, and the Fermi answer here is the range, not a false decimal. That factor of roughly twenty is the real number an electric-car engineer designs against: the reason an EV battery pack weighs many hundreds of kilograms to match what a gasoline tank does in a few dozen.

Equation / Model

Underneath every Fermi estimate’s multiplication is an addition, because of how exponents work.

Q ≈ f1 × f2 × … × fn   ⇒   log10Q = log10f1 + log10f2 + … + log10fn

Q is approximately equal to f sub 1 times f sub 2 times, and so on, times f sub n; therefore the base-ten logarithm of Q equals the sum of the base-ten logarithms of each factor.

Q
the quantity you actually want to estimate
f1, f2, …, fn
the chain of smaller, independently guessable factors whose product approximates Q
log10
the base-ten logarithm — the power you would raise 10 to, to get the number in question; log10(1,000) = 3

Writing an estimate as a sum of logarithms is what makes the method’s error-cancellation work the way it does: each factor’s error contributes its own small, independent term to that sum, and independent terms of mixed sign partly cancel rather than all pushing the total the same way. The resulting rule of thumb, carried forward from How It Works, is worth stating as its own working formula.

Typical error ≈ k√n   (rather than kn)

The typical error is approximately k to the power of the square root of n, rather than k to the power of n.

k
the typical factor-of-error on any one of the chain’s factors (for example, 2, meaning a given guess might be half or double the truth)
n
the number of independent factors chained together

This is a rule of thumb here, not a derivation — Day 16 derives the square root honestly, in the different-looking but mathematically related setting of why a poll of a few thousand people can estimate an entire country’s opinion to within a percentage point or two.

Why It Matters

A Fermi estimate is rarely the final answer to anything; it is the fast, cheap check that tells you whether a precise calculation, a vendor’s quote, or someone else’s confident number is even in the right neighborhood before you spend real effort on it. An engineer who can size the battery-weight gap above in two minutes, on the back of an envelope, is positioned to immediately spot a claimed pack weight that is off by a factor of five — long before a detailed spec sheet would catch the same error, and without needing one.

The habit matters as much as the arithmetic: breaking a hard, unanswerable-seeming question into a chain of smaller, answerable ones is a transferable move, not a trick specific to bombs or batteries. It is also the first appearance of a theme this course returns to again and again — that a complicated-looking system is very often a chain of simpler, individually tractable links, and that the discipline of pulling a problem apart along those links, one factor at a time, is worth more than any single fact you could memorize about any one of them.

Connections

Across. The Fermi-estimate chain you just used on a battery pack is, structurally, the same device Frank Drake built to estimate something far stranger: the number of communicating civilizations elsewhere in the galaxy. Preparing the agenda for a small meeting of radio astronomers and biologists at the Green Bank Observatory in West Virginia in November 1961, Drake wrote the galaxy’s count of detectable civilizations as a product of seven factors — the rate stars form, the fraction with planets, the fraction of those habitable, and on through the fraction of intelligent species that develop detectable technology and how long such a civilization keeps broadcasting. It is a Fermi chain in the exact sense of today’s lesson: break an unanswerable number into smaller, individually guessable pieces and multiply them. It is also where the method shows its own breaking point. Several of the Drake equation’s factors — especially the fraction of habitable planets where intelligent life actually arises, and how long a broadcasting civilization survives — are not just uncertain by a factor of two or three, the comfortable range this lesson’s error-cancellation argument covers, but by many factors of ten in each direction, so independently honest estimates of the final product can span ten or more orders of magnitude. The equation was built, by Drake’s own account, to organize a conversation rather than to produce a trustworthy number, and that is exactly the outcome its wildly uncertain factors still produce today.

Remember These
  • A Fermi estimate breaks an unanswerable question into a chain of smaller, independently guessable factors and multiplies them.
  • Carry units through every step; units that don’t cancel to the answer you expect are the fastest way to catch a dropped factor of ten.
  • Round each factor to about one significant digit — more precision in one link is wasted when the others are themselves only guesses.
  • Independent errors partly cancel: n factors each off by about k tend to leave the product off by roughly k√n, not kn.
  • The target is the right power of ten, not a decimal point — “about twenty times,” not “21.8 times.”
Apply It

A narrow-body airliner weighing about 75 tonnes at takeoff burns roughly 5 tonnes of jet fuel (energy-per-kilogram close enough to gasoline’s to use the same figure) on a two-hour flight: even granting batteries the car example’s roughly twenty-times useful-energy gap — generous, since a jet engine converts fuel to thrust more efficiently than a car engine converts it to motion at the wheels — could a battery storing the same useful energy fit within that airframe’s takeoff weight?
