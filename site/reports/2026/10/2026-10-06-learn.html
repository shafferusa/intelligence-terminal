The Number Mathematicians Mocked for Three Centuries, Now Wired Into Every Circuit

Tuesday, October 6, 2026 · 14 min read

Born as a bookkeeping trick for rescuing real answers from a 16th-century cubic equation, the number i spent three centuries as an insult before Euler’s identity revealed what it was really doing — tying the circle to the exponential — and engineers wired it into every circuit that runs on alternating current.

Mathematics

Day 7 of 300 · Mathematics 7 of 20 · Complex numbers: extending the number line into a plane

The Big Idea

Every real number, positive or negative, squares to something that is never negative: 3² = 9, and (−3)² = 9 as well. That one fact means no real number can ever be the square root of a negative number — there is simply no real x with x² = −1. For most of mathematical history, that looked like the end of the story: an equation that called for the square root of a negative number had no answer, full stop, and that was that.

The story didn’t end there, because a very practical 16th-century problem — solving cubic equations — kept producing square roots of negative numbers in the middle of a calculation, even when the final answer was already known to be an ordinary real number. Rather than abandon the method, one mathematician pushed the arithmetic through anyway, treating the square root of −1 as if it were a legitimate quantity. The negative square roots cancelled out on their own, leaving the correct real answer standing. That is the entire origin of what are now called complex numbers: not a new way of counting things, but a bookkeeping device that kept working, long before anyone could explain why it was allowed to.

Today’s lesson builds the whole picture from that one trick: the imaginary unit i, the complex numbers it builds, the two-dimensional complex plane where they finally found a believable home two and a half centuries later, and one equation — Euler’s identity — that ties i directly back to Day 5’s circle and Day 6’s exponential, closing a loop this curriculum has been quietly setting up all week.

How It Works

In 1545 the Italian physician and mathematician Gerolamo Cardano published Ars Magna (“The Great Art”), which contains, among other things, a general method for solving cubic equations — equations whose highest power is x³ — algebraically. Applying his own method to a problem as ordinary as “find two numbers that add to 10 and multiply to 40” forced him to write down 5 + √−15 and 5 − √−15. He could check, by direct arithmetic, that the two expressions really do add to 10 and multiply to 40 — (5+√−15)(5−√−15) = 25−(−15) = 40 — but he had no idea what a square root of a negative number was supposed to mean. He wrote the whole exercise off in a single dismissive line — it was, he said, “as subtle as it is useless” — unwilling to build anything further on a quantity he considered fictitious.

The breakthrough came a generation later from the Italian engineer Rafael Bombelli, in his 1572 book L’Algebra. Bombelli studied the cubic equation x³ = 15x + 4, which has an obvious, checkable real solution: x = 4, since 4³ = 64 and 15 × 4 + 4 = 64. But Cardano’s own cubic formula, applied to this exact equation, produces x = ∛(2+√−121) + ∛(2−√−121) — an answer dripping with square roots of negative numbers, for a problem whose answer everyone already knew was the whole number 4. Rather than throw the formula out, Bombelli invented formal rules for adding, subtracting and multiplying these expressions — he called √−1 “piu di meno” (“plus of minus”) — and carried the algebra through to the end. The negative square roots cancelled each other out exactly, and the formula collapsed down to the real answer, 4, right on schedule. Bombelli had shown something Cardano never saw: these “impossible” numbers were not nonsense. They were a necessary bridge partway through a calculation whose final answer was perfectly real.

That demonstration didn’t earn these numbers any respect for another 65 years. In 1637, the French mathematician and philosopher René Descartes, in his La Géométrie, gave them the name that stuck — calling them imaginary, a deliberate put-down meant to say that these roots were not really there, that an equation calling for one simply had no genuine solution a geometer could construct. The insult outlived the objection: mathematicians eventually accepted imaginary numbers as entirely legitimate but kept Descartes’s dismissive name for them anyway.

Strip away three centuries of argument and the definition is a single new rule. Leonhard Euler gave this quantity the letter it still carries — i, for “imaginary” — in a paper presented in 1777 and published in 1794, writing that he would “denote the formula √−1 by the letter i.” Define the imaginary unit, written i, by declaring that i² = −1. Every other square root of a negative number reduces to a real multiple of i: √−15 = √15 × √−1 = i√15; √−121 = 11i, resolving Bombelli’s cube roots above. A complex number is any expression of the form a + bi, where a and b are ordinary real numbers: a is the real part, b is the imaginary part. Real numbers are not replaced by this — they are simply the special case where b = 0; a purely imaginary number like 3i is the opposite special case, where a = 0.

Complex numbers add and multiply the way any two-term algebraic expression would, with one extra rule folded in: wherever i² appears, replace it with −1. Adding combines the real and imaginary parts separately: (3+2i)+(1−5i) = 4−3i. Multiplying uses the ordinary distributive law and then applies that one substitution: (3+2i)(1−5i) = 3 − 15i + 2i − 10i² = 3 − 13i − 10(−1) = 13 − 13i. Every rule of ordinary algebra carries over unchanged; the only new ingredient is a single fact about what i² equals.

A complex number a + bi is completely described by a pair of real numbers, (a, b) — and a pair of real numbers is exactly what you need to plot a point on a plane, not a line. That realization is what finally gave these numbers a picture anyone could believe in. Draw a horizontal axis for the real part and a vertical axis for the imaginary part, and a+bi becomes nothing more exotic than the point at (a, b) — or, equivalently, an arrow from the origin out to that point. The Danish-Norwegian surveyor Caspar Wessel presented this picture first, to the Royal Danish Academy of Sciences in 1797 (published 1799); the French bookkeeper Jean-Robert Argand rediscovered it independently and published it in 1806, and the complex plane still carries his name in some textbooks (the “Argand diagram”); and Carl Friedrich Gauss worked out the same geometric picture and, writing it up formally in 1831, gave these numbers the name they go by now — “complex numbers,” rather than merely “imaginary” ones. Once a+bi could be seen as a point in a plane instead of an affront to the number line, centuries of suspicion dissolved almost immediately: two-dimensional numbers turned out to be exactly as legitimate as one-dimensional ones, just harder to picture at first.

The complex plane does more than give these numbers a home; it reveals what multiplying by i actually does, which turns out to be the real key to today’s equation. Start at the point 1 (on the real axis) and multiply by i, repeatedly: 1 × i = i (straight up, on the imaginary axis); i × i = i² = −1 (straight left); −1 × i = −i (straight down); −i × i = −i² = 1 (back to the start). Four multiplications by i, and you land back exactly where you began — and at every step, the point has moved exactly a quarter-turn counterclockwise around the origin. Multiplying by i is not a way of making a number bigger or smaller, the way multiplying a real number by 2 or by 0.5 does; on the complex plane, it is nothing but a 90° rotation. That single fact is the bridge from the plane back to Day 5’s circle — and from there, to today’s equation.

Key Concepts

Imaginary unit (i)
Defined by i² = −1. Every square root of a negative real number reduces to a real multiple of i.
Complex number
Any number of the form a + bi, where a (the real part) and b (the imaginary part) are ordinary real numbers. Real numbers are the special case b = 0.
The complex plane
The two-dimensional plane whose horizontal axis plots a complex number's real part and vertical axis plots its imaginary part, turning a+bi into the point (a, b).
Euler's identity
eiπ + 1 = 0 — the equation tying together e, i, π, 1 and 0, and a single special case of the broader fact that exponentiating by an imaginary amount produces rotation.

Example / Application

These two-dimensional numbers found their first serious engineering job almost 300 years after Bombelli, in the electrical industry built around alternating current. A circuit carrying AC doesn’t just resist current the plain way a resistor does (Ohm’s law, V = IR); components like inductors and capacitors store and release energy every cycle, which shifts the timing of the current wave relative to the voltage wave — an effect with no equivalent in a battery-and-resistor circuit. Before complex numbers were brought to bear, correctly describing a whole AC circuit meant solving a genuine differential equation for every circuit, a serious undertaking for 19th-century engineers designing the first power grids by hand. The self-taught English physicist Oliver Heaviside first folded a complex-number shortcut into this problem in 1886, and the German-American engineer Charles Proteus Steinmetz, in a landmark 1893 paper, systematized it into something any working engineer could use without touching calculus at all.

Steinmetz’s trick: represent a circuit’s total opposition to AC current — its impedance — as a single complex number Z = R + jX, where R is the resistance (the part that actually dissipates energy as heat) and X is the reactance (the part contributed by inductors and capacitors, which store and return energy rather than dissipating it). Electrical engineers write j instead of i for exactly one reason: i was already taken, for current, so Steinmetz’s own convention swapped the letter rather than the concept. With impedance written this way, Ohm’s law works again exactly as it does for plain resistors — V = IZ — except now V, I and Z are all complex numbers, and the “multiplying by i rotates 90°” fact from above is doing real engineering work: it is exactly what encodes the timing shift between the current and voltage waves.

Reading a circuit's impedance. Consider a circuit with a 300-ohm resistor and an inductor contributing 400 ohms of reactance at the frequency in use, connected in series. The circuit's impedance is the complex number:

Z = 300 + 400j Ω

The circuit's overall opposition to current — the single number you'd compare to a plain resistor — is this complex number's magnitude, found the same way you'd find the length of any two-dimensional arrow, with the Pythagorean theorem from Day 4:

|Z| = √(300² + 400²) = √(90,000 + 160,000) = √250,000 = 500 Ω

The timing shift between the current and the voltage — how far the current's peak lags behind the voltage's peak — is the angle whose tangent is 400/300, from Day 5's tangent ratio: arctan(4/3) ≈ 53.13°. A plain resistor alone would report only the 500 Ω figure and say nothing at all about that 53° lag — the entire point of writing impedance as a complex number instead of a single real one.

Impedance is the clearest everyday case, but it's one instance of a much more general habit: wherever a system naturally combines a “size” with a “timing” or “direction,” physicists and engineers now reach for a complex number by default — describing a radio signal or a quantum particle's wavefunction leans on exactly the same two-dimensional arithmetic Bombelli stumbled into while chasing a real answer to a cubic equation.

Equation / Model

Euler’s identity is often called mathematics’ single most beautiful equation, because it ties together five fundamental constants — e, i, π, 1 and 0 — using nothing but addition, multiplication and exponentiation:

eiπ + 1 = 0

e to the i pi, plus one, equals zero.

e
Euler's number, about 2.71828 — the base of continuous growth, from Day 6
i
the imaginary unit, defined by i² = −1
π
pi, about 3.14159 — the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter
1
the multiplicative identity: the number that leaves any number unchanged under multiplication
0
the additive identity: the number that leaves any number unchanged under addition

That equation looks like a magic trick until you see where it comes from, and the source is a far more general fact connecting exponentials to rotation — the one piece of real derivation worth doing in full:

eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ

e to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta.

Day 6 defined ex through continuous growth: multiply by a fixed factor over and over, infinitely finely, and the result either grows or shrinks, depending on the sign of the growth rate. But raising e to an imaginary power can’t be growth or shrinkage at all, because, as the rotation fact above showed, multiplying by an imaginary number never scales a point up or down — it only turns it. So eiθ has to trace a path that never grows or shrinks either: a path that stays exactly distance 1 from the origin, always. The one kind of motion that stays a fixed distance from a point while continuously moving is motion around a circle — and the point on a circle of radius 1, swept out to angle θ, is precisely cos θ and sin θ, from Day 5’s unit circle. That argument is the intuitive heart of Euler’s formula, not a complete proof — a fully rigorous derivation needs calculus tools this curriculum hasn’t built yet — but it is exactly why the formula has to look the way it does, not an accident of algebra: raising e to an imaginary power doesn’t produce a bigger or smaller number; it produces a rotation.

Euler’s identity itself is nothing more than this general formula evaluated at one specific angle: a half-turn, θ = π radians (180°). At that angle, the point on the unit circle sits at the far left, straight across from the start: cos π = −1 and sin π = 0, from Day 5’s exact triangle values. Substitute directly into the general formula:

eiπ = cos π + i sin π = −1 + i(0) = −1

e to the i pi equals cosine pi plus i sine pi, equals negative one plus i times zero, equals negative one.

Add 1 to both sides, and the five constants fall into place on their own: eiπ + 1 = 0. Nothing was forced to make that happen — it is the unavoidable consequence of what a half-turn around a circle of radius 1 looks like, once exponentiation itself has been allowed to turn rather than only grow.

Why It Matters

Beyond being decorative, complex numbers close a real gap the real numbers alone could not close. Some perfectly ordinary polynomial equations — x² + 1 = 0 is the simplest one — have no real solution at all, because no real number squares to −1. Allow complex numbers, and that gap closes completely: every polynomial equation, of every degree, has exactly as many roots as its degree, counting repeated roots separately, once you are willing to count complex ones — a result now called the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. Carl Friedrich Gauss offered the first widely accepted proof, in his 1799 doctoral dissertation (later mathematicians found and patched a genuine gap in his argument, but the result itself has stood ever since). Real numbers, in other words, were never quite a complete number system for algebra; complex numbers are — nothing bigger is ever needed to solve a polynomial equation.

Today’s lesson is also the moment two separate threads from this curriculum’s first week turn out to be the same thread. Day 5 built sine and cosine as coordinates on a circle; Day 6 built e as the base of continuous growth. On their own, nothing connected a circle to an exponential function — rotation and growth look like opposite ideas. Euler’s formula is the single equation that says they were never separate: exponentiating by an imaginary amount simply is rotation, and the “growth” picture and the “circle” picture turn out to be two views of the exact same operation — one for real exponents, one for imaginary ones.

The habit worth carrying forward is treating “two real numbers bundled together” as its own legitimate kind of number, rather than an awkward pair that needs explaining away. That habit is what makes complex numbers transportable to problem after problem that has nothing obviously to do with √−1 at all — a circuit’s impedance, a wave’s phase, a quantum amplitude — anywhere a quantity naturally carries both a size and an angle.

Remember These
  • i is defined by i² = −1; every square root of a negative real number reduces to a real multiple of i.
  • A complex number a+bi combines a real part a and an imaginary part b; on the complex plane it is simply the point (a, b) — two real numbers paired together, nothing stranger.
  • Multiplying by i rotates a point 90° counterclockwise around the origin; four multiplications by i return you exactly to where you started.
  • Euler's formula, eiθ = cos θ + isin θ, says raising e to an imaginary power produces rotation rather than growth; Euler's identity, eiπ+1=0, is that formula at one angle: a half-turn.
  • Allowing complex numbers closes a real gap in algebra: every polynomial equation has as many roots as its degree, once you count complex ones (the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra).
Think About It

You showed that multiplying by i rotates a point 90° counterclockwise, and that four such rotations (360°) bring you back to exactly where you started, so i⁴ = 1. Using that rotation picture alone — not a formula — can you work out what i raised to the 100th power must equal? And what does the same reasoning tell you about why the powers of i can only ever cycle through four values (1, i, −1, −i), forever, no matter how large the exponent gets?
