Why Speed Alone Can’t Tell You Where You’re Going

Wednesday, October 14, 2026 · 13 min read

A number by itself can only say how much of something there is; it takes a second piece of information, direction, to say where it’s going — the one idea behind an airplane correcting for crosswind, a cable holding up a bridge, and every arrow on a weather map.

Mathematics

Day 13 of 300 · Mathematics 13 of 20 · Vectors: quantities with direction

The Big Idea

Your car’s speedometer reads 60. That number is complete in itself — it tells you exactly how fast you are going, and nothing more is needed to make the statement true or false. Quantities like that, fully described by a single number and a unit, are called scalars: mass, time, temperature, distance, energy, speed. Now ask a different question — not how fast, but which way — and the speedometer has nothing to say. Answering it needs a second car driving 60 the opposite direction, or a compass heading, or an arrow on a map. A quantity that needs both a size and a direction before it means anything at all is called a vector: velocity, force, displacement, acceleration, and momentum all work this way.

The distinction is not academic hair-splitting; it changes what the numbers actually tell you. Two cars each going 60 miles an hour toward each other are about to collide; two cars each going 60 miles an hour away from each other are getting safer by the second — same speed, opposite velocity, and the difference is everything. Walk once around your block and come back to your front door: you have covered real distance, a scalar that just keeps adding up step by step, yet your net displacement — how far you ended up from where you started, and in which direction — is exactly zero. Two ropes pulling on the same hook with equal force, directly opposite each other, hold it perfectly still; the same two forces pulling at right angles instead drag the hook off at an angle neither rope points in alone. The size of a push or a pull is never the whole story; which way it points decides what actually happens.

The picture to carry forward is an arrow: its length stands for the vector’s magnitude (how much), and the way it points stands for its direction. Two arrows count as the exact same vector if they have the same length and point the same way, no matter where on the page you happen to draw them — a vector describes a size-and-direction, not a fixed location, which is exactly what lets the next section’s addition rule work at all.

How It Works

Two ways of writing a vector down turn out to matter, and they describe the same thing from different angles. The first is geometric: draw it as an arrow, give its length (the magnitude) and the angle it makes with some reference direction. The second is algebraic: break the arrow down into how far it reaches along each axis of a coordinate grid — its components. A vector pointing 3 units east and 4 units north is written (3, 4); the first number is its east–west component, the second its north–south component. Day 5’s trigonometry is exactly the tool that converts between the two pictures: a vector of magnitude r at angle θ from the east axis has components (r cos θ, r sin θ), and the reverse conversion runs through the Pythagorean theorem and the arctangent.

Adding vectors is where the two pictures earn their keep, because each gives you a different way to do the identical calculation. The geometric method is called head-to-tail: draw the first vector, then draw the second starting from the tip (head) of the first rather than from the origin, and the resultant — the sum — is the single arrow running from the very start of the first vector to the very end of the second. This is exactly how combined motion behaves in the physical world: if you swim at a steady speed and direction relative to the water, while a current carries the water itself in some other direction, your actual path over the riverbed is the head-to-tail sum of the two. The algebraic method, component-wise addition, says the same thing without needing a single drawing: add the two vectors’ east–west components to get the resultant’s east–west component, and separately add their north–south components to get its north–south component. The two methods always agree, because the head-to-tail picture and the component grid are two descriptions of the identical arrow; component addition is simply the version you can compute with a calculator instead of a ruler and protractor, and it is the version used almost everywhere vectors actually get calculated, because it generalizes cleanly to three dimensions and beyond, where a careful drawing stops being practical.

One more operation rounds out the toolkit: multiplying a vector by an ordinary number (a scalar). Multiplying by a positive number stretches or shrinks the arrow without turning it — double a vector and you get an arrow twice as long, pointing the identical way. Multiplying by a negative number does that and also flips the direction a full 180 degrees; multiplying a vector by −1 produces the vector of the exact same length pointing exactly the opposite way, which is also how vector subtraction works: A − B is just A plus the reverse of B. A vector of length exactly 1, pointing in some direction, is called a unit vector; it carries pure direction with none of the scale, and any vector at all can be rebuilt by multiplying its own length by its own unit vector — a way of splitting “how much” apart from “which way” completely cleanly.

Everything so far used two components, east and north, because a flat map only needs two axes to pin down a point. Nothing about the idea stops there: add a third component, straight up, and the identical head-to-tail and component-wise rules describe a vector in three-dimensional space exactly as well as in two, with the resultant’s third component found by adding the two inputs’ third components separately, and its length found by a direct extension of the same Pythagorean reasoning — summing the squares of all three components instead of two before taking the square root. A GPS receiver lives in exactly this three-component world: your position is a single vector with an east, a north, and an up part, and your velocity is another three-component vector describing how fast, and in which of those three directions, you are currently moving. None of the underlying algebra changes; there is simply one more number to carry along through every step of it.

The arrow-and-components picture used throughout this lesson is itself the product of a fairly late argument. In 1843, the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton introduced quaternions, a four-part number system built specifically to handle three-dimensional rotation and direction, and spent much of the rest of his career convinced it was the right language for all of physics. It took until the 1880s — working independently of each other, after Hamilton’s death — for the American physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs and the British physicist Oliver Heaviside to strip Hamilton’s four-part system down to the simpler three-component vectors taught here, arguing that most of the physics people actually wanted (forces, velocities, fields) only ever needed the three-component piece and none of the baggage quaternions carried alongside it. Their stripped-down version won out slowly, after decades of genuine argument among mathematicians and physicists about which system was really more useful, with historians of mathematics generally dating its full acceptance to around 1910. The notation in your hands today is the side that won.

Key Concepts

Scalar
A quantity fully described by a single number and a unit — mass, time, temperature, speed. Has a size, no direction.
Vector
A quantity that needs both a magnitude (size) and a direction to be fully specified — velocity, force, displacement, acceleration.
Magnitude and direction
The two pieces of information every vector carries: how much (its length, as an arrow) and which way (the angle it points).
Components
A vector rewritten as how far it reaches along each axis of a coordinate grid (e.g. its east–west and north–south parts) — the algebraic alternative to drawing an arrow.
Resultant (vector addition)
The single vector produced by adding two or more vectors together, found either by joining them head-to-tail or by adding their components separately.
Unit vector
A vector of length exactly 1, used to represent pure direction with the size stripped away; any vector equals its own length times its own unit vector.

Example / Application

Pilots solve a vector-addition problem, by name, on every flight with any crosswind at all. An aircraft moves through the air at its airspeed, in whatever direction the nose points; but the air itself is usually moving too, as wind, relative to the ground. The plane’s actual path over the ground — its track — is the head-to-tail sum of those two vectors: airspeed plus wind. Point the nose exactly where you want to end up and a crosswind will carry you steadily off that line; the fix pilots use, called the wind correction angle, is to aim the nose slightly into the wind so the sideways push cancels out and the actual track comes out straight.

A pilot wants to fly a track of due north. True airspeed is 150 knots. The wind is blowing from the east (090°) at 30 knots — a pure 90-degree crosswind, pushing the aircraft toward the west. What heading should the pilot fly, and how fast will the plane actually cover ground?

Set up the components. Put north and east as the two axes. The wind vector has an east–west component of −30 knots (pushing west) and a north–south component of 0 (it is a pure crosswind, blowing squarely across the intended track). For the ground track to come out due north, the airspeed vector’s east–west component must exactly cancel the wind’s: it needs a +30-knot (eastward) component of its own.

Find the heading (the wind correction angle). The airspeed vector has length 150 and needs an eastward component of 30, so the angle it must lean away from due north is θ = asin(30/150) = asin(0.2) ≈ 11.5°. Since the push is westward, the correction leans the nose eastward to counter it: fly a heading of roughly 011° — about 11.5° east of north — not the 000° you might first assume.

Find the actual ground speed. With the sideways components now cancelling exactly, what’s left is the airspeed vector’s northward component, found with the Pythagorean theorem: ground speed = √(150² − 30²) = √(22,500 − 900) = √21,600 ≈ 147 knots, a little slower over the ground than the 150-knot airspeed, because part of the plane’s effort is now spent fighting the crosswind sideways rather than moving north.

The identical head-to-tail logic governs forces pulling on a single point from different directions, not just motion through air. Picture two tow trucks hooked to the same stuck vehicle: one crew pulls due north with 400 pounds of force; a second crew, positioned off to the side, pulls due east with 300 pounds.

Added as vectors rather than as plain numbers, the two forces combine into a single resultant force. Its magnitude, by the Pythagorean theorem: √(400² + 300²) = √(160,000 + 90,000) = √250,000 = 500 pounds. Its direction, by the arctangent of the two components: arctan(300/400) ≈ 36.9° east of north.

The vehicle does not move twice — once north, once east. It moves once, along that single diagonal resultant, at a size and bearing neither crew’s own rope alone would produce. It is the same two-step reasoning, components then the Pythagorean theorem, that the wind-triangle example used a moment ago, applied to forces instead of velocities.

Nothing in either calculation is special to aviation or towing. The identical reasoning — two vectors, head-to-tail or component by component, producing one resultant — is how a ship’s navigator corrects for ocean current, how a weather map’s wind-direction arrows combine with a storm’s own forward motion to predict where it will actually go next, and how a structural engineer checks a bridge or a crane: at every stable joint, every incoming force vector has to sum to exactly zero, with nothing left over to accelerate anything.

Equation / Model

Component-wise vector addition, the version almost all real calculation actually uses, says this: to add two vectors, add their matching components separately.

Rx = Ax + Bx    Ry = Ay + By

R sub x equals A sub x plus B sub x; R sub y equals A sub y plus B sub y.

A, B
the two vectors being added
Ax, Ay
vector A’s east–west and north–south components
Bx, By
vector B’s east–west and north–south components
R
the resultant: the single vector equal to A + B
Rx, Ry
the resultant’s own components, each simply the sum of the matching components of A and B

Once the resultant’s components are known, converting back to the magnitude-and-angle picture takes exactly the tools from How It Works: the resultant’s length comes from the Pythagorean theorem applied to its own components, treating them as the two legs of a right triangle.

|R| = √(Rx² + Ry²)

The magnitude of R equals the square root of R sub x squared plus R sub y squared.

|R|
the magnitude (length) of the resultant vector — a single non-negative number

That last step is exactly the calculation the worked wind-triangle example ran twice over — once to pick the heading that zeroes out the crosswind component, and once more to find the ground speed left over once it was cancelled.

Why It Matters

Scalars are enough for a great deal of ordinary measurement, but the moment a quantity has to describe motion, force, or anything else that happens somewhere and points somewhere, a single number stops being able to carry the whole idea. Vectors are the fix, and once you’re used to reading the world that way — asking not just “how much” but “which way, too” — a great deal of physics, engineering, navigation, and even a video game character’s on-screen motion turns out to be the identical head-to-tail or component-wise addition rule from today, applied over and over to different quantities.

It also sets up the next twist directly. Everything here involved one vector or two at a time, each a short list of numbers. Day 14 extends the idea to a matrix — a whole rectangular grid of numbers that acts on a vector all at once, rotating, scaling, or stretching it into a new vector in a single operation, the way a flight simulator rotates an entire 3D scene around a moving aircraft or a search engine ranks an entire web of pages simultaneously. None of that machinery means anything without first having a solid, computable idea of what a vector actually is — which is exactly what today built.

Remember These
  • A scalar is fully described by a single number and a unit; a vector needs a magnitude and a direction before it means anything.
  • Two vectors add either head-to-tail (geometrically, as arrows) or component by component (algebraically) — the two methods always produce the identical resultant.
  • Multiplying a vector by a positive number only changes its length; multiplying by a negative number also reverses its direction.
  • Displacement (how far, and which way, you ended up) is a vector; distance traveled is a scalar — walking a full loop gives real distance but zero displacement.
  • The component method — breaking a vector into its parts along each axis using trigonometry — is how virtually every real vector calculation, from flight navigation to physics, actually gets done.
Think About It

Suppose the same pilot wants to fly due north again, but today the wind is blowing from the west instead of the east, at the same 30 knots, with the same 150-knot airspeed. Using the same reasoning as the worked example — cancelling the sideways push with a component of the airspeed vector — should the compass heading now point east of north, west of north, or still exactly north, and why?
