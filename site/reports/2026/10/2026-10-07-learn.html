The Ancient Paradox That Taught Mathematicians How Infinity Adds Up

Wednesday, October 7, 2026 · 13 min read

Zeno argued that a runner can never overtake a tortoise with a head start, because closing the gap takes infinitely many steps — and the geometric series that resolves his paradox, the same tool Archimedes used to measure the area under a parabola two centuries later, shows that infinitely many steps can still add up to a perfectly ordinary, finite distance.

The Big Idea

Imagine Achilles, the fastest runner in the Greek army, racing a tortoise that gets a head start. Common sense says Achilles catches and passes the tortoise almost immediately. The fifth-century BCE philosopher Zeno of Elea argued the opposite: that Achilles can never catch the tortoise at all. His reasoning ran like this. Before Achilles can overtake the tortoise, he must first reach the point where the tortoise started. But by the time he gets there, the tortoise — however slowly — has crawled a little further ahead. So Achilles must next reach that new point. But by the time he arrives, the tortoise has again moved a little further. This repeats forever: every time Achilles reaches where the tortoise just was, the tortoise is already somewhere new. Zeno’s conclusion was that catching the tortoise requires completing infinitely many of these steps, and since, he argued, you cannot complete an infinite number of anything, Achilles can never actually catch up. Motion itself, on this argument, is a kind of logical embarrassment: provably impossible, yet observably happening every day.

Zeno’s argument survives almost unchanged in Aristotle’s Physics, written roughly a century later — which is, in fact, the only reason anyone knows about it at all, since none of Zeno’s own writing has survived. Aristotle didn’t fully solve it (his own answer, that the later subdivisions exist only “potentially,” satisfied few later readers), and the puzzle sat as a live irritant for roughly two thousand years. What finally dissolves it is not philosophy but arithmetic: the realization that adding together infinitely many numbers does not automatically produce an infinite total. If the numbers you’re adding shrink fast enough, their endless sum can settle on an entirely ordinary, finite number. That one fact — that infinity, handled correctly, can add up to something finite — is the subject of today’s lesson, and it is the tool that finally lets Achilles win his race.

Getting there takes two new pieces of vocabulary, sequence and series, and one sharp distinction between the two basic ways a list of numbers can grow or shrink: sequences that add the same amount every step, and sequences that multiply by the same amount every step. Only one of those two behaves well enough, when summed forever, to rescue Achilles — and the very same trick that rescues him is, essentially unchanged, the tool Archimedes used roughly two centuries later to calculate the exact area enclosed by a parabola, without any of the calculus that wouldn’t exist for another nineteen hundred years.

How It Works

A sequence is simply an ordered list of numbers, one after another, each one called a term. Write the terms as a1, a2, a3 and so on — a1 is the first term, a2 the second, and an stands for “whichever term comes n-th.” A series is what you get when you add a sequence’s terms together: a1 + a2 + a3 + … Two families of sequences come up constantly, and today’s lesson needs both, because they behave completely differently once you try to sum them forever.

An arithmetic sequence adds the same fixed amount — the common difference, d — to get from one term to the next: 2, 5, 8, 11, 14, … adds d = 3 each time. The n-th term is an = a1 + (n−1)d. Summing n terms of an arithmetic sequence has a shortcut worth knowing on its own: pair the first term with the last, the second with the second-to-last, and so on; each pair adds to the same total (a1 + an), and there are n/2 such pairs, so the sum of the first n terms is Sn = n(a1 + an)/2. A well-known school anecdote has the ten-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss discovering this pairing trick on the spot around 1787, summing 1 through 100 almost instantly while his classmates added the long way; the story has been retold so many times, with so many conflicting details across versions, that historians treat its specifics as embellished rather than verified — but the pairing trick it illustrates is real, and still the fastest way to sum an arithmetic series by hand. On a smaller example you can check yourself: 1 + 2 + … + 10 has 10 terms, each pair (1 + 10, 2 + 9, and so on) adds to 11, and there are 10/2 = 5 such pairs, so the total is 5 × 11 = 55 — no need to add ten numbers one at a time. Either way, summing an arithmetic sequence forever is hopeless: because the terms never shrink — the same difference is added again and again — the running total only ever grows without bound. 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … has no finite sum, and neither does any other infinite arithmetic series except the trivial case where every term is zero.

A geometric sequence instead multiplies by the same fixed amount — the common ratio, r — to get from one term to the next: 3, 6, 12, 24, … multiplies by r = 2 each time; 1, ½, ¼, ⅛, … multiplies by r = ½ each time. The n-th term is an = a1rn−1. This is the family that matters for Zeno, because unlike an arithmetic sequence, a geometric sequence can shrink toward zero — and when it does, summing it forever stops being hopeless.

To see why, look at the running total as you add more and more terms — mathematicians call this the partial sum, Sn: the sum of just the first n terms, stopping there. A series is said to converge if, as n keeps growing, these partial sums settle down and approach one fixed number ever more closely, rather than wandering off toward infinity or never settling at all (which is called diverging). For a geometric series, whether it converges comes down entirely to the size of r: if the ratio’s size is less than 1 (written |r| < 1), each new term is smaller than the last by the same proportion every time, and the partial sums creep toward a fixed ceiling they never quite cross. If |r| is 1 or larger, each term stays the same size or grows, and the partial sums never settle — the series diverges.

Now apply exactly this to Zeno. Take the simplest version of his argument, often called the Dichotomy: before you can cross a room, it seems you must first cross half of it. Before you can cross the remaining half, you must cross half of that remainder — a quarter of the original distance. Before crossing that remaining quarter, you must cross half of it — an eighth. And so on, forever: ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16, … — a geometric sequence with first term a1 = ½ and ratio r = ½. Zeno is entirely right that this produces infinitely many steps. But add them up: ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + 1/16 + … Each partial sum is 1 minus whatever fraction is still left to cross: after one step you’ve covered ½ = 1 − ½; after two, ¾ = 1 − ¼; after three, ⅞ = 1 − ⅛ — and that missing piece shrinks toward zero forever without ever quite reaching it. The infinite sum is exactly 1: the whole room, no more and no less. Infinitely many steps, each one genuinely completed, adding up to a perfectly ordinary finite distance. Zeno’s hidden assumption — that infinitely many steps must take an infinite distance or an infinite time — is simply false, once you know how to add a shrinking geometric sequence.

Key Concepts

Sequence
An ordered list of numbers, each one called a term (written a1, a2, a3, …).
Series
The sum produced by adding a sequence’s terms together, one after another.
Arithmetic vs. geometric
An arithmetic sequence adds the same fixed amount each step and never shrinks; a geometric sequence multiplies by the same fixed ratio each step, and shrinks toward zero when the ratio's size is less than 1.
Convergence
What a series does when its partial sums settle on one fixed number as more terms are added, rather than growing without bound or never settling (diverging).
Zeno’s paradox
The ancient argument that motion is impossible because covering any distance seems to require completing infinitely many smaller steps first; resolved once those steps are recognized as a convergent geometric series.

Example / Application

Zeno’s original version of the paradox — Achilles and the tortoise — uses exactly the same resolution, but with real speeds attached, so the geometric series plays out in actual seconds rather than fractions of a room.

Achilles catches the tortoise. Give the tortoise a 100-meter head start. Achilles runs at 10 meters per second; the tortoise crawls at 1 meter per second.

Step 1: Achilles needs 100/10 = 10 seconds to reach the tortoise’s starting point. In those 10 seconds the tortoise crawls 1 × 10 = 10 more meters, to the 110-meter mark.

Step 2: Achilles needs another 10/10 = 1 second to reach that new 110-meter mark. In that 1 second the tortoise crawls 1 more meter, to 111.

Step 3: Achilles needs 1/10 = 0.1 second to reach 111. The tortoise crawls 0.1 more meters, to 111.1 — and the pattern continues.

Every step takes exactly one-tenth the time of the step before it: 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, … — a geometric sequence with a1 = 10 seconds and r = 1/10. Summed forever:

Total time = 10/(1 − 1/10) = 10/0.9 = 11.111… seconds = 100/9 seconds.

Check this against plain algebra, with no series at all: Achilles closes the 100-meter gap at a relative speed of 10 − 1 = 9 meters per second, so the catch-up time is 100/9 = 11.111… seconds — exactly what the infinite geometric series gave. Zeno’s infinitely many steps are completely real; they simply all finish inside an ordinary 11.11-second span. After that instant, Achilles isn’t just even with the tortoise — he’s ahead of it.

This is not merely a cute fix for a Greek puzzle. Roughly two centuries after Zeno, Archimedes used the identical idea — summing a sequence of shrinking geometric pieces — to solve a genuine open problem: finding the exact area enclosed by a parabola and a straight line cutting across it. His method, laid out in Quadrature of the Parabola (3rd century BCE), filled the parabolic region with a sequence of triangles, where each new layer contributed exactly a quarter of the area the previous layer had contributed — a geometric sequence with ratio r = ¼. If that first, largest triangle has area 1, the running total after each added layer is 1, then 1 + ¼ = 1¼, then 1¼ + 1/16, creeping toward the same ceiling the infinite-geometric-series formula predicts: S = 1/(1 − ¼) = 4/3. Archimedes reached that four-thirds figure using only the method of exhaustion — comparing finite partial sums against the true area from both sides — because the formal idea of an infinite limit was still two thousand years away. The same kind of calculation, essentially unchanged, just resolved Achilles’s race.

Equation / Model

Every example above is one instance of a single general fact: a geometric series with first term a and ratio r converges, provided the size of r is less than 1, to a finite sum.

S = a / (1 − r), for |r| < 1

S equals a over the quantity one minus r, valid when the size of r is less than one.

S
the sum of the infinite series a + ar + ar² + ar³ + …
a
the first term of the sequence
r
the common ratio between consecutive terms; the formula applies only when the size of r is less than one

The formula isn’t a guess; it falls out of one algebraic trick applied to the finite partial sum first, then pushed to its limit. Write the sum of the first n terms, then write that same sum multiplied by r:

Sn = a + ar + ar² + … + arn−1

S sub n equals a plus a r plus a r squared, and so on, up to a r to the n minus one.

rSn = ar + ar² + ar³ + … + arn

r times S sub n equals a r plus a r squared plus a r cubed, and so on, up to a r to the n.

Subtract the second line from the first. Every middle term — ar, ar², and so on through arn−1 — appears in both rows and cancels exactly, leaving only the one term each row didn’t share:

Sn − rSn = a − arn

S sub n minus r S sub n equals a minus a r to the n.

Factor Sn out of the left side and divide: Sn(1 − r) = a(1 − rn), so Sn = a(1 − rn)/(1 − r). That is an exact formula for the sum of any finite number of terms, true for any r. The infinite case comes from asking what happens to this formula as n grows without bound: if |r| < 1, repeated multiplication by r drives rn toward zero (0.110 = 0.0000000001), so the term arn in the numerator vanishes in the limit, leaving S = a/(1 − r) exactly. If |r| ≥ 1 instead, rn never shrinks — it either stays put or grows — and the partial sums never settle on a fixed number at all, which is why the formula only applies, and only converges, when |r| < 1.

Why It Matters

Zeno’s paradox is often treated as a cute logical trick, but it posed a genuine problem for two thousand years: nobody had a rigorous way to say what an infinite sum even means, so arguments like his could be argued around but not definitively answered. Today’s result answers it completely: an infinite sum is not automatically infinite, and whether it is finite or infinite depends entirely on how fast its terms shrink. That reframes the paradox from a flaw in logic into a solved arithmetic problem — exactly the kind of victory that marks real mathematical progress, not avoiding a hard question but building the tools that make it stop being hard.

It also previews something this curriculum will need constantly from here on. The phrase used above — partial sums “settle down and approach one fixed number ever more closely” — is doing real work, but it is deliberately informal; it hasn’t said precisely how close counts as close enough, or what “approach” means for sequences that don’t come with a tidy ratio r attached. Day 9, The limit: approaching forever without arriving, formalizes exactly that idea into a single rigorous definition that applies not just to geometric series but to any sequence or function at all — the definition that finally let mathematicians trust infinity instead of merely working around it.

And the Archimedes example above is not a historical curiosity either: summing infinitely many shrinking pieces to find an exact area is the core idea behind integral calculus, developed independently by Newton and Leibniz roughly nineteen centuries after Archimedes used a cruder, entirely manual version of the same trick. The geometric series in today’s lesson is the direct, simplest ancestor of that much larger machine.

Remember These
  • A sequence is an ordered list of numbers (terms); a series is the running sum of that list.
  • Arithmetic sequences add a fixed amount each step and never shrink; geometric sequences multiply by a fixed ratio r, and shrink toward zero when the size of r is less than 1.
  • An infinite geometric series converges to a finite sum, S = a/(1−r), only when |r| < 1; otherwise it diverges.
  • Zeno’s paradox assumed infinitely many steps must take infinite time or distance; a convergent geometric series shows that assumption is false.
  • Archimedes used the same shrinking-geometric-series idea, two centuries after Zeno, to compute an exact area — the direct ancestor of integral calculus.
Think About It

The formula S = a/(1−r) breaks down the moment |r| reaches 1 — at r = 1 you’d be dividing by zero, and the derivation itself relied on rn shrinking to zero, which stops being true once |r| ≥ 1. Using the room-crossing version of Zeno’s argument, what would it mean, physically, for the ratio between your steps to be r = 1 instead of r = ½ — and why does that case match Zeno’s original intuition, that the steps really would add up to something infinite, instead of resolving it?
