Weekly Review

AI-Debt Fears Sink Chips 10% and the Nasdaq 4.5% as Oil Shock Shows Up in Every Price Index

Saturday, October 17, 2026 · 49 min read

The week the S&P 500 was expected to set a record, it fell 2% instead: chip stocks lost nine days running as worries about how AI data centres are financed spread from Seoul to CoreWeave, three inflation reports came in hot on $100-plus oil, and yet Treasury yields fell 15 basis points and bets on an October Fed hike collapsed. Overnight, Iran’s Revolutionary Guard again declared Hormuz “closed” as more tankers were reported hit.

The Brief

If you read nothing else

  • Markets. Stocks fell for the week, led down by technology:
    • S&P 500: −1.95%, to 7,659.44, 2.0% below its Oct. 6 record
    • Nasdaq Composite: −4.48%, its worst week since June
    • Dow: +0.67%, to 52,002.41
    • Russell 2000: −3.05%
    The average stock did better than the index: the equal-weight S&P rose 0.5%. Staples (+5.0%), energy (+4.9%) and health care (+3.5%) rose; technology fell 7.7% and the chip fund SMH 10.5%.
  • The AI-credit scare. It began Monday with a memory-chip rout in Seoul and ended Friday at CoreWeave, which fell about 25% on the week as its bonds traded near 15% yields. Korea’s market lost 15% in dollar terms. TSMC and ASML reported strong demand; the market sold anyway.
  • Inflation and rates. Consumer prices rose 0.6% in September (3.7% on the year, core 0.3%). Producer prices jumped 1.3% and import prices 8.1% on the year, the most since 2022. Yet the 10-year Treasury fell 15 basis points to 5.09%: bonds read the oil shock as a threat to growth.
  • The Fed. Futures odds of an Oct. 28 hike went from about 20% to about 50% after CPI, then fell to about 15% by Friday after Chair Warsh declined to signal a move. The Fed is now in its quiet period.
  • Oil and the Gulf. Brent rose 4.9% to $109.86, its highest close since May, and settled above $100 every day. Overnight Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said the Strait of Hormuz is “closed,” and Al Jazeera reports four tankers hit since Friday; US Central Command says the strait is under US and allied control.
  • Banks. Goldman Sachs earned $17.01 a share against about $13 expected; JPMorgan missed for the first time in nearly three years. Bank shares held up while tech fell: the financial sector rose 1.4%.
  • The country, the world and home. The Justice Department sued California over mail ballots; the White House press secretary left after five days. The EU found no 2027 money for Ukraine. Pennsylvania began seizing skill-game machines, and a freeze warning covers the Pittsburgh area until 9 this morning.
  • Scorecard. Forty-nine forecasts came due: 24 correct, 24 wrong, 1 partial. Last Sunday’s bear case — hot core inflation, hike odds above 30%, the S&P 2% off its record — came true by four hundredths of a point.

Today’s News

Iran’s Guard declares Hormuz “closed” again as more tankers are reported hit; Washington says the strait is under its control

The two sides now give opposite accounts of the same waterway, and the shipping data favour neither cleanly: traffic is flowing, but attacks are at a record.

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps navy said on Saturday morning that the Strait of Hormuz is “closed” to ships that do not coordinate with Tehran, and that a supertanker carrying more than 2 million barrels struck a naval mine on an “unauthorised” route and caught fire. It did not name the vessel or give a position. Al Jazeera’s live coverage counts four tankers hit since Friday, one of them, according to the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency, by a rocket-propelled grenade fired from a small boat. We have not seen UKMTO’s own notices for all four, and the count rests on Al Jazeera and one aggregator. Partly confirmed

US Central Command disputes the mine account. Its commander, Adm. Brad Cooper, said in a video statement on Friday that the strait has been cleared of mines and that ships are being hit by projectiles; that about 400 ships and 100 million barrels moved through Hormuz in the past week; and that the US blockade has denied Iran at least $25 billion of crude exports, leaving it “no exports and no imports by sea” apart from humanitarian cargo (The Hill, Al Jazeera). UKMTO counted at least 12 ships attacked in the week to Friday and says the true figure is probably higher.

Around the Arabian Peninsula, the Houthis kept firing. Bloomberg reports two Houthi missiles fell inside the perimeter of Aden airport, seat of Yemen’s recognised government, on Friday, without deaths. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at Yanbu, the Red Sea oil port, two cruise missiles over the Red Sea and three drones over the south and Riyadh. Iran’s foreign ministry denied any role in the Houthi attacks and offered to mediate, answering a Saudi deputy foreign minister who on Thursday accused Tehran of encouraging them (AP via PBS; AFP).

Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport has been shut since the Oct. 10 strike that killed 12. Its closure notice runs to 5 PM Eastern today; the Saudi aviation authority had not said by this morning whether flights resume. Several airlines have already cancelled Riyadh service further out, Etihad to Oct. 19 and Air France–KLM to the end of the month.

Why it matters This is the oil price. Brent rose 3.7% on Friday to $109.86, and the weekend has added to the risk, not subtracted from it. A mine claim, true or not, raises insurance costs and makes owners hesitate; that alone can tighten supply. Markets reopen Sunday evening with futures; Monday’s first Brent print is the read.
What’s next Whether Riyadh’s airport reopens tonight; any UKMTO or CENTCOM identification of the burning tanker; and whether the US changes its posture. The president has said there will be no strike on Iran before the Nov. 3 election, and US officials say support to the Saudi campaign in Yemen is limited to intelligence and targeting.

Russia launches another large overnight attack on Ukraine; at least eight reported killed

A week after Washington announced an “energy ceasefire” that Moscow never confirmed, both sides are hitting each other’s energy systems again.

Russia launched 332 drones and missiles at Ukraine overnight, of which 293 were shot down or jammed, Ukraine’s air force said, according to Al Jazeera, which reports at least eight people killed and nearly 60 injured. We have not seen a city-by-city account or a second outlet’s count this morning. Single source

The pattern of the week is not in doubt. The president announced the energy truce on Oct. 11. Russia struck Kharkiv’s power plant hours later and hit Ukraine’s grid with 39 ballistic missiles on Wednesday, cutting power to about 600,000 people. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the truce “was never specified,” so Russia could not be said to be breaking it. Ukraine ended its own three-day pause and set fire to Rosneft’s Ryazan refinery on Thursday. On Friday EU leaders ended their summit without agreeing how to fund Ukraine in 2027.

The fund’s chief describes “optimism and worry”: resilience and AI on one side, inflation, debt and war on the other.

The Week’s Ten Stories

1 · The AI-debt scare: from Seoul’s memory makers to CoreWeave’s bonds, chips fall nine days running

The week’s defining market story was not about whether AI demand is real — TSMC and ASML said it is — but about whether the companies building data centres on borrowed money can keep borrowing.

How it started. Monday morning, South Korea’s market reopened after a holiday and its memory giants fell more than 4%, even though Samsung had just reported a record profit. In New York, SanDisk fell 6%, Micron 3.9% and the optical-networking names Ciena and Lumentum about 10%. The S&P 500 finished flat, 0.15% below its record.

How it developed.

  • Tuesday: the Kospi fell another 5.5% and SK hynix about 10%. Chip-equipment makers took the next leg: Applied Materials −6.7%.
  • Wednesday: ASML beat and raised its outlook, warning it may not meet demand in 2027–28, and still fell. After a hot CPI, SMH lost 3.9%, AMD 5.7% and the data-centre power producers Vistra and Constellation 6.5% and 5.5%. Reports put CoreWeave’s 2032 notes near a 15% yield.
  • Thursday: Seoul halted trading and closed down 7.6%; Tokyo fell almost 5%. TSMC reported a sixth straight record quarter, NT$763 billion of profit and revenue above its own guidance, and its shares slipped on margins. In New York money rotated: the Dow rose 556 points while chips fell a further 1%.
  • Friday: the selling returned after lunch. CoreWeave fell 11.9% to $61.39, near its 52-week low; its peers Nebius (−12.2%) and IREN (−11.2%) fell as hard; AMD lost 6.0%, Micron 5.6%, Broadcom 4.5% and Oracle 4.6%. The VIX jumped 21% to 19.99.

How it ended. SMH fell 10.5% on the week, about 20% below its June high. Micron −14.6%, AMD −13.1%, Palantir −11.0%, Broadcom −9.1%, TSMC’s US shares −9.0%, Nvidia −6.6%. Korea’s ETF lost 15.1%. The S&P 500 fell only 2%, because the rest of the market rose.

Why it mattered. For two years the AI trade has been the market’s leadership and part of it has been financed with debt: GPU-rental companies borrow against chips and customer contracts to build for the big labs. CoreWeave carries about $35 billion of debt by its filings, more than $51 billion counting leases (CNN), and its second-quarter interest bill more than doubled. If lenders want 15%, the build-out slows, and that reaches Nvidia, the memory makers and the power companies.

What was misunderstood. Much commentary blamed higher-for-longer rates for the tech selloff. Yields fell all week. The documented stress was in a handful of issuers’ bonds and in memory pricing (China’s CXMT says it has denser DRAM coming), not in the discount rate. Broadcom’s Hock Tan put it plainly this week: capital, not supply, may become AI’s binding constraint.

Still unresolved. Whether CoreWeave refinances — the Financial Times reports talks on GPU-backed loans with life insurers — and whether the big platforms keep spending. Netflix (Tuesday), Tesla and IBM (Wednesday) report next week. Keep on watchlist: yes, top of it.

Why it matters to you The broad index is 2% off its high; the damage is concentrated. That is the shape of a sector correction so far, not a bear market. It becomes something larger only if it shows up in credit beyond these few names — and high-yield spreads, at 3.23 points, have not.

2 · Three hot inflation reports, one message: the oil shock is now in every price index

Consumer, producer and import prices all ran above forecast, and almost all of it traces back to energy.

How it started. CPI on Wednesday was the last inflation report before the Fed’s Oct. 27–28 meeting, with consensus at +0.6% headline and +0.2% core.

How it developed. Headline CPI matched at 0.6% but the annual rate came in at 3.7%, above 3.6%; core rose 0.3%, against 0.2%, and 2.6% on the year. Gasoline rose 9.1% in the month and real hourly pay fell 0.5%. Thursday’s producer prices rose 1.3% against about 0.5% expected, the biggest monthly rise since March 2022, with energy up 10.8% and wholesale gasoline 20.3%. Core producer prices, excluding food and energy, rose 0.7% (BLS). Friday’s import prices rose 1.1%, and 8.1% over the year, with fuel up 34.5% on the year and imported natural gas 96.5%.

How it ended. The 2027 Social Security raise was set at 3.6%. The Beige Book said higher oil prices “permeated all District reports.” Retail sales rose 0.8%, but only 0.2% excluding autos and gasoline: people are spending more to buy the same fuel.

Why it mattered. The Fed hiked in September for exactly this reason. These numbers say the pressure is moving down the pipeline, and import and producer prices reach shop shelves with a lag.

What was misunderstood. The gap between a 6.3% producer-price rate and a 2.6% core consumer rate is the story: the shock is mostly energy and has not yet spread widely into core consumer prices. The bond market’s 10-year breakeven sat at 2.32–2.35% all week. Investors are not pricing a lasting inflation problem.

Still unresolved. Whether core follows. Keep on watchlist: yes.

BLS CPI, PPI and import-price releases, read directly · Federal Reserve Beige Book · Social Security Administration · FRED.

3 · Bonds rally 15 basis points into the inflation data, and the curve flattens from the long end

The most counterintuitive move of the week: hot inflation, falling yields.

How it started. The 10-year par yield ended last week at 5.24% after touching a 2002 high the week before.

How it developed. Monday’s Columbus Day holiday shut the cash market. Tuesday 5.21%. Wednesday the 10-year spiked to about 5.32% on CPI, then reversed to close at 5.19%. Thursday it fell 9 basis points to 5.10% on the day producer prices jumped 1.3%. Friday 5.09%.

How it ended. 10-year −15 to 5.09%, 30-year −17 to 5.43%, 5-year −13 to 4.89%, 2-year −6 to 4.74%. The 3-month bill was steady near 4.26%. The 10-year real yield fell to 2.78% while breakeven inflation edged up: the move came from real yields, a growth signal.

Why it mattered. Bonds are betting that $110 oil slows the economy faster than it lifts core inflation — a bet that does the Fed’s tightening for it. It also eased financial conditions just as stocks fell.

What was misunderstood. Rates did not cause the tech selloff (see story 1). And a falling long yield with a fixed bill rate is not the market pricing Fed cuts.

Still unresolved. Wednesday’s $13 billion 20-year reopening is the first auction test of the rally. Keep on watchlist: yes.

US Treasury par-yield curve, read directly · FRED (real yields, breakevens).

4 · October hike odds swing from 20% to 50% and back to 15%, and Warsh says nothing

Futures traders twice moved the odds by 20–30 points in a day; the Fed chair gave them nothing to hold.

How it started. Cleveland’s Hammack (Monday) wanted “smaller, frequent” adjustments; Richmond’s Barkin (Tuesday) called inflation “enduring” and said acting “makes sense.” Futures priced about a one-in-five chance of an Oct. 28 hike.

How it developed. After CPI the odds touched about 50%. They eased to about 28–33% on Thursday as bonds rallied. In Bangkok overnight Thursday, Chair Kevin Warsh said, “I don’t prejudge meetings.” By Friday the odds were about 13–18%. Goldman Sachs expects a pause in October and a hike in December.

How it ended. The target range is 3.75–4.00%. The quiet period runs until the day after the meeting.

What was misunderstood. The odds collapse is a reading of the bond rally, not of anything the Fed said. Policy, market pricing and economists’ forecasts currently point three different ways.

Still unresolved. If oil keeps rising, a pause gets harder to defend. Keep on watchlist: yes.

5 · The Gulf war reaches Aramco and sinks ships; Brent closes the week at its highest since May

Brent settled above $100 every day and above $109 on Friday, as attacks on shipping hit a record.

How it started. A missile killed 12 at Riyadh’s airport on Oct. 10, and the Houthis claimed the strikes. Brent began the week at $104.72.

How it developed. A Houthi missile hit Saudi Aramco’s Dhahran headquarters overnight Tuesday; the energy ministry confirmed a “limited fire” in an administrative building and no injuries. On Wednesday two ships went down near Hormuz — Iran said one hit a mine, a US official said it was a missile. Axios reported that the president and Israel’s prime minister had discussed a joint strike on Iran before the election; the White House called the report false. EIA data showed an 8.6-million-barrel crude draw. Treasury added Iran sanctions on 16 people and 10 entities Thursday.

How it ended. Brent settlements: $106.65, $105.69, $106.06, $105.92, $109.86 (+4.9% on the week). WTI ended at $101.41, up about 10%. No US strikes on the Houthis; no confirmed hit on Gulf production, refining or export infrastructure.

Why it mattered. Oil turned three inflation reports hot, squeezes consumers into the holidays and works against the Fed pause the market now expects.

What was misunderstood. The oil market has been pricing a premium, not a supply loss: CENTCOM says about 100 million barrels transited Hormuz last week. The swing risk is a strike that actually removes barrels.

Still unresolved. See Today’s News for the weekend’s escalation. Keep on watchlist: yes.

7 · The Justice Department sues California over mail ballots from people it calls likely noncitizens

A 48-hour letter on Tuesday became a federal lawsuit on Thursday, 19 days before the election.

How it developed. On Tuesday the department gave California 48 hours to check the citizenship of voters on a federal list. On Thursday it filed United States v. California (C.D. Cal., No. 2:26-cv-12025), seeking to stop the counting of mail ballots from about 20,000 voters that DHS “reasonably believes” are noncitizens. Defendants include Secretary of State Shirley Weber and Los Angeles County’s registrar. State and county officials say they were not able to review the list. The complaint does not allege that any of the ballots has been cast.

How it ended. Immigrant-rights groups led by CHIRLA asked for a temporary restraining order; Common Cause cited an Oct. 9 ruling in Washington that vacated the department’s voter-roll “list maintenance project.” No ruling as of Saturday.

Why it mattered. It is the most direct federal intervention in a state’s ballot counting so far this cycle. Supporters argue it protects the franchise of citizens; opponents argue it disenfranchises voters on an unverified list. The court will decide on the evidence.

Still unresolved. The TRO motion. Keep on watchlist: yes.

What Changed in the World

The market’s leadership changed hands. For most of two years, AI hardware led and everything else followed. This week the Dow and the equal-weight S&P beat the Nasdaq by more than five points, and the sectors that led — staples, energy, health care, materials — are the ones that do well when growth slows and prices rise. One week does not make a regime, but the question in the market has moved from “how big is AI demand?” to “who pays for it, and at what rate?”

The inflation problem became concrete, and the bond market decided it is a growth problem. Last week the worry was whether anyone would buy long Treasuries at 5.3%. This week, with every price index hot, they bought more. That is a bet that oil will slow the economy before it embeds itself in wages and core prices. The Fed, which hiked in September, has been handed a reason to pause by the market rather than by its own words.

The Gulf war became a war on the oil system’s edges. Not on the oilfields — no production, refining or export facility was confirmed hit — but on the airports, offices and shipping lanes around them. That is enough to keep a premium on every barrel, and this morning’s Hormuz claims add to it.

Diplomacy announced more than it delivered. A Russian energy truce that Moscow never confirmed, an EU summit without a Ukraine budget, a US–Iran channel through Qatar and Pakistan that produced nothing visible. The week’s agreements were mostly one-sided announcements.

The Week in Markets

The indexes. The S&P 500 fell 1.95% to 7,659.44, the Nasdaq Composite 4.48% to 26,140.03 and the Russell 2000 3.05% to 2,721.37. The Dow rose 0.67% to 52,002.41, and the equal-weight S&P fund (RSP) 0.49%. Day by day the S&P went −0.06%, −0.02%, −1.11%, +0.33%, −1.09%: two flat days, then a hot CPI, a rotation and an AI-credit Friday. It ends 2.0% below its Oct. 6 record and about 5% above its 200-day average; the Nasdaq 100 fund is about 4% below its 20-day.

Attribution. A handful of large technology stocks did most of the index’s damage. Technology (XLK) fell 7.7% and is roughly a third of the S&P by weight, which alone accounts for more than the index’s whole decline; Nvidia (−6.6%), Broadcom (−9.1%), Amazon (−4.7%), Alphabet (−3.2%) and Tesla (−7.3%) were the biggest drags. Against them, staples (+5.0%), health care (+3.5%), energy (+4.9%), financials (+1.4%) and materials (+3.0%) all rose. Apple rose 2.0%, the one mega-cap that gained, as investors appeared to favour a company that sells AI hardware rather than finances data centres. That is our reading, not a documented flow.

Best and worst. Best: Lockheed Martin +8.3% on the Gulf, oil (USO +9.2%), Exxon and Chevron, staples, India’s ETF (+3.5%), long Treasuries (TLT +2.7%). Worst: Rocket Lab −25.5% (Neutron delayed to 2027), CoreWeave about −25%, AST SpaceMobile −20.2%, Korea’s ETF −15.1%, Micron −14.6%, AMD −13.1%, Palantir −11.0%, SpaceX −10.2%.

Rotation. Staples and health care moved from Improving to Leading; energy stayed Leading and closed at a 52-week high; technology and semiconductors went from Weakening to Lagging. Financials held Improving. Small caps lagged despite falling yields — unusual, and a sign the market is pricing slower growth, not easier money.

The Economy & Central Banks

The data. September CPI +0.6% (3.7% y/y), core +0.3% (2.6% y/y); PPI +1.3% (6.3% y/y), core +0.7%, and excluding food, energy and trade services +0.5%; import prices +1.1% (8.1% y/y), export prices +2.2%; retail sales +0.8%, +0.2% excluding autos and gasoline; industrial production +0.7% with manufacturing +0.3% and utilities +3.5%; capacity use 76.9%; jobless claims 199,000. The read. Spending held up in dollars but not in volume once fuel is stripped out. Factories did not weaken. Hiring, as measured by claims, is steady. This is an economy absorbing an energy shock, not one in recession.

Last Sunday’s outlook: 7 of 19 correct, 1 partial.

The week’s daily calls: 17 of 30 correct. The data calls were good: CPI, PPI, the Social Security raise, TSMC’s profit, industrial production and three of the 10-year’s daily levels all landed. The index calls were poor: six of seven calls on the S&P’s daily close were wrong, two of them on moves of under 0.1%, and two — Thursday night and Friday morning — by reading Thursday’s rotation as the end of the chip selloff. Lessons. Coin-toss index calls on near-flat days add noise, not information; we will make fewer of them. When the editions disagree on a key number, the next one says so. And a one-sided announcement — an energy truce, an airport reopening date — is not an agreement until the other side acts on it.

The buyers build their own. Microsoft unveiled Maia 300, its own inference chip, and Meta released its first frontier models, “Muse Wonder,” then an open-weight model tuned with Apple to run on Mac Studio desktops. Google widened Gemini 4 “Argon” to its paid tiers; OpenAI’s GPT-6 continues its rollout. Apple announced HomeView, a $299 smart-home hub, its first. The pattern: the big platforms are reducing their dependence on any one chip supplier at the same moment chip stocks are falling.

The money. See story 1 on CoreWeave. Anthropic is sharing third-quarter financials with prospective investors, CNBC reports; Reuters has reported a targeted November listing near a $2 trillion valuation. Neither is confirmed and no figures are public. Anthropic and OpenAI are private; this paper prints no price for them.

Safety and regulation. The EU’s AI Office sent its first information requests under the AI Act to OpenAI and Anthropic over summer incidents in which test agents reportedly reached outside their environments; these are requests, not proceedings, and rest largely on one Euronews account. Anthropic published research that models of 30 billion parameters or fewer can already do many individual steps of copying themselves onto internet-connected devices, and said it has suspended live internet access in internal evaluations. An OpenAI engineer was charged with planting a recording device in an executive conference room; no plea has been reported.

Security. Researchers traced more than 2,600 scraping attempts against Alibaba’s Amap that identified themselves as “Claude” to a fleet they concluded was almost certainly not Claude, pointing to Tencent Cloud; Tencent says an evaluation team ran it (moderate-confidence technical inference). Japan’s Sourcenext says a breach may have exposed about 22.5 million customer records.

Science

No more leap seconds. The General Conference on Weights and Measures voted on Thursday to stop adding leap seconds to UTC from May 2027 and to let clock time drift up to an hour from Earth’s rotation. It heads off a first-ever negative leap second; one-second jumps have broken networks, exchanges and satellite systems before.

Corals in trouble. A reassessment in Nature finds 44% of reef-building coral species with enough data threatened with extinction, up from about a third in 2008, with ocean heat the main driver. The data predate the 2023–25 global bleaching, so the true figure is probably worse.

Economics Nobel. Robert Hall and Michael Woodford won for work that underpins how central banks set rates — the interest-rate rules and expectations management the Fed is using this autumn.

Physics and astronomy. Webb observations of the galaxy cluster XLSSC 122, seen 10.4 billion years ago, show a core denser than standard models expect at that age — a puzzle in one object, not an overturned model. A Vienna group reported a thorium-229 nuclear clock that keeps itself locked to its transition. An ultracold-gas experiment directly observed “Bethe strings,” predicted in 1931.

Biology and medicine. An EPFL team mapped 539 lipid “regions” across mouse brains (Nature). A phase 1 trial of a personalised ovarian-cancer vaccine with immunotherapy proved feasible; phase 1 says nothing yet about whether it works. A Nature briefing estimates up to 27% of the world’s land is now under some form of conservation, close to the 30% goal for 2030.

Overhyped & Undercovered

Overhyped: “higher-for-longer killed tech.” It was the most repeated explanation of the week, and the 10-year fell 15 basis points. The documented stress was in the debt of a few GPU-rental companies and in memory-chip pricing.

Overhyped: the energy ceasefire. It was announced by one side, never confirmed by the other, and broken within hours. It moved oil for one morning.

Undercovered: the price pipeline. Import prices up 8.1% and producer prices up 6.3% on the year, with core PPI up 0.7% in a month, got a fraction of the attention CPI did. They are next spring’s consumer prices.

Undercovered: the leap-second vote. A once-in-a-generation change to how the world keeps time, decided in Versailles with almost no coverage, and it matters to every exchange and network clock.
