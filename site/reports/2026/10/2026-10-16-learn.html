In 1965, Gordon Moore Drew a Straight Line Through a Few Dots and Called 65,000. A Decade Later, the Chips Were Roughly There.

Friday, October 16, 2026 · 13 min read

A quantity that grows by a constant percentage compounds, and a logarithmic scale turns that compounding into a straight line — the same reading trick that let one engineer stretch ten dots into a decade’s forecast, and that later showed, days before the daily case count did, when a pandemic’s spread was actually slowing.

Mathematics

Day 2 of 293 · Mathematics 2 of 24 · Exponential growth and logarithms

A few dots on a log scale, extended ten years

The April 19, 1965 issue of Electronics magazine carried a short article by Gordon Moore, a chemist by training who was then director of research and development at Fairchild Semiconductor. Moore plotted the number of components — transistors, resistors, capacitors — on the most cost-effective integrated circuit his industry could build, for a handful of years from 1959 to 1965. The count rose from a single component to about 64. When he put those five or six points on a vertical axis marked off in powers of ten rather than equal steps, they fell close to a straight line, doubling roughly once a year.

Moore then did something bolder than describing what had already happened: he extended that line a full decade past his last data point and wrote that, by 1975, the leading chip would hold on the order of 65,000 components. He later called this “a wild extrapolation” — five or six points, one more doubling than the number of points themselves, stretched into a thousand-fold forecast. A decade on, in a 1975 paper, he reported that complexity had in fact kept roughly to that annual-doubling pace, pointing to a 16,384-bit charge-coupled-device memory chip then in development as evidence. In that same 1975 paper he revised his own forecast going forward, slowing the doubling from every year to roughly every two.

Why would anyone trust a straight line through a handful of points enough to forecast a thousand-fold increase a decade out — and why did putting those points on a scale marked in powers of ten, rather than equal steps, make that forecast look so much more reasonable than it would have on an ordinary chart?

The Big Idea

When something grows by a constant percentage of its current size each period — rather than by a constant amount — it compounds, and compounding grows explosively compared with anything that just adds a fixed quantity each time. A logarithmic scale is an axis marked off in constant ratios (steps of ×10, or ×2, or any other factor) instead of constant additions. On that kind of axis, constant-percentage growth draws a straight line, because equal distances up the axis now represent equal multiplications rather than equal additions.

The slope of that line is the growth rate itself. That is what makes a log-scale chart a reading instrument rather than just a different-looking picture: it is the fastest way to read a growth rate off a curve, to compare two different growth rates side by side, and — cautiously, as the story above already warns — to extend a trend forward the way Moore did with his handful of dots.

How It Works

Start with the distinction a straight line on an ordinary chart hides. Linear growth adds the same amount every period: a savings account earning a flat $50 a year, say. Exponential growth multiplies by the same factor every period instead: a savings account earning 5% interest a year does not add $50 each year: it adds 5% of whatever balance it has already reached, so the dollar amount added keeps rising as the balance itself rises. The same mechanism drives early bacterial division, viral spread before anything slows it down, and compound interest — anything where the next period’s increase scales with the current total rather than staying fixed.

A convenient way to characterize exponential growth is its doubling time: how long it takes the quantity to double, which stays constant even as the quantity itself grows ever larger in absolute terms. There is a shortcut for estimating it, the rule of 70: for a quantity growing at r percent per period, the doubling time is roughly 70 divided by r. Growing at 7% a year doubles in about ten years (70 ÷ 7); growing at 1% a year doubles in about seventy. The “70” comes from the natural logarithm of 2 (about 0.693) expressed as a percentage, and the shortcut is most accurate for modest growth rates — under roughly 15–20% per period — where each period’s compounding step is still small enough that the approximation holds up. The percentage rate itself is the same thing finance calls the compound annual growth rate: nothing new there for you, only a new reason to care what it looks like once it is plotted.

The logarithm is what makes a straight line out of this in the first place. The base-ten logarithm of a number is the power you would have to raise 10 to, to get that number: log10(1,000) = 3, because 103 = 1,000. A logarithm is the inverse of exponentiation in exactly the sense subtraction is the inverse of addition. That inverse relationship is what lets a logarithm turn multiplication into addition: the logarithm of a product equals the sum of the logarithms of its factors. Applied to a quantity that doubles every fixed interval, taking the logarithm of “how big is it after t periods” converts a repeated multiplication by 2 into a repeated addition of log10(2) — and a quantity that grows by repeated addition, plotted against time, is a straight line.

That is exactly what a logarithmic (ratio) scale does on paper: it is an axis where equal vertical distances mean equal ratios rather than equal differences. The gap from 1 to 10 takes the same space as the gap from 10 to 100, or from 1,000 to 10,000, because each step is the same multiplicative factor. Once you are plotting on an axis built that way, a curve that climbs ever more steeply on an ordinary linear chart — the visual signature everyone associates with “exponential” — flattens into a straight line, and the line’s slope is the growth rate, directly readable with a ruler rather than estimated by eye from a curve’s changing steepness. A chart that looks like a dead-straight line on a log axis but curves on a linear one is one of the most reliable fingerprints of constant-percentage growth there is.

That same reading advantage turns the log chart into a diagnostic for the opposite case too: a bend in a log-scale line means the growth rate itself is changing, not just the quantity. A line that bends upward on a log scale means growth is accelerating; a line that bends over and flattens means growth is slowing, even while the raw quantity is still rising. Either way, the bend is often visible on a log chart well before it would be obvious by eye on a linear one, where a slowing exponential can still look like it is racing upward because the absolute numbers keep climbing.

That reading power comes with a sharp limit, and it is the one Moore’s own 1975 revision illustrates directly: a straight line fit to past data describes the past rate. It is not a law of nature obligated to continue. Whether “Moore’s Law” in its original form has truly ended, and when, is Debated among semiconductor engineers and historians — transistor density and cost per transistor tell different stories about the trend’s health, and a related scaling rule governing transistor power (Dennard scaling) is widely agreed to have broken down by the mid-2000s even while density kept climbing for years afterward. Some historians of technology argue the original pace held as well as it did for as long as it did partly because chipmakers planned their own roadmaps around hitting it — meaning the trend was, in part, a target the industry organized itself around, not a force acting on the industry from outside. A line on a log chart tells you the slope that held up to today. It does not tell you why, or whether it will keep holding.

Back to the story

Moore’s handful of points was enough precisely because a log scale turns constant-percentage growth into a straight line: once five or six dots agree on a slope, extending that slope ten more years is simple arithmetic, not a new measurement. Sixty-four components doubling once a year for ten years is 64 × 210, which comes out to about 65,000 — the number Moore wrote down. The forecast held, roughly, for a decade. But the method’s own limit, explained just above, is exactly what caught up with the forecast next: a straight line describes the rate that held in the past, and in 1975 Moore himself revised the slope going forward, because the rate that had held for a decade was not a law of nature guaranteed to hold forever.

Key Concepts

Exponential growth
Growth in which each period’s increase is a constant percentage of the current total, not a constant amount — so the absolute size of each increase keeps rising as the total itself rises.
Doubling time & the rule of 70
How long a quantity growing at a constant percentage rate takes to double; approximated as 70 divided by the growth rate in percent per period, most accurate under roughly 15–20% growth.
Logarithm
The inverse of exponentiation: the base-ten logarithm of a number is the power 10 must be raised to in order to produce it. Logarithms turn multiplication into addition.
Logarithmic (ratio) scale
An axis on which equal distances represent equal multiplicative ratios rather than equal additive differences — on which constant-percentage growth draws a straight line.
Extrapolation
Extending an observed trend beyond the data that established it; reliable only as long as whatever drove the trend keeps operating the same way, which a fitted line itself cannot tell you.

Example: Reading a Pandemic’s Log Chart in Real Time

In March 2020, confirmed COVID-19 case counts in many countries were growing by roughly 15–30% a day. Journalists covering the outbreak, most visibly John Burn-Murdoch at the Financial Times, popularized log-scale charts that plotted each country’s cumulative cases against days since some common starting point, rather than against calendar date on an ordinary linear axis. Burn-Murdoch argued directly for the log scale’s honesty here: on a linear chart, a country early in its own outbreak can look reassuringly flat next to one further along, when in fact both are compounding at a similar rate and one is simply a few doublings behind the other.

1. Convert a daily growth rate to a doubling time. The rule of 70 (really natural-log-based, so more precisely “ln 2 divided by the daily growth rate”) gives, for 15% daily growth: ln(2) ÷ ln(1.15) ≈ 0.693 ÷ 0.140 ≈ 5 days.

2. Do the same for the faster end of the range. For 25% daily growth: ln(2) ÷ ln(1.25) ≈ 0.693 ÷ 0.223 ≈ 3.1 days.

3. Read what a bend means. When public-health measures or behavior change slowed the daily growth rate, the log-scale line of cumulative cases bent visibly flatter days before the linear-scale curve of new daily cases turned over and started falling — because the log chart shows the rate changing directly, while the linear chart of daily new cases has to wait for that changing rate to show up as an actual decline in the raw count.

Doubling times during the pandemic varied by country, by wave, and were distorted at times by changes in testing capacity rather than the virus’s own spread — a reminder that a clean mathematical doubling time is only as good as the data feeding it. The reading method itself, though, is the same one Moore’s five dots relied on: put compounding data on a log axis, and the slope — and any bend in it — tells you the story days before the raw numbers would.

Equation / Model

A quantity growing exponentially with doubling time Td follows:

N(t) = N0 × 2t/Td   ⇒   log10N(t) = log10N0 + (t/Td) log102

N of t equals N sub 0 times 2 to the power of t divided by T sub d; therefore the base-ten logarithm of N of t equals the base-ten logarithm of N sub 0, plus the quantity t divided by T sub d, times the base-ten logarithm of 2.

N(t)
the quantity’s size after time t
N0
the starting size, at t = 0
Td
the doubling time — how long the quantity takes to double
log10
the base-ten logarithm; taking it of both sides turns the doubling (multiplicative) relation into a straight line in t

The right-hand form is exactly why the chart is straight on a log axis: it says log10N is log10N0 plus a term that grows in direct proportion to t — the defining shape of a straight line. The rule of thumb connecting a percentage growth rate to a doubling time follows the same logarithmic logic:

Td ≈ 70 / r

T sub d is approximately equal to 70 divided by r.

Td
doubling time, in the same periods as the growth rate (years, days, and so on)
r
the growth rate, in percent per period

Why It Matters

Once you can read a log-scale chart, you can tell, at a glance, whether something is truly compounding at a steady rate, speeding up, or slowing down — a distinction that is nearly impossible to make by eye on an ordinary chart, where every exponential curve looks like it is racing upward right up until it suddenly does not. That distinction is worth having well before you need it for anything specific: it is the same reading skill whether the quantity on the axis is a chip’s component count, a disease’s case count, a population, or a bank balance.

The habit that matters even more than the chart-reading is the caution built into How It Works: a fitted straight line, however confidently drawn, is a statement about the past, and extending it is a bet that whatever made the rate what it was keeps operating unchanged. Moore’s own revision, one decade after his own forecast, is the method’s inventor making exactly that correction in public.

The same two-sided lesson — read the slope, then doubt the slope’s permanence — is why log-scale thinking shows up wherever someone has to plan years ahead against a compounding quantity: a chipmaker sizing a factory, a public-health official sizing hospital capacity, an actuary pricing a long-dated liability. In each case the chart answers “how fast, really, and is that changing,” and in each case the honest next question is what would have to be true for the rate to hold.

Connections

Back. Day 1’s Fermi estimates were built from factors tracked in scientific notation — powers of ten kept explicit at every step. A logarithmic scale is that same powers-of-ten bookkeeping turned into a picture: an axis marked off in orders of magnitude, which is why reading one comes naturally once you have already learned to think in them.

Across. In 2018, OpenAI researchers Dario Amodei and Danny Hernandez published an analysis, “AI and Compute,” finding that the amount of computing power used in the largest AI training runs had grown roughly 300,000-fold since 2012 — doubling about every 3.4 months, versus Moore’s original one-year and later two-year doubling for transistor counts. It is the identical method applied to a far faster-moving process: fit a straight line to a log-scale plot of a compounding quantity, and read the slope as the rate. The same caution applies doubly here — a growth rate measured over six years of a new, fast-moving field is an even thinner basis for extrapolation than Moore’s own, and compute growth has itself since slowed from that 2012–2018 pace.

Remember These

  • The rule of 70: doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ (percent growth per period).
  • A logarithm turns multiplication into addition; on a logarithmic (ratio) scale, constant-percentage growth draws a straight line.
