Friday, October 16, 2026 · 33 min read

A calm morning turned into a tech rout as CoreWeave fell 12% and chip stocks lost for a ninth straight day, the VIX jumped to 20 and oil rose more than 3%, while import prices posted their biggest yearly rise since 2022, Treasury yields barely moved and October hike odds fell further.

The Brief

Top Stories

The AI-debt scare returns: CoreWeave falls 12%, chips lose a ninth straight day, and the Nasdaq has its worst week since June

Thursday’s rotation looked like the chip selloff was burning out. Friday afternoon it spread back into the big technology names, and the VIX jumped to 20.

The day started quietly. Stocks opened mixed, with the S&P 500 up about 0.2% early (TheStreet) and the Dow higher at midday, while the Nasdaq slipped. By midday the Nasdaq 100 was down more than 1%, and the losses deepened through the afternoon. The S&P 500 closed down 1.09% at 7,659.44 and the Nasdaq Composite down 2.15% at 26,140.03 (Yahoo Finance; CNBC reports the same closes). Over the week the Nasdaq lost 4.5%, its worst week since late June according to Yahoo Finance and CNN, and the S&P 500 lost 1.95%.

The stocks at the centre were the ones that rent out AI computing power. CoreWeave fell 11.9% to $61.39, a whisker above its 52-week low of $59.51, and is down about 25% on the week. Nebius fell 12.2% and IREN 11.2%. CNN puts CoreWeave’s debt at more than $51 billion; other estimates differ by whether leases are counted. Its 2032 notes have traded at yields around 15%, CNBC reported this week, and Mizuho cut its price target to $88 from $115, while JPMorgan called the selloff overdone. Chief executive Michael Intrator said on Wednesday the selloff was irrational and that a backlog of about $104 billion supports the debt. Nvidia’s Jensen Huang said on Thursday that CoreWeave would not need a backstop.

The damage spread to suppliers and to the big platforms. AMD fell 6.0%, Micron 5.6%, SanDisk 6.9%, Broadcom 4.5%, Oracle 4.6% and Dell 4.6%. Meta fell 3.7% and Alphabet 2.5%. Nvidia lost a smaller 1.4%, to $214.13. The VanEck semiconductor fund (SMH) fell 3.4% to 539.79, its ninth straight decline; it is down 10.5% on the week and about 20% below its June high. Coinbase fell 5.8% as bitcoin slipped below $82,000.

Why it happened today is not settled. CNN, CBS and CNBC all point to doubts about how the AI build-out is financed, and to higher borrowing costs after this week’s hot inflation reports; CNBC also cites oil and worries about Chinese chip competition. Those are investors’ stated focus, not a documented trigger. There was no new CoreWeave filing or rating action that we could find. Monthly options expiration may have added to the afternoon swing; we cannot measure how much.

Why it matters

For two years the AI trade has been funded partly with debt: GPU-rental companies borrow against chips and customer contracts to build data centres for the big AI labs. If lenders now demand 15% to fund that, the build-out slows, and the slowdown reaches Nvidia, the memory makers and the power companies. The broad index is only 2% below its high; the stress is concentrated, not general, so far.

What’s next

Watch CoreWeave’s bond prices and any financing news; the Financial Times has reported it is seeking investment-grade-style, GPU-backed loans from life insurers. Next week’s earnings from Netflix (Tuesday) and Tesla, IBM and possibly Meta (Wednesday) will be read for AI spending plans.

Sources: Yahoo Finance closing data (checked against Twelve Data); CNBC; CNN; CBS News; Reuters via Yahoo; Benzinga; TheStreet; Mizuho and JPMorgan notes as reported.

Import prices rise 8.1% in a year, the most since 2022, and factory output jumps 0.7%

The third inflation report of the week tells the same story as the first two: energy is pushing prices up at every stage.

US import prices rose 1.1% in September after 0.5% in August, the Bureau of Labor Statistics said, against a Reuters-poll forecast of about 0.8%. Over 12 months they rose 8.1%, the largest annual rise since July 2022. Fuel import prices rose 4.8% in the month and 34.5% on the year; imported natural gas is up 96.5% from a year ago. Excluding fuel, import prices rose 0.7%, so the pressure is not only oil. Export prices rose 2.2%.

Industrial production rose 0.7% in September, the Federal Reserve said, and August was revised up to 0.1% from flat. Most of the jump was utilities, up 3.5%. Manufacturing rose 0.3%, in line with forecasts, and mining 0.2%. Capacity utilisation rose to 76.9%, still 2.5 points below its long-run average. Output is 2.2% above a year earlier.

Bonds did not react much. The 10-year par yield ended at 5.09%, down 1 basis point (Treasury). That is the second day in a row the bond market has looked past a hot price report.

Why it matters

Import prices feed into consumer prices with a lag, so September’s jump is pressure still to come at the till. The Fed raised rates in September for exactly this reason. But the market is reading the energy shock as something that will slow growth, not something that will force the Fed to keep hiking, and the factory data are not strong enough to contradict that.

Sources: BLS import/export price release (Sept 2026), read directly; Federal Reserve G.17 release, read directly; US Treasury par yield curve; consensus via Reuters and InvestingLive.

October hike odds fall to the teens as the Fed goes quiet

Two weeks before the Oct. 27–28 meeting, futures traders have mostly stopped expecting a second straight increase.

CME futures put the chance of a quarter-point hike on Oct. 28 at about 18% at midday (Benzinga), and one later reading had 13% (CoinEdition). This morning CME said the odds were below 30%; after Wednesday’s CPI they had touched 53%. We found no Fed speeches today. Chair Kevin Warsh’s remarks in Bangkok overnight, “I don’t prejudge meetings,” were the last word before the quiet period, which runs from Saturday until the day after the meeting. Goldman Sachs expects a pause in October and a hike in December.

The Fed raised its target range to 3.75–4.00% on Sept. 16. Since then, consumer prices rose 3.7% on the year, producer prices 6.3% and import prices 8.1%, while the 10-year yield has fallen to 5.09% from the 5.30% high yield at last week’s 10-year auction, as reported.

Why it matters

A pause with inflation this high is a bet that oil will do the Fed’s work by slowing the economy. If oil keeps rising, as it did today, that bet gets harder to defend, and the odds can swing back quickly; they moved by 20–30 points twice this week.

Sources: CME FedWatch as reported by Benzinga and CoinEdition; Federal Reserve; Goldman Sachs as reported by CNBC; Treasury auction results via TreasuryDirect aggregators.

Oil jumps more than 3%: Brent near $110, WTI back above $100

A soft morning gave way to a late rally as attacks on Gulf shipping and Ukraine’s strikes on Russian refineries continued.

Brent crude settled up 3.7% at $109.86 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate up 3.5% at $101.41, CNBC reports. Yahoo’s Brent quote, at $109.62 after the settle, agrees. Both had been lower in the morning. Ship-tracker Kpler estimates flows through the Strait of Hormuz at about 68% of normal. UKMTO data compiled today count 12 ships attacked near the strait this month, and the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted drones and missiles aimed at Riyadh, the Red Sea and Yanbu. Energy stocks rose 2.5% (XLE), to a 52-week high; Exxon and Chevron each rose about 2.5%.

The settlement figures come from one outlet; the futures contract Yahoo shows for WTI has rolled to December and is not comparable. Over the week, USO rose 9.2%.

Why it matters

Oil above $100 is what turned three inflation reports hot this week. It also works against the Fed pause the market now expects, and it squeezes consumers just as the holiday season starts.

Sources: CNBC (settlements); Yahoo Finance; Kpler via Yahoo UK; UKMTO via BBC; Saudi-led coalition statement via L’Orient Today and Reuters.

Banks finish the week as the market’s steady corner: Travelers rises after a big beat, Truist and Regions gain

Insurers and banks reported solid quarters, and unlike tech, their shares held up.

Travelers earned $9.18 a share on a core basis, against about $7.42 expected (LSEG via CNBC), with a combined ratio of 86.8% and record net investment income of $1.1 billion, its filing shows. Catastrophe losses were $538 million, up from $402 million a year ago. The stock fell about 4% before the open but closed up 1.5% at $389.54.

Truist’s adjusted earnings of $1.16 were roughly in line, after a $1.4 billion loss on selling securities to reinvest at higher yields; it closed up 1.1%. Regions beat ($0.73 adjusted against $0.66) with record capital-markets income and rose 0.5%. M&T fell 1.5% and Citizens was flat. JPMorgan (+1.3%) and Wells Fargo (+1.4%) also rose. The financial sector fund ended up 0.5% and the regional-bank fund was flat, both up more than 1% on the week.

Interactive Brokers fell 3.9% after missing estimates, and J.B. Hunt 2.8% after a miss on earnings. Alcoa rose 2.6% after beating on earnings.

Sources: Travelers and Truist 8-K filings (SEC EDGAR); Regions release (Business Wire); CNBC; LSEG and Zacks consensus as reported; Yahoo Finance closes.

EU leaders end their summit without a plan to fund Ukraine in 2027

Zelensky asked for another €70 billion; the leaders asked the Commission to come back with options.

After meeting European Council President António Costa and Commission President Ursula von der Leyen in Brussels, President Zelensky said there were “no signals” Russia was ready to end the war and asked for an extra €70 billion for 2027, plus help with energy and air defence, Euronews and others report. The leaders asked the Commission to propose how to fill the gap; Belgium still opposes using frozen Russian assets. Estonia’s prime minister told Reuters a new EU loan is probably needed. The summit also agreed to work on trade-defence tools against China and adopted a shortened statement on migration after a sharp dispute over Spain’s regularisation of about 600,000 migrants.

Euronews reports President Trump called Ukraine’s refinery strikes a “disgrace”; we could not pin down when he said it. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Thursday that Ukraine had “never stopped” the strikes.

Sources: Euronews; Reuters; The Tribune; Korea Times.

White House press secretary leaves after five days

Accounts differ on whether Katie Zacharia was pushed out or is moving to another role.

Katie Zacharia, who replaced Karoline Leavitt as press secretary, left the job on Friday, Axios reported, citing two senior administration officials; CBS, NBC and CNBC confirmed her departure. A White House official told CNN and NBC she is “transitioning” to a new role to be announced; other outlets describe her as ousted. NBC reported that she had presented herself as a lawyer but had not passed the bar exam. No successor had been named by our deadline.

Sources: Axios; CBS News; NBC News; CNN; CNBC.

The Economy

Today’s data. Import prices rose 1.1% in September (forecast about 0.8%) and 8.1% on the year; August stayed at 0.5% in today’s release, which Reuters describes as a downward revision from 0.7%. Fuel rose 4.8%, nonfuel 0.7%. Export prices rose 2.2% (BLS). These are nominal price indexes, and the 8.1% is a rate of change: import prices are rising faster, not just high. Industrial production rose 0.7% after a revised 0.1% in August; manufacturing rose 0.3%, utilities 3.5%. Capacity utilisation was 76.9% (Federal Reserve G.17).

The week in inflation. Consumer prices rose 3.7% on the year in September, with core at 2.6%; producer prices rose 6.3%; import prices 8.1%. The gap tells the story: the shock is mostly energy, and it has not yet reached core consumer prices in a big way. The 10-year breakeven inflation rate, the bond market’s inflation expectation, was 2.32% on Thursday, little changed for weeks (FRED). Markets are not pricing a lasting inflation problem.

The Fed. Quiet period from Saturday through Oct. 29. The target range is 3.75–4.00% after September’s hike. Market pricing (CME, as reported) points to a hold on Oct. 28; Goldman expects a December hike. Official policy, market pricing and economists’ forecasts are three different things, and right now they disagree.

The Treasury. The curve rallied all week: the 10-year par yield fell 15 basis points to 5.09%, the 30-year 17 to 5.43%, the 5-year 13 to 4.89%; the 2-year fell only 6 to 4.74%, and the 3-month bill was unchanged at 4.26%. The curve flattened from the long end. Next week Treasury sells $95 billion of 13-week and $82 billion of 26-week bills on Monday, $95 billion of 6-week bills Tuesday, a $13 billion 20-year bond reopening Wednesday and $26 billion of 5-year TIPS Thursday (TreasuryDirect). The 20-year sale is the one to watch after last week’s 10-year auction cleared at about 5.30%, reported as the highest since 2000. Treasury announced new Iran sanctions on Thursday on 16 people and 10 entities.

Credit. The ICE BofA high-yield spread was 3.23 percentage points on Thursday, down from 3.25, and the investment-grade spread 0.84 (FRED). High-yield bond funds fell 0.35% today. Spreads across the market are not wide; the stress is in a handful of AI-infrastructure issuers.

Global. Bank of Japan Governor Kazuhiko Ueda said rates will keep rising as underlying inflation nears 2%; the BoJ meets Oct. 29–30. China’s offshore yuan is near its strongest since 2022, about 6.68 per dollar. European stocks rose for the week, the Stoxx 600 up 1.4%. China reports third-quarter growth Sunday at 10 PM Eastern; the Reuters poll is 4.4% on the year.

Science

Corals. A peer-reviewed reassessment in Nature of 892 reef-building coral species finds that 44% of those with enough data (340 of 774) are threatened with extinction, up from about a third in 2008. Projected ocean heat is the main driver. The data predate the 2023–25 global bleaching, so the authors’ numbers probably understate today’s risk.

Time. The General Conference on Weights and Measures, meeting in Versailles, voted on Thursday to stop adding leap seconds to Coordinated Universal Time from May 2027 and to let clock time drift up to an hour from Earth’s rotation, Scientific American and others report. The change heads off the first-ever negative leap second, which software engineers feared as Earth’s spin has sped up slightly. It matters to anyone who runs networks, exchanges or satellites, where a one-second jump has broken systems before.

Blue Origin slips. New Glenn’s return to flight now looks like early 2027 rather than the December target Jeff Bezos gave on Oct. 7, Reuters reports; the company has not announced a date. The rocket has been grounded since a May 28 test-stand explosion. Blue Origin plans two more Florida pads and has asked the FAA for up to 50 launches a year. Blue Origin is private.

Pennsylvania Attorney General Dave Sunday sued Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk and Sanofi, along with Express Scripts, CVS Caremark and OptumRx, in Commonwealth Court, alleging a scheme that raised insulin list prices by more than 1,000%, WESA and WPXI report.
