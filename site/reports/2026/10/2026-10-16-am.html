Top Stories

Warsh declines to signal his next move, and markets take that as a likely October pause

The Fed chair’s last public remarks before the blackout were about global tightening and AI, not about Oct. 28. Traders and Goldman Sachs read the silence as no hike this month.

Speaking with IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva at the IMF and World Bank meetings in Bangkok, at about 11:30 p.m. Eastern on Thursday, Kevin Warsh refused to forecast policy. “I don’t prejudge meetings,” he said, and he does not “believe in saying now what I might do at the meetings that we have in the balance of this year” (CNBC, Euronews, Investing.com). He said the economy had “broadly” developed as the Fed expected at its September meeting, when it raised rates a quarter point to 3.75–4.00%, its first increase since July 2023. Central banks around the world are engaged in “a synchronous tightening,” he said, and “that means there’s no free riders.” He called the path of AI “very bumpy”: it could make economies more productive, and the investment boom behind it is also adding to demand now.

The market had come into the week pricing a real chance of a second hike on Oct. 28. After Wednesday’s September CPI (core +0.3%) and Thursday’s producer prices (+1.3%, the largest monthly rise since March 2022), CME Group says the odds peaked at 53% and then fell below 30% as Treasuries rallied. Seeking Alpha puts them at about 27%. A Bloomberg reading relayed by investingLive has them at 15% after Warsh spoke; we have not seen that figure elsewhere. Goldman Sachs economists wrote that Warsh’s remarks, “alongside other recent comments from FOMC participants, indicate that the FOMC is set to pause in October,” and they still expect a second and final hike in December.

Bonds had already moved. On the official Treasury curve, the 10-year fell 9 basis points on Thursday to 5.10% and every maturity from 2 to 30 years fell 5–9 basis points, even though producer prices came in far above forecasts. On the official curve it closed as high as 5.31% on Oct. 5. Overnight it is reported down about another basis point, near 5.06–5.08%. The dollar index fell to 101.62 (−0.4%, Yahoo, 6:22), and FXStreet says traders took Warsh’s refusal as a reason to sell the dollar.

Why it matters The question for markets this month has been whether an oil shock that is now in producer prices forces the Fed to hike again before it can see the damage. A pause on Oct. 28 would take some pressure off the front end. Nothing Warsh said rules a hike out, and the data could change minds before the blackout lifts. Exposure, not direction: 2-year Treasuries, the dollar, regional banks (KRE), homebuilders, and long-duration growth stocks.
What’s next The quiet period starts Saturday, so no Fed official will speak on policy before the decision on Wednesday, Oct. 28. Import prices (8:30) and industrial production (9:15) are today’s data.

Asia bounces after the chip crash, but the AI-debt worry has not gone away

Seoul and Tokyo recovered part of Thursday’s losses. CoreWeave’s bonds and default swaps are priced for real distress, and the chip index has lost a fifth of its value since June.

Seoul’s Kospi closed up 2.05% at 5,668.05 on Friday, after its 7.57% fall on Thursday triggered a circuit breaker. Foreign investors stopped a run of eight straight days of net selling, AJU Press reports, but the index still lost about 14.5% this week, its worst week since October 2008. The Nikkei rose 1.39% to 65,324.29, the Taiex 0.30% and Hong Kong about 0.5% (Yahoo Finance closes). Indonesia was the exception: the Jakarta index fell about 11%, its biggest drop since 1998, after index provider MSCI said the market no longer merits emerging-market status (Reuters, AP).

In New York the selling has moved from the stocks into the debt. The Philadelphia semiconductor index fell 0.88% on Thursday to 11,581.22, its seventh straight decline, and is more than 20% below its June high, a bear market by the usual definition. SMH, the VanEck chip fund, closed at 558.84, 8% below its 20-day average. Six memory and storage makers have lost about $1.1 trillion in market value since Oct. 2, Benzinga calculates. CoreWeave, the GPU cloud company that borrows heavily to buy Nvidia chips, rebounded 5.9% to $69.69 on Thursday, but its 9.625% notes due 2032 traded near 78 cents on the dollar, a yield above 15% (Reuters), and its five-year credit-default swaps were around 1,000 basis points, which CNBC says implies about a 63% chance of default within five years. Chief executive Mike Intrator says the company’s backlog “more than supports all of our debt obligations.” Oracle, a big CoreWeave customer and borrower in its own right, fell about 9% on Wednesday and rose 3.0% on Thursday.

Analysts have offered several causes, and none is confirmed: Chinese memory maker CXMT’s new server-memory design, hotter US inflation reviving rate fears, and doubts about how the AI build-out is financed. Several outlets credit Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang with steadying Asia by saying Nvidia does not need to support CoreWeave. Unconfirmed We could not find that remark in a primary source.

Why it matters Credit markets usually turn before equity markets do. A company-specific problem at CoreWeave becomes a market problem only if lenders start charging every borrower building data centers more. The broad high-yield spread has widened from 3.03% on Oct. 6 to 3.25% on Oct. 14 (FRED), noticeable but modest. Exposure, not direction: CRWV, ORCL, NVDA, MU, SMH, the Korean and Taiwanese ETFs (EWY, EWT), and HYG.

Truist, M&T and Regions beat this morning; Truist takes a $1.4 billion securities loss to reset its balance sheet

The regional banks reporting before the bell are earning more than forecast, but Truist’s headline profit is cut in half as it sells low-yielding bonds and its LightStream consumer-loan business.

Truist reported third-quarter adjusted earnings of $1.16 a share, up 12% from a year earlier, against analyst estimates of about $1.05 (company 8-K filed at 6:31; AlphaStreet consensus). Reported earnings were $0.56 a share, after a $1.4 billion pre-tax loss on selling available-for-sale securities, which cost $0.90 a share. Adjusted revenue was a stable $5.3 billion, and the net interest margin rose 2 basis points to 3.00%. The bank said it signed an agreement on Oct. 14 to sell $7.7 billion of unsecured consumer loans, substantially all of its LightStream platform, to Carlyle, after selling its Regional Acceptance auto-loan book in the quarter. Net charge-offs rose to 0.77% of loans from 0.50%, which the release links to the loan sale.

M&T Bank earned $5.04 a share, or $5.08 on its net operating measure, against forecasts of about $4.93 (8-K). Regions earned $0.71 a share, or $0.73 adjusted, against about $0.64–0.67 (8-K). Citizens Financial reported $1.41 against about $1.38–1.39 and raised its dividend 13% to $0.52, according to its release as republished by StockTitan. Travelers was due before the open and had not reported when this edition went to press; forecasts range widely, from about $6.77 to $7.30 a share.

Why it matters Truist is doing what several large regionals did in 2024: take a one-time loss to swap old, low-yielding bonds for higher-yielding ones. That lifts future interest income at the cost of this quarter’s capital (its CET1 ratio slipped to 10.7%). Exposure, not direction: TFC, MTB, RF, CFG, KRE (+1.8% Thursday).

Three take-privates in a day: Proofpoint buys Varonis, GTCR buys CCC, the Penskes buy out Penske Automotive

About $29 billion in deals were announced or signed on Thursday, a sign private-equity money is still being put to work while public markets wobble.

Proofpoint, owned by Thoma Bravo, agreed to buy data-security company Varonis for $49.75 a share in cash, about $6.3 billion, with Ontario Teachers’ also investing (company release, 8-K). It is described as the largest cybersecurity deal of the year. Varonis closed Thursday at $47.34, below the offer, a typical gap for a deal not expected to close until the first half of 2027. A GTCR-led group including Elliott will take insurance-claims software maker CCC Intelligent Solutions private for $7.75 a share, about $6.1 billion, with closing expected in the first quarter (8-K, Insurance Journal). And Penske Automotive Group signed a definitive agreement to be taken private by Penske Corp. and Mitsui at $212 a share, raised from $210 in July, for an enterprise value of about $16.3 billion (8-K).

Why it matters Buyers are paying cash with long-term debt at 5% Treasury yields plus spread, so the deals are a statement that these businesses are worth more than public markets value them. Exposure: VRNS, CCC, PAG; software peers in cybersecurity.

A tanker burns in Hormuz as Washington tightens Iran sanctions and the IEA releases diesel

Brent is little changed near $105. The attacks continue, but the IEA’s stock release and talk of easing some tanker sanctions are working the other way.

Iran’s army said on Thursday, via state broadcaster IRIB, that a “violating” tanker that had switched off its tracking was on fire in the eastern part of the strait. Who hit it, and how, is not established. US Central Command says the US and its partners control the strait, that more than 40 merchant ships passed through in the previous 24 hours and that the main channel is free of mines; Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said on Wednesday the strait is “closed.” The two sides also give different causes for the sinking of the container ship GFS Pride: the Guard says it hit a mine, CENTCOM says an Iranian projectile. Kpler counts about 11.6 million barrels a day passing through the strait in the week to Oct. 13, about two-thirds of the pre-war level (all via CNN, Times of Israel, This is Beirut and search summaries of Kpler’s data).

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Thursday that sanctions will continue until Iran’s coffers are empty, as Treasury designated 16 more people and 10 entities and acted against an Iraqi bank. The International Energy Agency agreed to release more than 20 million barrels of diesel from member stocks (Reuters). President Trump told Newsmax the US would not use nuclear weapons against Iran and that he does not see ground troops being used. CNN, citing US officials, reports new strike plans on Iranian energy sites in case combat resumes; that is a reported plan, not an order, and Trump has said he will not start attacks before the Nov. 3 midterms.

Brent was $105.44 at about 6:22, down 0.5% from Thursday’s $105.92 settlement (Yahoo Finance). WTI is near $97, down about 0.6% (Reuters via CNBC), and up about 6% this week; the EIA reported an 8.6-million-barrel draw in US crude stocks. Goldman Sachs estimates a geopolitical risk premium of about $22 a barrel in Brent.

Why it matters Energy is what put producer prices up 1.3% last month (energy +10.8%), and it is the Fed’s main inflation problem. Diesel is the tightest product, so the IEA release is aimed at the right spot. Exposure, not direction: Brent, XLE (−1.6% Thursday), airlines, tanker owners, marine insurers.

The Justice Department sues California to stop the counting of some mail ballots

DOJ says about 20,000 people on California’s rolls are likely noncitizens. State and Los Angeles officials say they have seen no evidence and that the list arrived on a thumb drive they would not open.

The suit, filed Thursday in federal court in Los Angeles against Secretary of State Shirley Weber and Los Angeles County registrar Dean Logan, asks a judge to bar the counting of mail ballots from people the federal government calls likely noncitizens until their status is confirmed, or to order the state to verify citizenship before tabulating them (AP via PBS, CNN, ABC). It follows a 48-hour deadline in an Oct. 13 letter from Acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter, which DOJ says California missed. Logan says the encrypted drive came with a handwritten password and that his office did not open it under its security protocols. Weber says her office received no list and notes that nearly 16,000 names flagged in Nevada all turned out to be citizens. Attorney General Rob Bonta says the state will not withhold ballots. CNN calls the suit a long shot; a federal judge recently dismissed a separate DOJ demand for California’s full voter rolls.

What’s next No hearing date had been reported by this morning. California counts mail ballots that arrive up to seven days after the Nov. 3 election, so any ruling would have to come quickly to matter.

Ukraine ends its refinery-strike pause as Russia launches one of its biggest drone attacks

Kyiv says Russia never honored the energy truce. A Starlink speed limit on Ukraine’s military terminals has become a political fight, and SpaceX shares fell again.

President Zelensky told the Financial Times that Ukraine is “done” with the energy truce President Trump announced, and a drone attack set fire to Rosneft’s Ryazan refinery, about 5% of Russia’s refining capacity, the regional governor said. Vladimir Putin said there was no significant damage. Russia, for its part, sent what Zelensky called about 850 drones over roughly 24 hours; Ukraine’s air force reported 580 drones and two missiles in the main overnight wave, 536 of them shot down. Death tolls differ: at least 10 people were killed nationwide by some counts and 17 by others, including three emergency workers in the Kirovohrad region (Kyiv Independent, Meduza, ABC News).

Ukraine says the military Starshield terminals it relies on were limited to about 160 km/h without warning this week, disrupting interceptor drones. Elon Musk says Starshield is “owned and controlled by the U.S. government” and that the limits are set by the military and “ultimately POTUS”; CNBC reports service was partly restored on Thursday. Which side made the decision is disputed. SpaceX shares (SPCX) fell 2.3% to $148.62 on Thursday, their fourth straight loss, after being down about 6.5% in the morning (Yahoo Finance, Investing.com).

Why it matters Refinery strikes tighten the global diesel market at the same moment as the Gulf disruption, which is part of why the IEA is releasing diesel. Exposure, not direction: diesel cracks, European refiners, SPCX.

Commodities and crypto. Oil a little lower, metals higher: gold up 0.8%, silver and copper each about 2%. Copper rising alongside the Asian rebound is a mildly pro-growth signal. Bitcoin is flat for the week near $82,000, well below its 52-week high of $116,000.

Positioning. FINRA’s short-volume file for Thursday shows 74% of SMH volume marked short, against 48% across the market, 51% for SPY and 54% for QQQ. That counts market-maker hedging as well as bets against the chips, so it is not short interest, but it shows where the hedging was concentrated. The most fragile assumption. That the bond rally is about a Fed pause, not about growth. The tape is pricing a benign story: hike odds down, yields down, small caps and banks up, AI hardware cleaned out. If today’s import prices are hot (the oil shock is feeding straight through) or if CoreWeave’s debt keeps falling and other AI borrowers’ spreads follow, that story breaks. What would invalidate it: the 2-year back above 4.85%, or high-yield spreads through 3.40%.

The Economy

Inflation. The week’s three readings all point the same way. September CPI rose 0.6% on the month and 3.7% on the year, core 0.3% and 2.6% (BLS; FRED’s index levels, 336.298 and 338.896, confirm). Producer prices rose 1.3%, the largest monthly rise since March 2022, and 6.3% on the year, with energy up 10.8% and gasoline 20.3%; producer prices excluding food, energy and trade services rose a more moderate 0.5%. Those are rates of change: high prices are not the same as accelerating inflation, and the Fed’s question is whether the energy jump spreads to core. Today’s September import prices (8:30) will show how much reaches the border; forecasts collected by different outlets disagree on the consensus.

Activity. Industrial production for September comes at 9:15, with forecasts around +0.4% after a flat August (consensus as reported by Trading Economics and others; hurricane shut-ins in the Gulf may weigh on mining output). Retail sales rose 0.8% in September, but only 0.2% excluding autos and gasoline. Unemployment was 4.2% in September (from 4.1%) and payrolls rose 29,000 (FRED, preliminary). Weekly jobless claims have stayed below 200,000 for five straight weeks (199,000 in the week to Oct. 10), so employers are not firing even as hiring slows.

The Fed. Target range 3.75–4.00%. Decision Oct. 28; blackout from Saturday. The case for waiting, which Warsh did not argue against: core inflation is 2.6%, the labor market is cooling, and long yields have done some tightening for the Fed. The case for moving: producer prices and energy, and San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly’s view (Reuters) that more tightening is “more likely than not.”

AI & Technology

The FTC subpoenas OpenAI and Anthropic. The Federal Trade Commission has sent each company a civil investigative demand of more than 50 pages seeking records on security incidents, safeguards, marketing and privacy policies, Axios reported; an FTC spokesperson confirmed the demands to CBS News. It is an investigation, not a finding of wrongdoing. OpenAI says it will “engage constructively.”

Eight states sue OpenAI. Attorneys general from Iowa, Nebraska, Kansas, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana filed separate state-court suits on Wednesday alleging ChatGPT harms minors, mainly under consumer-protection law, and seek changes to the product, civil penalties and disgorgement. These are allegations; OpenAI points to its parental controls (Al Jazeera, Engadget).

Anthropic’s revenue. Reuters, citing one investor at a pre-IPO meeting, reports Anthropic’s third-quarter revenue at about $16.8 billion, up 45% from the second quarter; the figures are preliminary and the company has not confirmed them. Anthropic is private and has filed confidentially for an IPO, so there is no share price.

TSMC’s numbers in full. The company’s release shows 2-nanometre chips already 8% of wafer revenue and 3-nanometre 33%; earnings were NT$29.43 a share ($4.60 per ADR). The ADR fell 1.8% on Thursday to $422.25, on margin worries according to Investing.com.

Science

Battery chemistry. A study in Nature by Schrödinger, Columbia University and the New York Structural Biology Center uses large machine-learning simulations to show lithium ions clustering in common battery electrolytes, behavior that affects how fast batteries charge. It is peer-reviewed; our account rests on the company’s release, and it has not been independently replicated.

HIV treatment. A randomized trial in the New England Journal of Medicine found long-acting injectable HIV therapy matched daily pills in viral suppression at 48 weeks among African adults who struggle to take pills consistently, a group for whom an injection every few months could matter most.

Space

A Space Force launch. A Falcon 9 launched the classified USSF-481 mission from Vandenberg at 8:01 p.m. Eastern on Thursday, and the booster, on its 28th flight, landed on a drone ship (Launch Library, Spaceflight Now).

Artemis. NASA has finished stacking the twin solid boosters for Artemis III at Kennedy Space Center. That mission, now planned for 2027, will test docking between Orion and SpaceX and Blue Origin lander test articles in Earth orbit without landing; the first crewed landing is planned on Artemis IV in 2028.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

PRT wants to close the Wabash Tunnel for good on Dec. 1

Pittsburgh Regional Transit says the road tunnel between West Carson Street on the South Side and Mount Washington needs about $43 million of work, with its lighting, ventilation and electrical systems in “poor to critical condition.” About 1,500 private vehicles a day use it; no scheduled bus does, and the Wabash park-and-ride stays open. PRT says it does not plan to demolish the tunnel. The PRT board votes Oct. 23. For drivers from the South Hills, the alternatives into town are the Liberty Tunnel and the West End routes, which are busy enough already.
