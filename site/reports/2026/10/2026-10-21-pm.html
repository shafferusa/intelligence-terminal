Top Stories

Meta beats, narrows its spending range and plans a non-voting share class that works like a two-for-one split

Revenue rose 26% and topped the company’s own range. Free cash flow was negative $2.1 billion after $34.9 billion of capital spending. The stock was about 6% higher in late trading.

Meta Platforms reported third-quarter revenue of $64.60 billion, up 26% from a year earlier, in its 8-K filed at 4:03 PM. Its July forecast was $61–64 billion and the consensus about $63.3 billion. Ad impressions rose 10% and the average price per ad 14%. Net income was $9.86 billion, or $3.83 a diluted share. That includes a $9.89 billion charge for the August agreement with 52 state attorneys general over teen mental health, which provides for payments of up to about $18.6 billion. Excluding the charge, operating income would have been $22.88 billion and EPS $7.40, according to the company. Pre-report EPS estimates ranged from about $6.00 to $6.76.

Spending is the number investors were waiting for. Capital expenditures, including finance-lease principal, were $34.91 billion in the quarter. Operating cash flow was $32.79 billion, so free cash flow was negative $2.12 billion. Meta narrowed its 2026 capital-spending range to $135–145 billion from $130–145 billion, which raises the bottom of the range. It raised its 2026 expense range to $175–179 billion to include the legal charge. It gave no 2027 capex figure. It said it expects to “continue investing aggressively” and to “sustain strong overall revenue growth throughout 2027.” Fourth-quarter revenue guidance is $73–76 billion. Headcount fell 15% from a year earlier, to 66,775. Reality Labs losses are expected to narrow to about $15 billion next year.

The release also set up a new class of stock. The board, on the unanimous recommendation of a special committee of independent directors, approved a publicly listed, non-voting Class C share. Each existing Class A and Class B share will receive one Class C share as a dividend once a new certificate of incorporation takes effect, expected Jan. 19, 2027. Meta says the Class A price should then reflect “the effect of a two-for-one stock split.” Voting power does not change. The company gives three reasons: a currency for capital raising, pay and acquisitions; long-term value; and keeping Mark Zuckerberg in control, or as the filing puts it, continuing “the founder-led model.”

The stock closed at $697.52, up 0.5%, and was about $738, up 5.8%, at 4:45 PM on delayed Yahoo Finance data. The call was still going at press time.

Why it matters

The AI spending is hitting cash flow, and Meta is building a way to keep paying for it. Investors rewarded the revenue, but free cash flow is now negative. Non-voting stock lets Meta issue equity for capital, pay and acquisitions without diluting Zuckerberg’s votes. Supporters will call that flexibility; governance groups have long opposed share classes like this, and some will again. Sunday’s edition expected Meta to hold or raise its capex outlook (80–90%). It held the top and lifted the bottom.

What’s next

The call, and especially any 2027 capex range. A shareholder vote is not mentioned in the release; watch the amended charter and any challenges to it before January.

Sources: Meta Platforms Form 8-K, Exhibit 99.1 (primary, SEC EDGAR, 4:03 PM); Yahoo Finance (close EOD official; after-hours Delayed); consensus as reported by CNBC and Fortune.

SpaceX rises 7.8% and Tesla 3.4% on the day after the merger-committee news; Tesla’s own quarter beats on margins

SpaceX gave back some of its early gain but held most of it. Tesla’s results came out after the bell; its filing had not reached EDGAR when this edition went to press.

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX) closed at $159.31, up 7.79%, on about 217 million shares, more than 2.5 times its 20-day average volume. It had been up about 10% before the open. A day earlier the company reported revenue of $14.58 billion, up 161%, and Elon Musk said on the call that SpaceX and Tesla have each formed special committees of independent directors to consider combining. Nothing has been agreed and nothing has been filed about the committees. Tesla rose 3.41% to $386.47.

After the close, Tesla reported its third quarter. Electrek and Teslarati report revenue of about $28.05 billion, flat on a year ago and in line with the roughly $28.0 billion consensus, and adjusted EPS of $0.48 against about $0.44. They put automotive gross margin excluding regulatory credits at 18.1%, up from 16.3% last quarter and above estimates, and free cash flow at about $1.5 billion. GAAP net income fell sharply as spending on AI and the Optimus robot rose.

Unconfirmed We could not check Tesla’s figures against its 8-K, which had not appeared on EDGAR at press time, and one outlet published different EPS numbers. The shares were up about 1% at 4:45 PM. The call starts at 5:30 PM.

The 20-year bond auction tails by a basis point; yields barely move

Foreign and other indirect buyers took less than usual and direct bidders more. It was not the failure the 20-year’s premium to the 30-year had hinted at.

Treasury sold $13 billion of reopened 20-year bonds at a high yield of 5.469%, with a bid-to-cover ratio of 2.73 (TreasuryDirect results). The last 20-year sale cleared at 5.420%. Market reports put the when-issued yield just before the 1 PM deadline at 5.460%, a tail of 0.9 basis points against a recent average of about 0.2. Indirect bidders, a group that includes foreign central banks, took 58.2% against a 65.2% average, and direct bidders 31.6% against 22.9%, as reported by investingLive. Dealers were left with about 10%, by subtraction.

On Treasury’s end-of-day curve the 20-year was unchanged at 5.47% and the 30-year at 5.42%. The 10-year rose a basis point to 5.13% and the 2-year 2 to 4.81%. The 2-year to 10-year spread narrowed to 32 basis points. The AP reported that the 10-year briefly dipped to 5.09% in the morning.

Why it matters

The 20-year yields more than the 30-year, which usually signals weak demand for it. Today’s result was mediocre but orderly. Lighter indirect bidding is worth watching at the next long-bond sales. The next supply is Thursday’s $26 billion five-year TIPS sale.

Sources: TreasuryDirect auction results (primary); US Treasury daily par yield curve (EOD official, Oct 21); investingLive; Associated Press.

Crude stocks fall 3.1 million barrels, nearly four times the forecast, and the US says an Iranian projectile sank a container ship

Brent ended flat at $108.82 after trading above $110 overnight. The strategic reserve fell again, to about 282.7 million barrels.

Commercial crude inventories fell 3.06 million barrels in the week to Oct. 16, to 412.5 million, against a forecast draw of about 0.8 million, the Energy Information Administration reported at 10:30 AM. It was the third straight weekly draw. Gasoline stocks fell 2.95 million barrels and distillates rose 1.68 million. Stocks at the Cushing, Oklahoma hub rose about 1 million barrels. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve slipped to about 282.7 million barrels, according to secondary summaries of the release.

December Brent settled at $108.82, up 3 cents, and December WTI at $97.15, down 67 cents, according to CNBC. WTI’s apparent fall from Tuesday’s $101.79 is mostly a contract roll: Tuesday’s figure was the expiring November contract. Bloomberg’s market wrap said crude firmed late on concern that Middle East fighting could resume.

Disputed US Central Command said the container ship GFS Pride was hit by an Iranian projectile in the Strait of Hormuz and sank under tow, CBS News reports. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard says its mines struck ships on routes it calls illegal. Central Command calls the mine claims false. No independent account has been published, and we found only one detailed report of the sinking. Separately, Central Command says 86 commercial vessels have now been redirected under the US blockade of Iranian ports.

Why it matters

A draw this large while the strait is shut points to tightening US supply. It supports oil prices and keeps headline inflation, 3.7% in September, uncomfortable for the Fed a week before it meets.

Sources: EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report (as reported by Reuters, Newsquawk and investingLive); CNBC settlements; CBS News; Bloomberg; US Central Command statements as reported.

The Supreme Court leaves in place an order blocking the end of gender-affirming care in federal prisons

The unsigned order gave no reasons. Justices Thomas and Alito would have granted the government’s request.

The Supreme Court denied the administration’s emergency request to enforce a February 2026 Bureau of Prisons policy that would end hormone therapy, surgery and other accommodations for transgender federal inmates, according to SCOTUSblog, NBC News and Courthouse News. A preliminary injunction from U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth stays in force, and the D.C. Circuit had already declined to suspend it. About 1,000 transgender people are in federal custody. A Justice Department spokesperson said the order is not a ruling on the merits and that the appeal continues in the D.C. Circuit.

Supporters of the policy argue that taxpayers should not pay for these treatments in prison. Opponents argue the care is medically necessary and that the government must provide it to people in its custody. The case now returns to the appeals court.

Sources: SCOTUSblog; NBC News; Courthouse News; Newsweek.

Kremlin says it is ready for three-way talks with Ukraine and the US after mid-November

Steve Witkoff met Vladimir Putin for about three hours. Moscow called the proposals he brought of limited use; Zelensky said Ukraine could accept a freeze on the current line.

Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said Russia is prepared to resume trilateral talks with the United States and Ukraine after mid-November, possibly in Abu Dhabi, after Witkoff’s meeting with Putin in St. Petersburg (CNN; AP). A US official told Axios both sides had agreed to resume. Ushakov also said the proposals Witkoff carried from Ukraine and the European Union contained “little that was useful,” according to bne IntelliNews. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said talks would “take a long time.” Witkoff called his meetings in Oslo and St. Petersburg constructive. President Zelensky said Ukraine could accept a freeze on the current front line followed by US and European security guarantees (Reuters, as reported).

Russia freed Stephen Hubbard, 74, a retired Michigan teacher held since 2022 and the last American the State Department had designated as wrongfully detained there. President Trump said Putin released him at his request with no swap. CBS reported that Trump linked the release to holding off new sanctions.

Sources: CNN; Associated Press; Axios; bne IntelliNews; CBS News; NBC News; Reuters via Investing.com.

AI & Technology

The NSA tells organisations to patch two PHP flaws that AI tools found, or take systems offline. The flaws affect PHP, the language behind a large share of websites. According to Fortune, the security firm Calif found the first one in August using an OpenAI model, and Wiz found a related one using models from Google and OpenAI along with its own tools. The NSA said organisations that cannot update right away should take affected systems offline (The Hill). OpenAI says more disclosures are coming. Calif’s chief executive warned that attackers can also use AI to work backward from a patch to an exploit. Headlines saying “70% of the internet” are using OpenAI’s estimate of PHP’s share of websites; it is not an independent figure. Read-through: more budget for patching and security tools, and evidence that AI is speeding up both finding bugs and exploiting them.

Anthropic’s draft federal AI bill draws criticism. Anthropic has confirmed it is circulating draft legislation. Under it, the government could block a model’s release only on an imminent risk of more than 50 deaths or $1 billion in damage, and only with a judge’s approval. It would also override certain state laws. Critics say that bar is too high; reports say the company has promised to revise parts of it. Chief executive Dario Amodei posted a long defence of the company’s safety record, conceding that some decisions were mistakes. Congress is out until after the election.

Science

The Big Bang’s helium, measured to half a percent. Astronomers using the Large Binocular Telescope measured the primordial helium mass fraction at 0.2458 ± 0.0013, from 15 galaxies with very few heavy elements, in five papers in The Astrophysical Journal (University of Minnesota). The standard theory of how the first elements formed, using Planck satellite data, predicts 0.2467. The two agree. That leaves less room for unknown particles in the early universe. The university says the precision is three times better than before; an independent paper puts it at about twice.

Mars’ great canyon is still active. A team led from Imperial College London traced a marsquake recorded by NASA’s InSight lander to slip on a deep fault beneath Valles Marineris. The canyon formed billions of years ago, but its faults can still move today.

Sources: University of Minnesota via Newswise; ScienceDaily; Imperial College London; Phys.org.

A bill closing a loophole in the open-meetings law heads to Shapiro
