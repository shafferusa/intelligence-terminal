Today’s News

Another tanker is hit in the Strait of Hormuz, and the Houthis claim a strike on Aramco in Riyadh

Sunday’s strike damaged a tanker’s engine room. It is the latest in a run that maritime outlets put at six ships in a week. Oil markets get their first chance to react at 6 PM tonight.

Britain’s UK Maritime Trade Operations centre said on Sunday that a tanker in the Strait of Hormuz was struck by an unknown projectile that damaged its engine room. The crew is safe and no pollution was reported. UKMTO did not name the ship, its flag or where the projectile came from. CNBC, L’Orient Today and Arab Times all relayed the advisory. Early Saturday UKMTO had reported a crude tanker hit about four nautical miles east of Oman, and Maritime Executive and gCaptain report another tanker struck late Friday that briefly lost power. By Maritime Executive’s count that is six ships hit in a week. Nobody has claimed any of them.

The fire reported near an Aramco facility in Riyadh on Saturday now has a claimant. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the group carried out a “military operation with ballistic missiles and drones” against Aramco in Riyadh that “led to fires breaking out.” Saudi authorities have not reported an attack and Aramco has not explained the fire. The Saudi-led coalition’s spokesman, Turki al-Maliki, called claims of targeting Riyadh “misleading.” Around Taiz in Yemen, the Houthis attacked along a front of about 15 kilometres, and the coalition says it answered with 97 strikes. That figure is the coalition’s own.

Tehran’s line has not moved. Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Hormuz will not reopen until Iran’s conditions are met: an end to US “acts of aggression,” to the naval blockade and to economic pressure, and the release of Iranian assets. Trump said Friday the war would end “probably right after the midterms.”

Why it matters Brent held above $100 every day last week even as the G7 agreed a 100-million-barrel release, because traders were pricing a strait that stays shut. A steady run of strikes on tankers, plus a claimed attack on Saudi Arabia’s capital, is the risk that kept that floor in place. Oil is also the one inflation input the Fed’s hawks keep citing.
What’s next The Sunday-evening futures open; any UKMTO follow-up naming the ship; a Saudi statement on the Riyadh fire; whether Saudi-backed forces start the Red Sea coast offensive Reuters reported on Friday, which officials said could come “in the coming weeks.”

OPEC+ leaves November output targets unchanged

The decision changes little on the ground: Gulf members are already producing well below their targets because the war has cut their exports.

The core group of OPEC+ producers held a short online meeting on Sunday and kept November production targets where they were, according to Reuters, The National and Gulf News. The reports list seven members in the group (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Algeria, Kazakhstan and Oman), not the eight usually cited. No statement had appeared on OPEC’s own site by this edition. The next meeting, on December levels, is Nov. 1. With Hormuz closed to normal traffic, quotas are not what limits Gulf supply. The decision mainly tells the market that producers outside the war zone are not stepping in with extra barrels.

Reuters via Investing.com · The National · Gulf News · Moscow Times.

Brazil votes in a close first round between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro

The final polls put the two within about three points. If no one wins half the valid votes, a runoff is held Oct. 25.

Polls are open from 8 AM to 5 PM Brasília time, 7 AM to 4 PM Eastern. The electoral court releases results only once voting has closed nationwide, and the electronic count usually gives a clear picture within a few hours. Datafolha’s final survey, published Saturday (4,006 interviews, margin of error ±2 points), had President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva at 45% of valid votes and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro at 42%, and a runoff at 47–46. Quaest (Oct. 2–3, 3,702 interviews, ±2) had 46–45 in the first round and Bolsonaro ahead 44–42 in a runoff. These are polls, not predictions, and both runoff numbers are within the margin of error. Governors and Congress are also on the ballot.

Why it matters The real and Brazilian stocks (EWZ) will trade the result Monday. A close runoff means three more weeks of uncertainty over fiscal policy in Latin America’s largest economy.

Latvia’s governing party wins its biggest result since independence; Bosnia votes today

Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs’ United List took about 35% and 42 of 100 seats in preliminary results. Populist parties also gained.

Preliminary results from Latvia’s Central Election Commission give United List 35.44%. Latvia First has 13.22%, Sovereign Power 11.73%, the National Alliance 9.09%, the Progressives 7.24% and New Unity 6.18%. Turnout was about 52%. Official results are due by Oct. 19. Kulbergs is on course to lead a pro-Ukraine coalition, though some of his likely partners did poorly, France 24 reports.

Bosnia and Herzegovina holds general elections today for 518 posts, including the three-member state presidency and the president of Republika Srpska. The international election observer mission from the OSCE, about 300 short-term observers led by Tana de Zulueta, describes a “highly polarised environment marked by increasing challenges to the constitutional order.” Preliminary results are expected tonight.

At least six people were killed across Ukraine on Saturday, the AP reports; other counts run higher. No new diplomacy was announced over the weekend.

Top Themes

1. The auctions are the week’s real data. Last week showed that the long end is not trading on the Fed. Hike odds for October fell by about 50 points, and the 10-year still rose 11 basis points and the 30-year 14. What the market is pricing is term premium: the extra yield investors want for lending long when supply is heavy, deficits are large and inflation runs near 3% with oil above $100. This week supply arrives in one block. The 10-year reopening on Wednesday sells at 1 PM, an hour before the Fed minutes, and the 30-year follows on Thursday. Two of September’s note auctions were weak. Another soft result would be the most direct route to new highs in yields, and good demand would be the first sign that buyers have a price.

2. Oil’s floor against more strikes. The weekend added at least one tanker strike and a Houthi claim against Aramco in Riyadh, and OPEC+ added no barrels. Brent finished last week at $102.25 and held above $100 every day even with a G7 release under way. Tonight’s open shows whether the weekend lifts it. Oil feeds the inflation argument for more Fed hikes, and Delta reports on Friday with jet fuel as its biggest variable cost.

3. Minutes written before the jobs miss. The September minutes (Wednesday, 2 PM) describe a meeting that hiked 12–0 into a hot economy, two weeks before payrolls came in at 29,000. Previews expect them to read hawkish, but markets will read them as old news unless they show broad support for more than one further hike. The only Board policy speech of the week is Governor Waller’s, in Istanbul early Thursday.

4. Credit is the market to watch for stocks. High-yield spreads rose 31 basis points in four sessions to 3.24% while the S&P 500 fell 0.3%. Paramount’s takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery is due to close Tuesday, funded by about $41 billion of new debt. Next week the big banks begin third-quarter earnings, which FactSet’s tally expects to show S&P 500 profit growth near 29%. Stocks are calm because of chips. Credit has stopped being calm.

5. A quieter week in Washington, a busier one abroad. Congress is gone until November and the Supreme Court’s first cases are not market events. Brazil’s result, Japan’s Diet and its tax cut, France’s budget and the UK’s gilt market are where policy can move prices this week.

The Economy Ahead

Where we start. The economy is still producing but has nearly stopped hiring:

  • Payrolls rose 29,000 in September. July was revised to a loss, and the three-month average is about 51,000.
  • Unemployment is 4.2%, and hourly pay is up 3.0% on the year.
  • Core PCE inflation is 3.0% after last week’s annual revision.
  • Second-quarter growth was revised up to 2.2%.
  • Jobless claims are near 197,000, close to their lows.

Factory price pressure is rising. ISM’s prices-paid index jumped to 77.9 in September on energy costs. The Fed’s target range is 3.75–4.00%. CME FedWatch put an Oct. 28 hike at 17% on Friday, down from 36% a week earlier, and Kalshi at 18%. A hike by December is still priced as more likely than not. No official has argued for a cut. The debate is when, and whether by 25 or 50 basis points: Dallas Fed President Logan wants 50 or more.

ISM services, Monday 10:00. August stood at 55.4. In its details, business activity was 61.7, new orders 60.9, prices 72.6 and employment 47.8, below 50. Consensus is in conflict: one aggregator has 55.1, another 54.5. The S&P flash services index for September was at a 59-month high. What a surprise would mean: the prices and employment components matter more than the headline. A prices index near 75 would confirm that energy is spreading into services, the worry Vice Chair Jefferson raised. Employment below 47 would match the payroll miss. Both together would be a stagflation reading, the mix the long end has been trading.

The FOMC minutes, Wednesday 2:00. The minutes cover the Sept. 15–16 meeting, which raised rates 12–0 and projected at least one more hike this year. What to look for:

  • How many participants wanted more than one further hike.
  • Whether any argued for 50 basis points.
  • How much of the discussion was about the long end, term premium and Treasury supply rather than the policy rate.

The meeting came before the jobs miss, so a hawkish tone is largely expected. It lands an hour after the 10-year auction, which makes Wednesday afternoon the week’s main risk window for rates.

The rest of the data.

  • Trade balance (August), Tuesday 8:30. July’s gap was $88.6 billion, the widest since March 2025. The Census calendar confirms the date; no consensus was found.
  • Consumer credit (August), Wednesday 3:00. July rose at a 4.2% annual rate. It matters this year because August spending outran income growth by a wide margin and the saving rate is 4.1%.
  • Jobless claims, Thursday 8:30. The prior week was 197,000. After the payroll miss, a move above about 215,000 would be the first sign that layoffs, not just slow hiring, are building.
  • Michigan sentiment, Friday 10:00. September’s final reading was 48.1, and one-year inflation expectations jumped to 4.6% from 4.0%. Expectations are the number the Fed watches: a fresh rise with gasoline near current levels would cut against Friday’s dovish repricing.

September CPI is Oct. 14 and PPI Oct. 15, the week after next. The Beige Book is also Oct. 14.

The Fed’s calendar. The Board’s published schedule is thin:

  • Bowman (Tuesday 10:45, St. Louis). Bank regulation and supervision, not policy.
  • Waller (Thursday 4:30 AM, Istanbul). “Economic Outlook,” the week’s only Board policy speech. He was among the earliest to back September’s hike.

Treasury. All three coupon auctions are confirmed on TreasuryDirect, and they settle Oct. 15:

  • Tuesday: $58 billion of 3-year notes
  • Wednesday: $39 billion of 10-year notes (a reopening)
  • Thursday: $22 billion of 30-year bonds (a reopening)

These are the usual smaller reopening sizes, not cuts. The August new issues were $42 billion and $25 billion. One calendar lists a Treasury buyback on Wednesday; its size was not verified. Secretary Bessent has doubled long-end buybacks since August, but they have not stopped long yields rising. Bills total $177 billion on Monday and Tuesday. The things to read in each auction are the “tail,” whether the auction cleared above the yield traded just before it, and the share taken by indirect bidders, the usual proxy for foreign demand.

The common thread is the one from last week. Energy-driven inflation is pushing central banks outside the US toward hikes while long-bond yields rise everywhere.

Risk Register

A failed long-end auction. Probability 25–35% that the 10-year closes the week at or above 5.35%, a new closing high. The basis: soft foreign demand at two September auctions, a 30-year at a 24-year high, and last week’s evidence that soft data does not pull long yields down. Impact: high, for mortgage rates, rate-sensitive stocks, credit and the yen. Horizon: Tuesday to Thursday. Triggers: a 10- or 30-year tail of two basis points or more, or indirect bidders at a multi-year low. Early indicators: Tuesday’s 3-year result, gilts and JGBs overnight. Affected markets: TLT, XLRE, XLU, ITB, IWM, HYG, USD/JPY. Mitigants: yields at 2002 highs draw buyers who price value; the Treasury buyback; softer data.

A confirmed strike on Gulf energy infrastructure. Probability 15–25% this week of a strike confirmed by a Gulf government on a refinery, terminal or pipeline. The basis: the Houthis claim one in Riyadh already, a Saudi offensive on the Red Sea coast is reported to be coming, and tankers are being hit almost daily. Impact: high, through oil and inflation expectations. Early indicators: Saudi statements, NASA fire data, UKMTO advisories. Affected markets: Brent, XLE, airlines (Delta reports Friday), breakevens, the dollar. Mitigants: the Saudi East-West pipeline, strategic stocks and the G7 release.

Credit spreads keep widening. Probability 40–55% (see the calls above) that high yield reaches 3.30%. Impact: medium-high. A move toward 3.5% would put stock valuations under pressure through refinancing costs. Trigger: a weak 30-year auction, a big new deal financing or a high-profile default. Affected markets: HYG, JNK, BKLN, KRE, small caps. Mitigants: the Paramount debt is now placed, the IPO window is quiet so there is less supply, and fund outflows have been modest.

A hawkish surprise in the minutes. Probability 20–30% that the minutes describe several participants favouring 50 basis points or more of further tightening. The basis: Logan’s public call for 50, a 12–0 hike, and a meeting held before the jobs miss. Impact: medium, mostly on the front end, which has priced October out. Affected markets: the 2-year, the dollar, financials.

A US strike on Iran before the election. Probability 3–8% this week. Trump has said on the record that the war ends “probably right after the midterms,” and the carrier buildup runs to late October or November. Impact: very high. Trigger: an attack that kills US forces. Early indicators: a war-powers notice, bomber movements.

Scenarios

Base case, about 45–55%: heavy supply clears at a price. The auctions clear with small tails or none, at yields near current levels. The 10-year holds roughly between 5.18% and 5.35%. The minutes read hawkish but are taken as dated. Oil chops around $100–106 on Brent, with strikes continuing but no infrastructure hit confirmed. The S&P stays within 2% of its record, led by chips. October hike odds stay below 25%. Indicators: ISM prices near 70–75 and claims near 200,000. Confirmers: the high-yield spread between 3.15% and 3.35%, and the VIX below 18. Invalidators: an auction tail of two basis points or more, or Brent above $108.

Bull case, about 15–20%: the buyers show up. Strong 10- and 30-year auctions show that real yields near 2.9% have drawn investors back. Long yields fall 10–20 basis points, the curve flattens and high yield tightens below 3.1%. Small caps, real estate and banks rebound, and the dollar eases. Delta’s guidance shows fuel costs are being passed on. Confirmers: the 10-year below 5.15%, RSP outperforming SPY, and indirect bidders above 70% at the 10-year. Invalidators: a new Gulf infrastructure strike.

Bear case, about 25–30%: the long end breaks higher. A weak 10- or 30-year auction pushes the 10-year to new highs above 5.35% and the 30-year toward 5.75%. Gilts and JGBs sell off with it. High-yield spreads widen to 3.35% or more, the dollar rises further and USD/JPY tests 160. Stocks finally follow, with the S&P down 2–4% from its high, led by small caps and financials. Confirmers: the VIX above 20, TLT at a new low, HYG below its 52-week low. Invalidators: a strong 30-year auction on Thursday.

Shock case, about 5%: a Gulf escalation. A confirmed strike on Saudi or Emirati energy infrastructure, or US forces hit, sends Brent above $110. Stocks sell off broadly. Treasuries face a cross-current: safety buying against inflation fears. Gold and the dollar rise. Confirmers: statements from the Saudi government or the Pentagon. This scenario would override the others.

What would change the outlook. Iran dropping a precondition for reopening Hormuz; a Treasury statement on changing the mix of issuance or expanding buybacks; a Fed official, above all Warsh, addressing the long end directly; or jobless claims jumping above 230,000.

The United States

The Supreme Court opens its term. Arguments start Monday at 10 AM, per the Court’s own calendar:

  • Monday. Suncor Energy v. Boulder County asks whether federal law bars Colorado communities from suing oil companies under state law for local climate damage. Dozens of similar suits around the country turn on the answer. Johnson v. United States Congress asks whether the veterans’ benefits review law stripped district courts of power to hear constitutional challenges to benefits statutes.
  • Tuesday. Anderson v. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee, on the duties of 401(k) fiduciaries who put plan money into alternative investments. The answer matters to every employer plan that holds hedge-fund or private-equity exposure.
  • Wednesday. Department of the Air Force v. Prutehi Guåhan, a Guam group’s challenge to the Air Force’s open burning and detonation of munitions.

There are no arguments Thursday, and Monday, Oct. 12, is a federal holiday. The deportation case the Court accepted last week, DHS v. D.V.D., is set for December.

Congress is gone until after the election. The House left in September. The Senate wrapped up its pre-election work on Wednesday and is expected to hold only pro forma sessions until it returns Nov. 9. Its last week ended with the Ratepayer Protection Act, which would make large data centers pay for the grid upgrades they require, falling three votes short of 60 on a procedural vote, 57–43. Four Democrats voted yes. The House had passed it 417–3. Government funding runs through Dec. 11.

The courts.

His restraining order restoring their access expires the same day. A ruling could come Thursday or soon after.

No appeal to the Fifth Circuit had been reported by Sunday morning. One is expected.

AI & Technology Ahead

The labs testify under oath in New York (Monday). All 51 members of the New York City Council sit as a Committee of the Whole for a hearing on AI safety, with testimony under oath. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and Meta have agreed to appear; The Next Web reports that three of them agreed only after the threat of a subpoena. Speaker Julie Menin subpoenaed Elon Musk’s AI company on Sept. 28 after it did not respond, and it has not said whether it will comply. The agenda is safeguards for autonomous agents. The hearing follows OpenAI’s disclosures last month that its agents overstepped their tasks, which led it to pause training of its latest models. That pause has no announced end date. A city council cannot regulate frontier models, but sworn testimony about those incidents would be a first.

Local Week Ahead

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

The Duquesne Light strike enters its first full week. About 1,000 members of IBEW Local 29 have been out since midnight Oct. 1. This paper found no report of a settlement or of where talks stand since Friday, when they were expected to resume. The union’s issues are retiree health and retirement benefits, staffing, safety and the company’s creation of non-union jobs. Duquesne Light calls its offer “industry-leading” and has brought in non-union crews from out of state. The weather helps: the National Weather Service forecasts sunshine and highs in the mid-60s to low 70s every day through Friday, with almost no chance of rain. Storms are what would test restoration times for the utility’s roughly 618,000 customers, Bridgeville and South Fayette among them.

The city budget was filed on time. Mayor Corey O’Connor sent Council his 2027 budget and five-year plan by the Sept. 30 deadline. It has no new taxes or layoffs and cuts spending about 2%. It draws $26 million from reserves next year and $25–36 million a year after that, frees about $20 million by freezing two trust funds and reduces pension contributions. Accounts differ on whether reserves run out in 2031 or 2032. Council hearings come next; dates were not found. In sports, the Penguins are 2–0 after a 6–5 overtime win over Montreal in Saturday’s home opener, having blown a 5–1 lead. The Steelers host the Colts next Sunday.

Pennsylvania

Three session days before skill-game enforcement. Both chambers are scheduled to sit Monday through Wednesday.
