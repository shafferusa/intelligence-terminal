How to Measure a Speed That Never Stands Still

Friday, October 9, 2026 · 13 min read

A speedometer claims to tell you your speed right now, at this exact instant, even though speed is ordinarily a ratio of distance to time — and at a single instant, no time has passed to divide by; the derivative, built directly from yesterday’s limit, is the precise idea that makes that claim true rather than a convenient fiction.

The Big Idea

Here is a question that sounds too simple to be a trap, and is one anyway. Your car’s speedometer says 60 miles per hour. But speed, by its ordinary definition, is distance traveled divided by time elapsed — and the speedometer is reporting a reading for this exact instant, not for some stretch of road you just covered. At a single instant, zero time has elapsed and zero distance has been covered. Zero divided by zero is not sixty; it is not anything. So what, precisely, is the speedometer measuring?

The answer is the same move yesterday’s lesson made formal: stop demanding the ratio at the instant, which is a dead end, and ask instead what the ratio approaches as you measure over shorter and shorter stretches of time surrounding that instant — a second, a tenth of a second, a thousandth — without ever actually letting the stretch reach zero. That approached value is called the derivative, and a speedometer is, in effect, a machine that estimates one in real time. It is the slope of a curve at one single point, measured exactly, not approximated, and it is built from nothing but the limit you already have in hand.

The derivative is not only about speed. It is the general answer to “how fast is this quantity changing, right now,” for any quantity that changes smoothly — a population, a temperature, a bank balance, the concentration of a chemical mid-reaction. That generality, more than any one application, is why this single idea from the 1660s and 1670s still sits underneath an enormous share of modern science, engineering, and quantitative reasoning.

How It Works

Start where yesterday’s revenue example already left off, because it did almost all of the work. There, the quantity R(t) = 3t² had an average growth rate over any stretch of time — change in R divided by change in t — and the question was what that average approached as the stretch, called h, shrank toward zero. That shrinking-stretch limit, computed for one specific function at one specific instant, is exactly what the derivative generalizes to any function, at any point.

Formally: start with two points on the curve of a function f, a distance h apart along the input axis — the points (x, f(x)) and (x + h, f(x + h)). The line connecting them is called a secant line, and its slope, [f(x + h) − f(x)] / h, is called the difference quotient — it is the average rate of change of f over that stretch of length h, in exactly the same shape as “rise over run” from ordinary slope. Now shrink h toward zero. Geometrically, the second point slides along the curve back toward the first, and the secant line connecting them rotates, approaching a limiting position: the tangent line, the line that touches the curve at exactly one point and matches its direction there, rather than cutting across it. The slope of that tangent line — the limit of the secant’s slope as h shrinks to zero, provided the limit exists — is the derivative of f at x, written f′(x).

That one operation answers several questions at once, depending only on what the original function represents. If f(t) is an object’s position at time t, f′(t) is its instantaneous velocity — precisely the speedometer question from the Big Idea. If f(t) is velocity, f′(t) is instantaneous acceleration. If f(x) is the total cost of producing x units of something, f′(x) is the marginal cost: roughly, the cost of producing one more unit right at that output level. The mathematics never changes; only the label on what f measures does. Finding a derivative, or working with the general rules for doing so, is called differentiation.

Computing a derivative directly from the limit definition every time, as done above, is called differentiating “from first principles,” and it works for any function where the limit exists — but it is slow, and doing it line by line for every function you ever meet would make calculus impractical. What makes it practical is that first principles, applied once to a whole family of functions (powers of x, for instance), produces a reusable shortcut — a pattern proven once and reused forever rather than re-derived each time. The Equation / Model section below carries out exactly that proof for the simplest case, x², and the pattern it reveals is the seed of the fuller toolkit of differentiation rules this subject will keep building on.

One more thing is worth knowing before you meet a derivative in the wild: it goes by three different-looking notations, and all three mean precisely the same thing. Isaac Newton, who developed his version of this machinery privately in the 1660s without publishing it at the time, wrote a derivative with respect to time as a dot over the letter — ẏ for the derivative of y — a notation still favored in physics for exactly that reason, rates of change over time. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who developed an independent version of calculus roughly a decade later, in the mid-1670s, and published his account of it first, in 1684, sketched a different-looking notation in his manuscripts that treated the derivative as a ratio of two infinitesimally small differences — dy/dx — a form that spread through print mainly via Leibniz’s students and collaborators, the Bernoulli brothers and Guillaume de l’Hôpital, in the 1690s. That dy/dx notation is the one most useful when the units themselves need tracking, which is why engineers and physicists reach for it constantly. More than a century later, in 1797, the mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange popularized a third option in his book Théorie des fonctions analytiques — the plain prime mark, f′(x), sometimes credited to Leonhard Euler before him — and it is from that book’s own name for these quantities, “fonctions dérivées,” that the modern word derivative itself descends. Newton and Leibniz each thought the other had stolen the idea; a Royal Society investigation in 1712 formally sided with Newton, but its conclusion was written by Newton himself, in his capacity as the Society’s president at the time — and most historians today treat the two as independent discoverers who happened to arrive within a decade of each other.

Key Concepts

Derivative
The instantaneous rate of change of a function at a point — the limit of its average rate of change over shrinking intervals as the interval length approaches zero. Written f′(x).
Instantaneous rate of change
How fast a quantity is changing at one exact point or moment, as opposed to its average rate of change over some stretch — the thing a derivative measures.
Tangent line
The line that touches a curve at exactly one point and matches the curve’s direction there; its slope is the derivative at that point.
Difference quotient
[f(x + h) − f(x)] / h — the average rate of change of f over an interval of length h; the derivative is this quotient’s limit as h approaches zero.
Differentiation
The process, or operation, of finding a derivative, whether from the limit definition directly or using a proven shortcut rule.

Example / Application

Return to the speedometer question with real numbers. Drop an object from rest near Earth’s surface, ignoring air resistance. Its height fallen after t seconds is given by s(t) = 4.9t² meters — a figure built from the standard acceleration of gravity, about 9.8 meters per second squared, halved, a relationship the next lesson’s companion idea, the integral, will explain the origin of. The question: what is the object’s instantaneous velocity at exactly t = 3 seconds — not its average speed over some interval containing that instant, but its speed at that precise moment?

Apply the difference-quotient definition directly at x = 3, with s in place of f:

The pattern closes in on 29.4, and algebra confirms it exactly, the same way yesterday’s revenue table did: [s(3+h) − s(3)] / h = [4.9(3+h)² − 4.9(3)²] / h = 4.9[(9 + 6h + h²) − 9] / h = 4.9(6h + h²) / h = 4.9(6 + h) = 29.4 + 4.9h, for any h other than zero. As h shrinks toward 0, that expression closes in on 29.4 with no ambiguity left. The object is falling at exactly 29.4 meters per second — about 66 miles per hour — at the single instant t = 3, even though no actual stretch of time, however short, was used to arrive at that number. That is the derivative s′(3) = 29.4, and it is the same calculation, on a different function, that a car’s speedometer is built to approximate continuously as you drive.

Swap the function and the label changes, not the mathematics. If C(x) were the total cost in dollars of producing x units of something instead of a fall height in meters, this exact same shrinking-interval calculation applied to C would give C′(x): the marginal cost at that output level, roughly what the next unit costs to produce. A falling object and a factory have nothing in common except that both are described by a function that changes smoothly — and that is all the derivative ever actually needs.

Equation / Model

Here is the definition itself, built from yesterday’s limit with nothing extra added:

f′(x) = limh→0 [f(x + h) − f(x)] / h

f prime of x equals the limit, as h approaches zero, of the quantity f of x plus h, minus f of x, all divided by h.

f(x)
the original function, whose instantaneous rate of change is being measured at the point x
f′(x)
the derivative of f at x — the instantaneous rate of change, or the slope of the tangent line, at that point
h
the length of the shrinking interval between x and the second point used to form the secant line; never actually equal to zero, only approaching it
[f(x+h) − f(x)] / h
the difference quotient — the average rate of change of f over the interval from x to x + h, i.e. the slope of the secant line through those two points

That reads as pure definition, so it is worth watching it actually produce a reusable rule, the way the lesson promised above. Apply it to f(x) = x², leaving x general rather than fixing it at a number:

f′(x) = limh→0 [(x+h)² − x²] / h = limh→0 (2x + h) = 2x

f prime of x equals the limit, as h approaches zero, of the quantity x plus h, squared, minus x squared, all divided by h — which equals the limit, as h approaches zero, of 2x plus h — which equals 2x.

The decisive step is the middle one. Expanding (x + h)² gives x² + 2xh + h², so the numerator (x + h)² − x² simplifies to 2xh + h², in which every term already carries a factor of h — dividing that whole numerator by h leaves 2x + h, exactly, for any h that is not itself zero (the same legitimate cancellation yesterday’s lesson used, since h is approaching zero but is never actually equal to it). Only after that cancellation is it safe to let h shrink to zero, at which point 2x + h simply closes in on 2x, with no 0/0 anywhere in sight. The result, f′(x) = 2x, is not a one-off fact about one function; it is a proven rule good for every input x, derived once and reusable forever. It also checks directly against yesterday’s own arithmetic: for R(t) = 3t², this same pattern gives R′(t) = 6t, and R′(4) = 24 — the exact $24,000-per-year figure yesterday’s table closed in on by brute force, now produced in one line instead of a table of shrinking intervals.

Run the identical first-principles argument on x³ instead of x², and the same cancellation trick produces f′(x) = 3x²; run it on x raised to the fourth power and it produces 4x³. The pattern staring back — bring the exponent down in front, then reduce it by one — is the power rule, one of the first general shortcuts this block’s later lessons will put to proper use, so that differentiating x raised to the 199th power no longer requires expanding a 199-term binomial by hand.

Why It Matters

The derivative is the mathematical language almost every quantitative field reaches for the moment a quantity changes over time or in response to something else, and the reader asks “how fast, right now.” A physicist writing Newton’s laws of motion is writing derivatives of position. An economist describing marginal cost or marginal utility is describing a derivative of a cost or satisfaction function. An epidemiologist asking whether an outbreak is accelerating or slowing is asking about the derivative of a case count. None of those fields needed to reinvent the idea; they all borrowed the same tool built here.

Modern artificial intelligence leans on the same idea at enormous scale. Training a neural network means adjusting millions, sometimes billions, of internal numbers so a single quantity — a loss function, measuring how wrong the network’s current guesses are — gets smaller. The standard method for deciding which direction to nudge each number, called gradient descent, works by computing the derivative of that loss with respect to each parameter and stepping in whichever direction decreases it fastest. Because a network is really one enormous chain of simpler functions stacked on top of each other, computing all of those derivatives efficiently requires repeatedly applying the chain rule — a differentiation rule built directly from today’s own limit definition, though this block has not yet derived it. The algorithm that applies it systematically across an entire network is called backpropagation; underneath the name, it is nothing more exotic than derivatives of derivatives, chained together exactly the way this lesson chains a shrinking interval into a slope.

One particular use is worth planting now, even though this subject will not build it out in full: wherever a smooth curve reaches a peak or a valley — a maximum profit, a minimum cost, the top of a thrown ball’s arc — the tangent line at that exact point is momentarily flat, meaning the derivative there equals zero. That single observation, that locating the best or worst case of something smooth means finding where its rate of change vanishes, turns the derivative from a description of motion into a tool for optimization, one of the most widely used applications of calculus anywhere outside pure mathematics.

Within this subject, today’s idea points directly at tomorrow’s. Day 11 takes up the reverse question: given a rate of change, how do you recover the total amount accumulated? That is the integral, and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus proves that integration and differentiation are, in a precise sense, inverse operations of each other — two faces of the same limit-built machine this lesson and yesterday’s assembled together.

Remember These
  • A derivative is the limit of a difference quotient — the average rate of change over an interval — as that interval shrinks to zero; it is never computed by setting the interval to zero directly.
  • Geometrically, the derivative is the slope of the tangent line at a point; physically, it is the instantaneous rate of change of whatever the function represents (velocity, marginal cost, and so on).
  • The same operation, differentiation, answers every “how fast, right now” question — only the label on the function changes between fields.
  • Three notations — Newton’s dot, Leibniz’s dy/dx, and Lagrange’s prime, f′(x) — all mean exactly the same derivative; Lagrange’s own name for it is where the word “derivative” itself comes from.
  • Proving a derivative rule once for a whole family of functions, as with x² above, replaces re-deriving it from scratch every time — the foundation of calculus’s larger rulebook.
Think About It

The derivative’s definition only works if the secant line's slope approaches the same value whether h shrinks toward zero from the positive side or the negative side — approaching the point from the right or from the left. Picture the graph of f(x) = |x|, the absolute value function, which forms a sharp V-shaped corner at x = 0. What do you think happens to the secant line's slope as h shrinks to zero from the right at that corner, versus from the left? Based on that, does this function have a derivative at x = 0 at all, and what does that tell you about whether every continuous function — one with no jumps or holes — is automatically differentiable too?
