Weekly Review

Alphabet and Intel Sink as the AI Sell-Off Spreads From the Spenders to the Suppliers

Saturday, October 31, 2026 · 47 min read

In the week of Oct. 26–30 investors marked down the companies paying for AI with borrowed money, then the companies selling them chips, while the Fed held with two officials pushing for a hike, Treasuries ended their best month since 2023, and oil stayed above $100 on an unresolved Strait of Hormuz going into the midterms.

The Brief

If you read nothing else

  • The week in markets. The S&P 500 fell 0.57% to 7,580.07 and the Nasdaq 2.00% to 25,437.00, their third straight weekly loss, the longest since March. The Russell 2000 rose 1.29% and the equal-weight S&P 0.82%. Almost all of the damage came Friday (S&P −1.82%), when Intel fell 28.6%, its worst day since 1974.
  • The AI line. Alphabet lost 12.6% on Thursday, its worst day since its 2004 listing, after a second quarter of negative free cash flow. Microsoft, which pays for its build-out from operating cash, ended the week almost flat. Investors are rewarding AI spending paid for out of operating cash and punishing spending paid for with new stock and debt.
  • The Fed and the bond market. The Fed held at 3.75–4.00% on a 10–2 vote, with both dissenters wanting a hike. Treasuries rallied anyway: the 10-year ended at 4.94%, 35 basis points lower in October. A December hike is about 72% priced.
  • Overnight. China’s official manufacturing index fell to 49.3 in October, below every forecast. Iran still has not answered the latest US proposal. Binance has not reopened all withdrawals after a theft of about $6.3 billion, and bitcoin is near $77,300. OPEC+ meets on Sunday.
  • Oil. Expiring December Brent settled at $117.64 on Friday, its fourth monthly gain. Iran says tankers hit mines south of Hormuz; US Central Command calls the claim false. Neither account has been independently confirmed.
  • The country. Three days before the midterms, more than 46 million ballots have been cast, a midterm record. Courts have rejected the Justice Department’s bid to set aside about 20,000 California ballots three times, and on Friday a federal judge barred it from threatening election workers with prosecution.

Today’s News

China’s factories shrink again in October, missing every forecast

The official manufacturing index fell to 49.3, its third reading below 50 in four months. Services slipped below 50 too.

China’s official manufacturing purchasing managers’ index fell to 49.3 in October from 50.1 in September, the National Bureau of Statistics reported on Saturday morning Beijing time. Reuters had polled 50.0 and Bloomberg’s median was 49.9. A reading below 50 means more purchasing managers reported contraction than expansion. New orders fell to 49.4 and production to 49.8. Export orders stayed in contraction, and the AP reported that some holiday-season orders had been pulled forward into September. The non-manufacturing index, which covers services and construction, fell to 49.7 from 50.2. High-technology manufacturing, at 51.3, was the one area still growing (AP, Business Standard, Finimize).

The figures follow a third-quarter GDP report two weeks ago that beat forecasts at 4.5%. They suggest the fourth quarter has started more weakly. Shanghai stocks fell about 2.5% over the past week to 3,599, near their 52-week low (Yahoo Finance).

Why it matters A slowing China cuts both ways for markets already worried about inflation. It lowers demand for oil and metals, which helps, and lowers demand for the industrial and chip companies that sell to China, which hurts. Copper ended the week flat. Monday’s Asian open is the first market reaction.

National Bureau of Statistics via AP, Business Standard and Finimize · Reuters and Bloomberg polls as reported · Yahoo Finance (Shanghai Composite).

Iran has not answered the latest US proposal; tanker claims in the Gulf remain unresolved going into the election

Oman says it rescued 25 crew from a burning tanker. Who attacked it, and whether any ship hit a mine, has not been established.

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard navy said on Friday that three tankers hit mines and caught fire on what it calls an “unauthorized” southern route past Hormuz; Iran’s regular army later raised the count to four. US Central Command called the claims false and said all main transit lanes “have been cleared of sea mines by US forces and remain so.” Its commander, Adm. Brad Cooper, said he flew over the strait and saw no Revolutionary Guard presence (Gulf News, Kurdistan24, VOA). Independent evidence points to at least one real attack. Oman said it rescued 25 crew from a merchant tanker set on fire off Musandam, and the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre reported a tanker hit by explosive devices attached from a small boat. Neither Oman nor UKMTO has said who carried out the attacks or whether a mine was involved. Disputed

On diplomacy, a Qatari delegation reached Tehran on Friday, and Iran has not formally answered a letter from Vice President Vance. On Wednesday a senior Iranian official told Reuters that Qatar’s earlier draft contained “unacceptable ideas.” Iran wants the US port blockade and oil sanctions lifted first; Washington wants nuclear issues addressed from the outset. President Trump has said he is weighing a deal against action to “finish them off,” and has been briefed on options to resume strikes. The White House has said there will be no attack before Tuesday’s election. A Fox News report this morning says no final decision has been made (CBS, CNBC, CNN, Fox News).

What’s next OPEC+ meets online on Sunday. Reuters, citing three sources, says the seven core members are likely to keep December targets unchanged; several Gulf members cannot ship their full quotas while the strait is restricted. From Monday the benchmark is January Brent, which settled at $113.43 on Friday, about $4 below the expired December contract. That gap is the contract roll, not a fall in prices.

Binance has not reopened all withdrawals after the largest crypto theft on record; bitcoin steadies near $77,300

The exchange says it will cover all user losses and has frozen about half of the stolen money.

Binance said attackers took about $6.3 billion from its wallets on Thursday afternoon, most of it within about 20 minutes across ten blockchains. Estimates from blockchain-analysis firms range from $5.9 billion to $6.43 billion. TRM Labs, Chainalysis and Elliptic attribute the theft to North Korea’s TraderTraitor group. No government has confirmed that attribution. Binance first traced the breach to one engineer’s compromised laptop; co-chief executive Richard Teng later said the attack also used a wallet delegation that “did not pass through our normal approval controls” (Forbes, Cointelegraph). Estimates of how much is frozen range from about $2.7 billion (TRM) and $3 billion (Binance’s verified figure) to the $5 billion the exchange cited on Friday afternoon. Tether and Circle have frozen stablecoins. Binance says it has set aside more than $6.34 billion of its own money to cover losses. As of Saturday morning we found no confirmation that withdrawals have fully resumed.

Bitcoin was near $77,300 on Saturday morning, little changed from Friday and down about 4% on the week (Yahoo Finance; CoinDesk had $77,487 at 8:55 AM). Ether was near $2,240.

A federal judge bars the Justice Department from threatening election workers with prosecution; early voting passes 46 million

That is the third court loss in a week for the department on the election, three days before polls close.

US District Judge Trina Thompson in San Francisco issued an emergency order on Friday barring the Justice Department from threatening criminal prosecution of election workers who decline its demands or process ballots lawfully. She also ordered the department to disclose any pending grand jury subpoenas or search-warrant applications. Civil-rights groups and unions, including one representing California election workers, brought the suit (Axios, ABC News, CNN). The same day a divided Ninth Circuit panel refused again to set aside about 20,000 California mail ballots that federal agencies flagged as likely cast by noncitizens. It was the department’s third straight loss on the question; reports say it is expected to go to the Supreme Court, and we found no filing as of this morning. Three Los Angeles County election employees have grand jury subpoenas for Nov. 4. No one has been charged.

The University of Florida Election Lab counts more than 46 million early ballots, already above 2022’s full early-vote total. President Trump holds in-person rallies in Iowa and Kansas on Sunday.

Poland’s constitutional court split turns into a physical standoff

A judge suspended the tribunal’s president, police were deployed at the court, and the president of Poland cut short a foreign trip.

On Friday Judge Anna Korwin-Piotrowska issued orders suspending Constitutional Tribunal President Bogdan Święczkowski and his deputy for a month and took over as acting head. Police were deployed at the building and protesters clashed outside. The tribunal said its website had been taken over illegally. President Karol Nawrocki, aligned with the opposition Law and Justice party, turned his plane back from a state visit to Senegal. Party leader Jarosław Kaczyński called it a “coup.” Prime Minister Donald Tusk’s government says it is restoring the rule of law to a court whose appointments it considers invalid (Anadolu, Kyiv Post, Euronews). The Week’s Ten Stories

1. The market stops paying for AI it can’t see being paid for, then sells the suppliers too

Alphabet had its worst day since 2004, ServiceNow its worst ever and Intel its worst since 1974. The technology and communication sector funds fell, and nine of the eleven sectors rose.

How it started. Two weeks ago Meta fell 7% after its results, despite holding its spending plan, because it is selling stock to pay for data centres. On Monday it announced a stock offering of about $35 billion, with Nvidia putting in $3 billion and AMD $1 billion. Critics called that circular: suppliers funding a customer that buys their chips. On Tuesday banks led by Bank of America, Citi and Morgan Stanley, with Blackstone, were reported to be arranging about $63 billion to buy Broadcom chips and lease them to Anthropic. Chips rallied on the financing news.

How it developed. Wednesday night gave the market its comparison. Microsoft reported Azure growth of 48% and capital spending covered by its operating cash flow. Alphabet reported Google Cloud revenue up 103% but quarterly capital spending of $51.4 billion, free cash flow of −$9.1 billion for a second quarter, and $41 billion raised from stock sales and bonds in the quarter (8-K). On Thursday Alphabet fell 12.6%, losing roughly $500 billion of market value, and Microsoft closed flat. On Thursday night Amazon showed trailing free cash flow of −$42.4 billion but AWS growth of 39%, its fastest in five years, and closed Friday only 1.4% lower. Intel guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15–16 billion against about $17 billion expected and warned the PC market would shrink again in 2027 as memory costs rise.

How it ended. Friday the selling reached the suppliers: Intel −28.6% to $65.76, AMD −10.9%, Marvell −10.1%, Nvidia −4.2%, the semiconductor fund (SMH) −7.0%. For the week Alphabet fell 12.6%, Meta 4.3%, Amazon 3.1%, Nvidia 3.0%, Apple 0.9% and Microsoft 0.5%. The technology fund fell 2.0% and the communication-services fund 5.3%. The other nine sector funds rose.

Why it mattered The question in the market has changed from how much AI demand there is to who pays for it, and how. Companies that pay out of operating cash, or show cloud revenue growing faster than spending, keep investors’ patience. Companies issuing stock and debt to keep up do not. Because those companies are the largest weights in the indexes, the cap-weighted S&P fell 0.9% in October while the equal-weight version rose 5.0%.

What was misunderstood. Friday was widely read as “the AI trade unwinding.” The weekly numbers are milder. SMH fell only 0.9% on the week, because chips had rallied 7.7% from Monday to Wednesday. Broadcom rose 1.4% and Micron 0.8% for the week. Most of the selling hit companies with their own bad news (Intel’s guidance, Alphabet’s cash flow) and growth stocks that guided lower. AI demand itself was not marked down.

Still unresolved. Alphabet’s 2027 capital plan, which it says will “increase significantly”; whether AMD (Tuesday), Qualcomm (Wednesday) and Arm (Thursday) confirm Intel’s PC warning; and Apple’s first report under John Ternus on Monday. Keep on watchlist: yes, top of it.

2. The Fed holds, but two officials want a hike, and Warsh won’t rule one out

This is the first time since 1992 that a governor and a regional president dissented in the same direction at one meeting.

The Federal Open Market Committee held its target range at 3.75–4.00% on Wednesday by 10–2. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both wanted a quarter-point increase (Fed statement). The statement upgraded growth to “strong” and called inflation “still elevated.” Chair Kevin Warsh said he does not “believe in forward guidance, absent exigent circumstances,” and that politics played no role. Futures odds of a December hike rose to about 85% after the meeting. They then fell to about 80% after Thursday’s in-line inflation report and to about 72% after Friday’s sell-off and soft wage report, as reported.

The week’s data pulled in different directions. Third-quarter GDP grew at a 3.4% annual rate, above forecasts. September PCE inflation was 3.6% headline and 3.1% core, still rising but as expected. The Employment Cost Index rose 0.8%, with wages up 3.1% for the year, the slowest since 2021. Private-sector wages adjusted for inflation fell 0.6% (BLS).

What was misunderstood. Many read the dissents as a December hike being locked in. The bond market did not: two-year yields fell 13 basis points over the week. A divided committee that the market expects to hike once more is a different message from one committed to a hiking path. Still unresolved: the October jobs report (Nov. 6) and CPI (Nov. 10) before the Dec. 8–9 meeting, and the White House hearing on whether Cook can be removed. Keep on watchlist: yes.

Federal Reserve FOMC statement, Oct 28 · BEA via CNBC and Morningstar · BLS Employment Cost Index · CME FedWatch as reported by Fox Business, ActionForex, GuruFocus · Fortune · CNN · Bloomberg.

3. Treasuries finish their best month since 2023

The 10-year fell from 5.29% to 4.94% in October, during a month in which the Fed’s next move was still expected to be a hike.

The week began with long yields as the market’s pressure point. On Wednesday the 10-year par yield rose to 5.05%, its highest close of the month, and the 30-year to 5.37%, while the two-year fell. Then Thursday’s $44 billion seven-year auction cleared at 4.894% with no tail, the inflation report matched forecasts, and the stock sell-off sent money into bonds. The 10-year ended Friday at 4.94% (−6 basis points on the week) and the two-year at 4.56% (−13). The two-to-ten-year spread widened to 38 basis points from 31 (Treasury).

What was misunderstood. The rally is not a bet on Fed cuts. Futures still lean to a December hike. Most of the October fall came in real yields, not inflation expectations: the 10-year real yield fell from 2.95% on Oct. 5 to 2.63%, while breakevens barely moved (FRED). That pattern fits slower expected growth more than a cooler inflation outlook. Still unresolved: the quarterly refunding. Borrowing estimates are expected Monday and the refunding statement on Wednesday, Nov. 4, which sets coupon sizes for November–January. Keep on watchlist: yes.

4. Anthropic files to go public as the financing of AI moves off balance sheets

The S-1 shows first-half revenue of $16.3 billion and an $80.7 billion net loss, most of it an accounting charge.

Anthropic, PBC filed its registration statement publicly on Monday, Oct. 26, after five months of confidential drafts. It plans to list Class A shares on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the symbol “ANT.” The price range and share count are blank (S-1, SEC EDGAR, CIK 0002130357). First-half revenue was $16.29 billion, against $1.16 billion a year earlier. Of the $80.7 billion net loss, $72.4 billion is the change in fair value of convertible instruments, which grows as the company’s valuation rises and is not cash spent. Seven co-founders keep 50.1% of the vote through a Class F share. CNBC reports the filing shows payments to SpaceX of about $3.1 billion a month for computing capacity once all contracted capacity is delivered. The roughly $63 billion Broadcom chip-leasing vehicle reported Tuesday has not been confirmed by either company. (This newspaper is written by an Anthropic model; it applies the same standard to the company as to any other.)

Why it mattered. Anthropic, Meta and Alphabet each showed this week that frontier AI spending now runs ahead of what operating cash can fund, and that the gap is being closed with lease vehicles, equity offerings and bonds. Top-rated data-centre securitizations now yield about 7%, up from under 5% earlier this year (CNBC, Morgan Stanley). Still unresolved: the price range, which would come in an amended filing; a mid-November listing is reported but not confirmed. The company remains private: EDGAR lists no ticker or exchange. Keep on watchlist: yes.

5. Oil holds above $100 as the Hormuz stand-off outlasts the diplomacy

Brent posted its fourth monthly gain. Energy was the best sector in October.

Oil fell early in the week after Saudi, Pakistani and Turkish officials met Iran’s leadership in Tehran, with WTI settling near $99.90 on Tuesday. It then climbed after reports that Washington told Gulf states to prepare for renewed strikes after the midterms, a fall in US crude stocks, and Friday’s tanker claims. Expiring December Brent settled at $117.64 and WTI at $104.12 on Friday, both up about 0.8% on the week. Brent rose about 14% in October and WTI about 15% (Reuters/AFP wire). Exxon and Chevron beat on refining and touched record highs. The energy sector fund rose 13.9% in October.

What was misunderstood. The “$118 oil” headline is the expiring December contract. January Brent, the benchmark from Monday, settled at $113.43. Still unresolved: Iran’s answer to the US and Qatar; whether the president orders strikes after Tuesday; and Sunday’s OPEC+ meeting. Keep on watchlist: yes.

6. Growth stocks that guided lower were sold hard

ServiceNow fell 34% on the week, Reddit 36%, Fortinet 24%, Twilio 22% and Roblox 19%.

ServiceNow fell 27% on Thursday, its worst day on record, after guiding its main contracted-revenue measure to 18.5% growth against about 19.8% expected. Fortinet fell 20% after cutting its services outlook and reporting a lower gross margin on component costs. On Friday Reddit lost 29% on daily users of about 130 million against about 134 million expected, Roblox 18% on a weaker fourth-quarter outlook, and Twilio 15% on slower expected growth. Each beat on the quarter just ended and guided lower (company results via Reuters, CNBC, Bloomberg).

What was misunderstood. Some coverage attributed the software selling to AI agents replacing paid seats. That is a market narrative; none of the companies said so. The common thread is simpler: growth valued as if it would accelerate, guided to slow down. Keep on watchlist: no. The point is made; Palantir and AppLovin report next week.

7. The largest crypto theft on record

About $6.3 billion left Binance in roughly 20 minutes on Thursday. By Friday it said much of it was frozen.

The theft began at about 4 PM Eastern on Thursday. Private analysts attribute it with high confidence to North Korea’s TraderTraitor group, the unit the FBI blamed for the $1.5 billion Bybit theft in February 2025. Bitcoin touched a six-week low near $76,400 on Friday, and about $1.6 billion of leveraged positions were liquidated (CoinDesk). Coinbase fell 10.6% Friday and 12.7% on the week; Robinhood fell 10.6% on the week. Bitcoin ended October down about 8%. The weekend developments are in Today’s News.

What was misunderstood. Early reports described an exchange in trouble. The money taken was Binance’s own hot-wallet funds, and the company says it can cover them from its balance sheet. The real risk was forced selling of crypto, and frozen stablecoins limited it. Still unresolved: full withdrawals, the proof-of-reserves report Binance promised for Monday, and any government attribution. Keep on watchlist: yes.

8. The election moves into the courts before a single result

The Justice Department lost three times on California ballots, faces a new order protecting election workers, and has a suit pending in Alaska.

Judge David Carter refused the department’s request to stop California counting about 20,000 mail ballots after a three-hour hearing on Tuesday. He called it a “monumental change” to an election already under way and told the government to “go out and indict” if it believes crimes occurred. Ninth Circuit panels then turned the department down twice. In Alaska a federal judge is weighing, on an expedited schedule, a department suit that could delay certification if Sen. Dan Sullivan loses narrowly. The governor ordered a state inquiry into the Peltola campaign, and PBS and NPR report federal grand jury subpoenas over a second candidate named Dan Sullivan; no one has been charged. A federal grand jury declined to indict former White House aide Cassidy Hutchinson (Axios).

Why it mattered. Federal action against state election offices has become a real factor in how quickly results are certified. Still unresolved: a possible Supreme Court filing, the Alaska ruling, and the Los Angeles grand jury on Nov. 4. Keep on watchlist: yes.

9. Central banks abroad lean hawkish: Canada hikes, the ECB debates a hike, and Lagarde says she will leave early

Each faces the same energy-driven inflation, with weaker growth than the US.

The Bank of Canada raised its rate a quarter point to 2.50% on Wednesday, its first hike since 2023, despite 68,000 jobs lost in September, and raised its 2027 inflation forecast to 3.1%. On Thursday the European Central Bank held its deposit rate at 2.50% after debating a hike, with euro-area inflation at 3.8%. Christine Lagarde announced she will step down on Feb. 7, 2027, nine months before her term ends. The euro touched a 2026 low near $1.111 and ended the week at $1.117. The Bank of Japan held at 1.25% on Friday by 8–1, with one member voting to hike.

Still unresolved: the race to succeed Lagarde, while France’s borrowing costs over Germany sit near 157 basis points. Keep on watchlist: yes, for the ECB succession.

The Globe and Mail · The Canadian Press · ECB statement as reported by Euronews and AP · CNN · FXStreet · Yahoo Finance.

10. Two elections abroad leave unfinished business: Israel deadlocked, Brazil’s market in shock

Netanyahu’s bloc fell eight seats short of a majority. Brazilian stocks lost 18% in a week after Lula won.

Israel’s final results give Prime Minister Netanyahu’s bloc 53 of 120 seats, the opposition bloc around Gadi Eisenkot 48, and Arab-led parties a record 19, on 77.9% turnout (Central Elections Committee via the Times of Israel). The opposition cannot reach 61 without Arab parties, which two of its leaders have ruled out. Results go to President Herzog on Nov. 4. In Brazil, President Lula won Sunday’s runoff 51.8% to 48.2%. The Ibovespa fell about 14% on Monday, its worst day since March 2020, and 18.1% on the week (Yahoo Finance); the real fell sharply. Flávio Bolsonaro has not conceded.

Why it mattered. A caretaker government in Israel would overlap with a US decision on Iran. Brazil’s sell-off shows how much a market had priced one outcome when the polls showed a tie. Keep on watchlist: Israel yes; Brazil no.

What Changed in the World

The market set a standard for AI spending: show the cash. A month ago every capital budget increase was rewarded. This week investors separated companies funding AI from operating cash from companies funding it with new stock, bonds and lease vehicles, and then cut exposure to the suppliers that depend on both. That standard will apply to every AI-linked report from here, starting with AMD on Tuesday.

The bond market looked past a hawkish Fed. Policymakers are split between holding and hiking, but long yields fell a third of a point in October, led by real yields. Bond investors appear to be pricing slower growth (weak real wages, falling stocks, a contracting China) more than further tightening. If the jobs report and CPI agree, December gets harder to justify.

Leadership rotated, and so far it has held. In October the equal-weight S&P rose 5.0% and the cap-weighted index fell 0.9%. Energy, staples, utilities and health care each rose about 10% or more, while technology and communication services fell 8–10%. It is the clearest rotation away from the largest companies in more than a year.

The election’s second front opened. The fight now includes federal prosecutors, grand juries and certification deadlines, not only ballots. Three days out, courts have checked the Justice Department each time it has asked them to act.

The indexes. The S&P 500 fell 0.57% to 7,580.07, the Nasdaq Composite 2.00% to 25,437.00 and the Dow 0.85% to 51,770.31. The Russell 2000 rose 1.29% to 2,811.84 and the equal-weight S&P fund 0.82%. The Economy & Central Banks

The data. GDP grew at 3.4% in the third quarter (advance estimate; the second estimate is Nov. 25), with final sales to private domestic buyers up 4.9%. September PCE prices rose 0.5% for the month and 3.6% for the year, with core up 0.3% and 3.1%. Real disposable income fell 0.3% and the saving rate was 3.8%. The ECI rose 0.8% in the third quarter. Wages are up 3.1% from a year earlier, so after inflation they are falling. Conference Board confidence jumped to 91.7. September durable-goods orders rose 1.8%. Jobless claims were 194,000 for the week to Oct. 24 (FRED). The Chicago PMI eased to 55.3. Overall, the economy is growing faster than expected, inflation is still rising, and households are losing purchasing power.

The Fed. The Fed held at 3.75–4.00% with two dissents for a hike; the details are in story 2. Warsh also said one of the eight yearly meetings will become a strategy session with no rate decision; reports differ on whether that starts in 2027 or 2028. The blackout is over. Models. Science

A cancer vaccine in a large trial. At the ESMO congress, Merck and Moderna reported that their personalised mRNA vaccine, intismeran, given with Keytruda after surgery for stage IIB–IV melanoma, cut the risk of recurrence or death by 42% against Keytruda alone. The phase 3 trial had 1,137 patients and a median follow-up of 24.6 months. These are company results presented at a congress; the New England Journal of Medicine paper has not yet appeared. The FDA on Oct. 26 expanded talazoparib with enzalutamide to men with HRR-mutated metastatic prostate cancer.

Astronomy. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory produced its first dwarf-planet candidate, 2026 ED138, beyond Pluto. Its estimated size of 600–1,500 km depends on how reflective it is, which has not been measured. JWST found a raised deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio in the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS, a sign it formed somewhere cold around another star (Nature Astronomy).

Earth. Laboratory experiments reported in Nature Geoscience suggest the early Earth could have made much of its own water from hydrogen reacting with molten rock. That supports one side of a long debate without settling it. The UN’s Emissions Gap Report puts current policies on course for about 2.8°C of warming.

Health. A small human challenge study in Science Translational Medicine found dengue virus can re-emerge in vaccinated volunteers, which bears on how long protection lasts. Two large observational studies linked heavy artificial-sweetener use to faster memory decline and more daylight to lower dementia risk. Both show associations, not causes.

That returns India’s NavIC system to four working satellites after NVS-02 failed to reach its orbit in January 2025. We have seen one report of the result. Single source

Overhyped & Undercovered

Overhyped: “the AI trade is over.” One violent Friday does not end the trade. On the week SMH fell 0.9%, Broadcom rose 1.4% and Micron 0.8%. Microsoft ended almost flat. Investors are pricing AI companies on cash generation, which is a narrower judgement than giving up on AI.

Overhyped: the mine claims. Iran’s count of tankers hit has ranged from two to six depending on the outlet and the hour, and no independent monitor has confirmed a mine strike. The verified facts are narrower: one tanker attacked with explosive devices and one crew rescued by Oman.

Undercovered: real wages. Private-sector pay adjusted for inflation fell 0.6% over the year. That squeezes households at the same time as consumer confidence jumped, and it weakens the case for a wage-driven inflation spiral that a December hike assumes.

Undercovered: credit reached its threshold. The high-yield spread hit 3.40 on Oct. 22, the line we had marked as the stress signal, then narrowed again. Almost nobody noticed, which may be the right reaction, but it was there.
