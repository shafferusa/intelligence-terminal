1 · Refresher: revenue recognition in five steps

From the income statement module you finished on Nov 22.

Revenue follows delivery, not cash. A sale belongs to the period in which the seller hands over what the customer paid for. Selling on credit creates a receivable; cash taken in advance creates a liability that turns into revenue as delivery happens (pp.40–41).

The five steps. IFRS and US GAAP share one model: identify the contract; identify each distinct promise in it (a performance obligation); set the transaction price; allocate that price across the obligations; recognize revenue as each is satisfied, at a point or over time. Steps 3 and 4 decide how much; step 5 decides when. Uncertain extras, such as bonuses, are left out until a reversal is highly unlikely (pp.41–44).

Where the money sits meanwhile. Revenue earned before the right to bill is unconditional is a contract asset; once only time stands between the seller and the cash, it becomes a receivable; cash before delivery is a contract liability. A firm acting as agent reports only its commission; a principal reports the full sale (pp.43–44).

Memorize
  • Contract → obligations → price → allocate → recognize
  • Cost-to-cost: revenue to date = (costs incurred ÷ total expected costs) × transaction price
  • Agent = net (commission); principal = gross

2 · Today: how monetary policy works, and where it stops

How does a change in one short interest rate end up changing inflation, what makes markets believe a central bank, and when does the lever fail? Lesson 10 covered what central banks are for; today is how they act.

Three tools, one target: bank reserves. In open market operations the central bank buys government bonds from banks and pays with new reserves, so banks can lend more and broad money can grow; selling bonds drains reserves (p.112). The policy rate is what the central bank charges banks for short secured loans (repos). Banks will not lend to customers below their own funding cost, so their base rates follow it (p.112). A reserve requirement is the rule, not the money: it sets the minimum share of deposits banks must hold. Raising it caps how much lending each unit of reserves supports, so it tightens; developed markets rarely use it (p.113). Each tool fails if banks sit on their reserves.

The transmission chain. Raise the policy rate and four things follow: market and bank lending rates rise; asset prices fall; expectations and confidence weaken; the currency tends to strengthen. Households and firms borrow and spend less, imports get cheaper, demand cools, and inflation falls. It works only because money is not neutral in the short run: prices adjust slowly, so a rate change moves real activity first (pp.113–115).

What makes a central bank effective. Independence: politicians like low rates before elections, so the decision sits with a body they cannot lean on. With operational independence the government sets the target and the bank chooses how to hit it; with target independence the bank also defines the target (p.117). Credibility: a government with large debts gains from inflation, so its promise is doubted; a believed target fulfils itself, because wages and contracts are written to it. Transparency: regular inflation reports build that belief (pp.117–120). Inflation targets sit near 2%, not zero, to leave room against deflation, and look about two years ahead (p.119). Developing economies face extra obstacles: thin bond and interbank markets, an unclear neutral rate, a weak inflation record and limited independence (pp.121–122).

A peg borrows credibility at a price. Fixing the currency to an anchor imports the anchor’s inflation. If home inflation runs higher, the currency weakens, so the central bank sells foreign reserves and buys its own currency. That shrinks money and lifts rates even in a weak economy: the peg sets domestic rates. Reserves are finite and published, so speculators can watch the defence run down (pp.123–124).

Stance is relative. A rate is contractionary only if it sits above the neutral rate, the rate that neither pushes nor restrains growth; below it, policy is expansionary (p.125).

Neutral policy rate = real trend growth rate + long-run expected inflation

Real trend growth
the economy’s sustainable real growth rate, % per year, an estimate (p.126)
Expected inflation
long-run expected inflation, % per year; the target itself if it is credible (p.126)
Result
% per year; for example (hypothetical), 1.8% trend growth and a 2% target put neutral near 3.8%

Match the medicine to the shock. Tightening suits prices pushed up by strong spending (a demand shock). Against a cost shock, such as dearer oil, it squeezes an economy already hurting and can deepen the downturn (p.126).

The limits. The bank sets a short rate; long rates follow what investors expect short rates to be. A hike can even lower long yields if investors read it as a coming recession, and bond vigilantes push long yields up when they doubt the bank’s control (pp.126–127). Nominal rates cannot go much below zero. In deflation real debt burdens grow and buyers delay purchases; in a liquidity trap extra money no longer lowers rates. Quantitative easing, very large asset purchases that create reserves, is the response, but it cannot make banks lend (pp.127–131).

Monetary and fiscal policy together. They work through different channels and change who does the spending. Easy fiscal policy with tight money raises rates and enlarges the government’s share of output (p.135); tight fiscal with easy money tilts the economy toward private spending. When a central bank buys government debt on a large scale (monetization), its independence blurs, and persistent deficits can lift real rates and crowd out private investment (pp.132–136).

Assumptions and exceptions
  • A rate that looks high may still be expansionary if neutral is higher.
  • Under a peg, domestic rates cannot be set freely.
  • QE is a balance-sheet tool, not a rate cut, and guarantees no lending.

Module 5 opens: why countries cooperate. Geopolitics is how geography shapes politics and relations between nations. State actors (governments and leaders) control security and resources; non-state actors, such as multinationals and NGOs, take part without that control (p.144). Political cooperation is agreement on rules and standards, and it is a separate axis from globalization, the flow of goods, money and people, which the next pages cover (p.145). Countries cooperate to reach resources they lack, to share standards that cut the cost of trade, and to win influence by attraction (soft power) (pp.147–148). Strong institutions, such as the rule of law and property rights, make a country’s word credible and defection costly (p.148). Each country ranks its interests with survival first; when two goals clash, the higher one wins, and short political cycles favour short-term goals. Risk rises when partners’ rankings diverge (p.149).

Memorize
  • Neutral rate = real trend growth + expected inflation; above it = contractionary
  • Transmission: rates, asset prices, expectations, exchange rate
  • Effective central bank: independence, credibility, transparency
  • Tighten against demand shocks, not supply shocks
  • Cooperation (rules among states) is not globalization (flows)
