Week Ahead

The Election, Apple and the Jobs Report Hit a Market Down Three Weeks in a Row

Sunday, November 1, 2026 · 37 min read

The S&P 500 starts the week 3% below its record, with the midterms on Tuesday, the Treasury’s refunding on Wednesday, the October jobs report on Friday and chip results all week; OPEC+ held output steady on Sunday, and Iran says Hormuz could reopen in two days if Washington meets its terms.

If you read nothing else

Today’s News

OPEC+ keeps December output targets unchanged and leaves the hard choice for Nov. 29

Gulf producers cannot ship their full quotas while Hormuz is restricted, so raising targets would have changed little. The meeting that matters is the one on 2027 quotas at the end of the month.

The seven core members of OPEC+ that have been adjusting output this year (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Algeria, Kazakhstan and Oman) agreed at an online meeting on Sunday to keep December production targets where they are, according to the group’s statement as reported by Reuters and other outlets. Reuters had reported on Saturday, citing three sources, that the members had agreed in principle to hold. Friday’s closing edition put the chance of no December increase at 55–70%.

The decision is mostly a formality. The group finished unwinding a 1.65 million barrel-a-day cut in September, and still has a further layer of about 2 million barrels a day of cuts in place for most members through the end of 2026. Several Gulf producers are already pumping and exporting less than their quotas allow because tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz is restricted by the US blockade of Iran and Iran’s own actions. A capacity review is due to finish by mid-November, and ministers meet on Nov. 29 to set the 2027 production framework (CNBC, Reuters via Business Standard and ANI).

Oil goes into the week near its highs. January Brent settled at $113.43 on Friday and January WTI near $103. The expired December Brent contract settled at $117.64; the roughly $4 gap is the contract roll, not a fall in prices. Brent rose about 14% in October.

What’s next The decision should not move prices much on its own: it matched what was reported in advance. What moves oil this week is Iran: whether the two-day Hormuz plan gets a US answer, and whether the president orders strikes after Tuesday. The Energy Information Administration’s weekly inventory report on Wednesday is the week’s main supply data point.

Iran says Hormuz could reopen in two days on its terms; Washington has not answered, and the supreme leader warns against new strikes

Tehran’s conditions include lifting sanctions, releasing frozen assets and recognising a shipping route it worked out with Oman. The US says no decision has been made on resuming attacks.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said on Saturday that Iran and Qatari mediators have a plan under which shipping through the Strait of Hormuz could return to normal within two days, shortened from a seven-day plan Iran put forward earlier. He tied it to US concessions. As reported, Iran wants sanctions imposed after the Islamabad memorandum collapsed in July lifted, oil waivers restored, frozen Iranian assets released (including funds held in Qatar), a smaller US military presence in the region and formal recognition of a route through the strait developed with Oman. Araghchi called US outreach, including Vice President Vance’s letter, a “positive step” but said Iran has no plans to meet US officials directly. A senior Iranian official told the Times of Israel’s live coverage on Saturday that there would be no nuclear talks or reopening until the US accepts Iran’s demands. We found no US response to the two-day plan as of Sunday morning.

Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei warned that any new US attack would bring a “geographically unrestricted response” (Euronews). Secretary of State Marco Rubio said President Trump “hasn’t made a decision” on resuming strikes. The president said on Saturday that the US is “not in that much of a hurry” to reach a deal and, according to CBS News, that it still has to “take care of” one or two more targets, including a suspected nuclear site. Fox News reports that further strikes are not likely before the midterms, in line with the White House’s statement that there will be no attack before Tuesday. Earlier reporting from CNN, the Financial Times and Axios said Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu has pressed for a joint operation and that plans are ready for the president’s approval after the election; one report says the US has warned Gulf allies that strikes could come as early as Nov. 4. Single source

At sea, Iran’s Revolutionary Guard repeated claims that tankers are on fire in the strait (Euronews); US Central Command, which said on Friday that the main lanes are clear of mines, says its forces have redirected 144 commercial vessels under the blockade. Neither side’s account of the tanker incidents has been independently confirmed beyond Oman’s rescue of 25 crew from one burning tanker on Friday.

Why it matters for the week The two-day plan gives both sides a way to reopen the strait without a full agreement, which is the one outcome that would quickly lower oil prices. But its conditions are close to what Washington has already refused, and the White House’s pause on strikes ends with the vote. That makes Wednesday through Friday the window to watch for oil, defence stocks and Treasuries.

Binance restarts withdrawals in stages after the $6.3 billion theft; its full proof of reserves slips to Tuesday

The exchange says it has replaced most of the stolen user assets from its own reserves. The FBI has attributed the attack to North Korea, according to reports.

Binance has reopened withdrawals on a first group of blockchain networks, starting with bitcoin and followed by Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, XRP and Base. Reports differ on how many networks are back (12 or 15) and on the exact start time, and the exchange has not given a date for the second phase. Binance’s own estimate of how much of the stolen user assets it has covered has risen from 80% to 90% and, in its latest statements, about 95%, paid from its user-protection fund, surplus in its futures insurance fund and its own treasury (CNBC, Bloomberg via Insurance Journal, CoinCentral). These are the company’s figures and have not been independently checked.

Binance published an updated balance snapshot dated Oct. 31. The full cryptographic proof of reserves it promised for Monday takes 40 to 50 hours to compute and is now expected on Tuesday, Nov. 3 (crypto.news). Reports say the FBI formally attributed the theft to North Korea on Saturday; private analysts had already blamed the group known as TraderTraitor.

Bitcoin was at $77,950 on Sunday morning, up about 0.7% in 24 hours, and ether at $2,267 (CoinGecko). The total value of all crypto is about $2.61 trillion, with bitcoin 59.9% of it.

Campaigns make their last push as early voting passes previous midterm records

Most forecasts favour Democrats in the House. The Senate is closer, and several of the deciding states count slowly.

CBS News counts 49.6 million early and mail ballots cast as of Friday evening; it cites the University of Florida Election Lab at more than 52 million. Either figure is above the full early vote of the 2022 midterms. These are running tallies, not official turnout. President Trump holds rallies in Iowa and Kansas on Sunday, both states with Senate races in play, and candidates in both parties are on the Sunday shows urging turnout (CBS, Al Jazeera).

All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot. Democrats lead the generic congressional ballot in the polling averages we found, and prediction markets price a Democratic House as very likely; markets are prices, not forecasts. Democrats need a net gain of four seats for the Senate. Analysts list Maine, Ohio, Texas, Michigan, North Carolina, Iowa, Kansas and Alaska as the races that decide it (CBS, Al Jazeera). Alaska counts slowly and uses ranked-choice voting, and the Justice Department has a pending suit there that could affect certification if Sen. Dan Sullivan loses narrowly.

The courts are part of the count. The Justice Department has lost three times in its bid to set aside about 20,000 California mail ballots it says may have been cast by noncitizens; we found no Supreme Court filing as of Sunday. A federal judge in San Francisco on Friday barred the department from threatening election workers with prosecution. Three Los Angeles County election employees have grand jury subpoenas for Wednesday, Nov. 4. No one has been charged.

Top Themes

1. Who pays for AI, and whether the chip slump spreads. Last week the market drew a line: AI spending paid for out of operating cash (Microsoft) was forgiven; spending paid for with new stock and bonds (Alphabet, Meta) was not. On Friday the selling reached the suppliers when Intel fell 28.6% on a weak outlook and a warning that PCs will shrink again in 2027 as memory costs rise. This week’s results test both halves of that. Apple on Monday shows what memory prices are doing to the biggest hardware margin in the world. AMD on Tuesday, Qualcomm and Arm later in the week show whether Intel’s warning was about Intel or about the industry. Palantir, Super Micro, Arista, AppLovin, Cloudflare and Datadog show whether AI demand is still growing faster than costs.

2. The election and the count. Markets usually take midterms calmly, and a split Congress has historically been a common outcome. The risk this year is not who wins but how long it takes to know, with federal lawsuits in California and Alaska and grand jury activity in Los Angeles. A Democratic House would end one-party control of Washington and change the outlook for tariffs, spending fights and AI regulation, which Reuters has flagged as one of the policy areas at stake.

3. Jobs, wages and the December hike. Futures price about a 72% chance that the Fed raises rates on Dec. 9, after two officials voted for a hike last week. But wages are slowing (the Employment Cost Index showed 3.1% annual wage growth, the slowest since 2021), September added only 29,000 jobs, and the bond market rallied 35 basis points in October. Friday’s report decides which of those stories the Fed reads.

4. Supply of Treasuries. Monday’s borrowing estimate and Wednesday’s refunding statement set how much debt the Treasury sells through January. Dealers expect no change in coupon auction sizes and the first increases in early 2027. A surprise in either direction would hit long-dated yields, which have been the bond market’s pressure point all autumn.

5. Iran after the vote. The White House has ruled out strikes only before Tuesday. Iran has offered a two-day route to reopening Hormuz on terms Washington has so far rejected. Oil, defence stocks and the dollar are the most exposed.

The Economy Ahead

The jobs report (Friday, 8:30 AM). September’s report showed 29,000 jobs added and a 4.2% unemployment rate. For October, Barclays and Newsquawk’s survey put payrolls around 50,000; CNBC reports a consensus of 55,000, and KPMG expects 45,000. Unemployment is expected at 4.2%. Weekly jobless claims have stayed low, at 194,000 in the latest week, which argues against a sudden collapse. A gain above about 100,000, or a fall in unemployment, would likely push December hike odds back towards the 85% seen after the Fed meeting and test the bond rally. A negative number or unemployment at 4.4% or higher would shift the market’s worry from inflation to recession, and the Fed’s hawks would have a harder case. Watch average hourly earnings too: the third-quarter Employment Cost Index showed wages growing 3.1% a year, below inflation of 3.6%.

Before Friday. ISM manufacturing on Monday (forecast 54.7, against 54.5 in September, per Trading Economics) and its prices index (forecast 77.5) show whether energy costs are still feeding through factory prices. Tuesday’s JOLTS report shows September job openings. ISM services on Wednesday is forecast near 54.8. Thursday brings third-quarter productivity and unit labour costs, which tell the Fed whether wage gains are being matched by output.

The Fed. No meeting this week. The committee held at 3.75–4.00% on Oct. 28 with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack voting to hike. Chair Kevin Warsh has said he does not believe in forward guidance “absent exigent circumstances,” so each data release counts for more than speeches. We did not find a confirmed schedule of Fed speakers for the week. The next decision is Dec. 9, after October CPI on Nov. 10.

The Treasury. Monday afternoon the Treasury publishes its borrowing estimates for October–December and January–March. Wednesday at 8:30 AM it announces the November refunding: the sizes of the three-, 10- and 30-year auctions next week and its guidance for coming quarters. Its last statement said it expects to keep coupon auction sizes unchanged “for at least the next several quarters.” Dealers expect that wording to stay and the first increase in early 2027 (Treasury dealer survey and MNI, as reported). Any sign of larger auctions sooner, or more bills in place of bonds, would matter for the 10- and 30-year yields, which at 4.94% and 5.31% are well off their October highs but still near the top of their two-year range.

Central banks abroad. The Reserve Bank of Australia decides on Tuesday afternoon Sydney time (late Monday in New York). It raised its rate to 4.60% in September, its fourth increase this year; CommBank, ANZ and Westpac expect a quarter-point rise to 4.85%, though market-pricing reports we found conflict sharply. The Bank of England decides on Thursday with a new forecast. It held at 3.75% in September on a 6–3 vote, with three members wanting a hike; Goldman Sachs now expects a hike, while a prediction market prices a hold as most likely. Both meetings test whether the energy-driven inflation that pushed Canada to hike last week is pushing others the same way. The European Central Bank’s succession race after Christine Lagarde’s early exit in February is the background story in Europe.

Earnings & Business

About 755 companies worth at least $2 billion report this week (StockTitan). These are the ones that matter most for the AI question and the indexes.

Apple (Monday after the close). John Ternus’s first results as chief executive. Apple guided to fiscal fourth-quarter revenue growth of 9–11%, about $111.7–113.7 billion; analysts’ estimates sit around $113–115 billion and earnings per share near $1.98–2.02 (Alpha Vantage, Finnhub). Only nine days of iPhone 18 Pro sales fall in the quarter, so the guidance for the holiday quarter, the gross margin as memory prices rise, and any comment on the foldable iPhone that went on sale Oct. 23 matter more than the quarter itself. Apple fell 1.2% on Friday to $344.63 and is about 2% below its 52-week high, the strongest of the large technology companies in October.

Palantir (Monday after the close). The software company most closely tied to government and AI spending fell 6.3% on Friday to $184.11. Forecasts for quarterly earnings differ between data vendors ($0.33 to $0.41 a share), a sign of how much estimates have moved; the outlook for US commercial revenue is what the stock trades on.

Its data-centre graphics chips and its PC processors answer two different questions: whether AI demand is intact, and whether Intel’s PC warning is industry-wide. Super Micro and Arista, also Tuesday, show server and networking demand.

A flat short end means options traders are paying almost as much for protection over the next nine days, which include the election and the jobs report, as over the next three months. Financials and regional banks (KRE) are the most direct election trade, through regulation and the yield curve (2s10s 38 basis points). Health care (XLV, near its high) has a heavy earnings week. Company catalysts beyond earnings. SpaceX (SPCX, $134.72) fell 8.7% last week and has a national-security Falcon 9 launch on Monday night. History, not a forecast. Stocks have tended to rise from the midterm election into year-end; CFRA has found the S&P 500 higher in the 12 months after every midterm since the Second World War. That pattern covered very different markets from this one and says nothing about this week.

Scenarios

Each scenario is measured by where the S&P 500 closes on Friday, Nov. 6, against Friday’s 7,580.07. The ranges add to roughly 100%.

Base case — a choppy week that ends within 2% of where it started (7,428–7,732): 45–55%. Conditions: an orderly election night with the House called by Wednesday; chip results mixed; jobs between 0 and 100,000. Expected behaviour: rotation continues, with equal-weight and small caps holding up better than the largest stocks; 10-year yield stays between 4.8% and 5.05%. Indicators: VIX between 16 and 23; Brent between $105 and $120. Confirmers: AMD guiding in line; claims near 200,000. Invalidators: a clear chip miss or a jobs shock.

Bull case — a relief rally of more than 2% (above 7,732): 18–28%. Conditions: a quick, uncontested result; Apple and AMD reassure on margins and demand; jobs near forecast with slower wages, so yields fall without recession fears; progress on Hormuz. Expected behaviour: the largest technology stocks lead again, the VIX drops below 16, oil falls. Confirmers: a US reply to Iran’s two-day plan; semiconductors up for the week. Invalidators: rising yields after the refunding; strikes on Iran.

Bear case — a fall of between 2% and 5% (7,201–7,428): 18–28%. Conditions: the chip warning spreads; a negative payroll number; a disputed Senate count. Expected behaviour: Nasdaq underperforms, Treasuries rally, the dollar mixed, credit spreads widen past 3.4 percentage points. Confirmers: VIX above 25; high-yield spreads widening. Invalidators: strong AMD and Qualcomm guidance.

Shock — a fall of more than 5% (below 7,201) in the week: 3–8%. Conditions: US strikes on Iran that hit Gulf energy infrastructure or a confirmed attack on tankers in the main channel, oil above $125, combined with a weak jobs report or a contested election. Expected behaviour: VIX above 30, oil and gold up, stocks and credit down together. Indicators: Brent above $120, the 30-year yield moving sharply either way.

What would change the outlook. A US answer to Iran that leads to ships moving through Hormuz would be the biggest positive change: it would lower oil, inflation expectations and December hike odds together. The biggest negative change would be the AI-spending worry becoming a demand worry, which would show up as chip companies cutting orders, not just guidance.

The United States

Election night. Polls close in parts of Indiana and Kentucky at 6 PM Eastern and in most of the country between 7 and 9 PM; Alaska’s last polls close at 1 AM Wednesday. Early results from Ohio, North Carolina and Maine will indicate the Senate picture first. Michigan, Texas, Iowa and Kansas follow, and Alaska and close Western races may take days. Mail ballots can change late counts in both directions, so early leads are not results. In the House, a Democratic majority would mean divided government for the final two years of President Trump’s term; a Republican hold on both chambers would extend single-party control.

The legal front. Three things to watch: whether the Justice Department takes its California ballot challenge to the Supreme Court after three losses in lower courts; a federal judge’s ruling in Alaska on the department’s suit that could delay certification there; and the Los Angeles grand jury on Wednesday, where three county election workers have subpoenas. No one has been charged. Supporters of the department’s actions argue it is enforcing citizenship requirements for voting; election officials and civil-rights groups argue the efforts are designed to intimidate workers and delay certification, citing the courts’ rulings so far.

The Supreme Court opens its November sitting on Monday, with about seven hours of argument over five days through Nov. 10, including a Catholic preschool’s challenge to its exclusion from a Colorado programme (SCOTUSblog).

The World Ahead

Iran and the Gulf. The week’s central question is above: whether Washington answers Iran’s two-day Hormuz plan or resumes strikes once the election is over. The US blockade of Iranian ports continues. Saudi Arabia reported intercepting a drone on Saturday (Gulf News), a reminder that Gulf infrastructure remains within range.

Israel. Official results go to President Isaac Herzog on Wednesday, Nov. 4, and he then has a week to ask a candidate to form a government. Prime Minister Netanyahu’s bloc won 53 of 120 seats and Gadi Eisenkot’s opposition bloc 48; neither can reach 61 without parties the other refuses to sit with. Ynet reports that Herzog has raised a Netanyahu–Eisenkot unity government; the president’s office denies any such talks, and Eisenkot says Netanyahu cannot serve as a minister. A caretaker government would coincide with any US decision on Iran.

Poland. The fight over the Constitutional Tribunal, where a judge suspended the court’s president on Friday and police were deployed at the building, continues. President Karol Nawrocki cut short a visit to Senegal, opposition leader Jarosław Kaczyński calls it a coup, and Prime Minister Donald Tusk responded defiantly (Euronews); his government says it is restoring the rule of law to a court whose appointments it considers invalid. Watch for whether the tribunal’s rival leaderships issue competing rulings.

Russia and Ukraine. Euronews reports evacuees from occupied Oleshky reached Ukrainian-held territory during a local truce, and Russia reported a large drone attack on an industrial plant. We found no wider change in the war this weekend.

Elsewhere. Canada’s Prime Minister Mark Carney said Canada aims to become an “associate member” of the European Union by June 2027 (Euronews). AI & Technology Ahead

Earnings are the AI news this week. Apple’s margins and holiday guidance show how memory prices, which AI data centres have pushed up, are hitting consumer hardware. AMD, Super Micro and Arista measure AI infrastructure demand; Qualcomm and Arm measure phones and PCs; Palantir, AppLovin, Cloudflare and Datadog measure whether AI-linked software is still growing quickly enough to justify its valuation. IonQ reports Thursday for quantum computing.

The financing question. Last week showed that the largest AI spenders now raise outside money to keep building: Meta’s roughly $35 billion stock offering, Alphabet’s $41 billion of stock and bonds in a quarter, and a reported $63 billion vehicle to lease Broadcom chips to Anthropic. Top-rated data-centre securitizations now yield about 7% (CNBC, citing Morgan Stanley). Anthropic’s IPO price range, if it comes, will be the market’s clearest price on a frontier lab.

Policy. Reuters notes that the midterms could change the outlook for federal AI regulation. A Democratic House would bring oversight hearings and a different negotiating partner on any national AI framework.

Cyber. The Binance theft, attributed by private analysts and, according to reports, the FBI to North Korea, is the largest crypto theft on record. Binance’s explanation so far points to a compromised engineer’s laptop and a wallet delegation that bypassed normal approvals. Its full proof of reserves on Tuesday will show whether customer assets are fully backed.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

County voters decide on council staff and offices; the DA says City Hall charges are due this week

Allegheny County voters will decide on Tuesday whether to repeal the home-rule charter section that bars council members from county-funded personal staff, district offices and fringe benefits such as health insurance and pensions; council members’ stipends would not change. Council put the question on the ballot by two 9–6 votes in June, and the county’s Government Review Commission recommends a no vote (Axios Pittsburgh, county ballot text). District Attorney Stephen Zappala told WPXI last week that charges are expected this week in his investigation of professional-services contracts under former Mayor Ed Gainey. Gainey denies wrongdoing, and no one has been charged. Single source

Pennsylvania

Shapiro goes into Election Day far ahead of Garrity; mail ballots must arrive by 8 PM Tuesday

Gov. Josh Shapiro leads state Treasurer Stacy Garrity by 20 points or more in every recent poll we found; Quinnipiac’s Oct. 1–5 likely-voter poll had him at 59% to 34%, with Libertarian Ken Krawchuk at 2%, and a later poll reported by Fox 29 shows a 25-point lead. All 17 US House seats are on the ballot, several of them competitive, and there are no statewide ballot questions. Mail ballots must reach county election offices by 8 PM Tuesday; postmarks do not count. Under current law counties cannot begin processing mail ballots until Election Day morning, so Pennsylvania’s full count usually takes longer than its neighbours’.
