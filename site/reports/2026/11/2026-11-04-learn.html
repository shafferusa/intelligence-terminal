Jay Forrester Held GE’s Orders Constant on a Notepad. The Kentucky Plants Still Hired and Fired in Waves.

Wednesday, November 4, 2026 · 14 min read

Add a delay to a loop that is trying to correct itself and correction turns into overcorrection — the same three-step mechanism behind a factory’s hiring waves, a supply chain’s bullwhip, and a hog farmer’s price cycle.

Systems Thinking

Day 27 of 293 · Systems Thinking 3 of 12 · Delays, overshoot and oscillation

A factory that made its own boom and bust

In the mid-1950s, the engineer Jay Forrester left MIT’s Servomechanisms Laboratory — where he had spent the war years and afterward built radar and guidance systems — for the university’s new school of management. People from General Electric brought him a question about the company’s household-appliance plants in Louisville, Kentucky: why did the plants run three and four shifts for a while, then lay off half their workforce a few years later, in a repeating wave that GE’s own managers could not pin on anything happening outside the company? The business cycle was the obvious suspect, but it did not fit — the swings at the plants did not line up cleanly with swings in the economy.

Forrester did not have a computer model to run. He had the plants’ own, quite ordinary rules for hiring, production and ordering — rules written to keep inventory near a target and output matched to incoming orders — and he worked them through by hand, step by step, on paper. He held the plants’ incoming orders dead flat in the calculation, removing any outside swing in demand as a possible cause. Employment still rose and fell in waves, cycling over roughly three years from peak to peak, by his own later account of the exercise. He called this hand-simulation the beginning of what became the field of system dynamics.

If the orders coming in were steady the entire time, where did the waves of hiring and layoffs come from?

The Big Idea

Today’s concept: a balancing loop that is trying to correct itself overshoots and settles into a cycle of oscillation when a delay sits inside it, because the people running the loop act on information that is already out of date, and because they forget how much correction is already in the pipeline, still working its way toward the goal.

A balancing loop on its own, as Day 26 showed, pushes a system back toward some target: a thermostat reads the room, compares it to a setpoint, and adjusts. That works cleanly when the adjustment takes effect right away. The moment a delay separates the action from its effect — turning the furnace on does not warm the room instantly — the loop is steering on news that is already stale. It keeps correcting past the point where correction was still needed, because the earlier correction has not finished arriving yet.

The result is not random noise. It is a specific, repeatable shape: the corrected quantity swings past its target, the controller notices the overshoot and reverses, that reversal also overshoots in the other direction once its own delay plays out, and the cycle repeats — oscillation generated entirely from inside the loop, with no outside push required to keep it going.

How It Works

Every delay in a system is either an information delay or a material delay, and most real loops carry both. An information delay is the time between something changing and someone noticing — a manager who checks inventory once a month is working with up-to-a-month-old numbers. A material delay is the time between deciding to act and the action actually landing — the weeks between placing a factory order and the finished goods arriving on a shelf. Either kind of delay has the same effect on a balancing loop: it widens the gap between when a correction is needed and when its consequences are visible.

Picture a simple goal-seeking rule: compare the actual level of something — inventory, employment, a thermostat’s reading — to a target, and adjust in proportion to the gap. With no delay, that rule glides smoothly to the target and stays there, because the adjustment and its effect are the same event. Add a delay of even a few steps between the adjustment and seeing its result, and the rule starts reacting to a gap that its own earlier adjustments are already closing, unseen. It keeps adding more correction on top of correction that has not landed yet. By the time all of it lands at once, the system has overshot the target in the other direction, the rule reads a gap on the opposite side, and it corrects hard the other way. The bigger the delay is relative to how fast the rule tries to close the gap, the larger the overshoot and the longer the resulting cycle takes to die out — and past a certain point it does not die out at all; it keeps oscillating or grows wider with each swing.

The piece almost every real decision-maker drops is what system dynamicists call the supply line: the stock of corrective action that has already been set in motion but has not yet arrived. Day 25 named inventory as a stock that changes only through its inflows and outflows. The supply line is a second, usually invisible stock sitting right next to it — orders already placed but not yet delivered, job openings already posted but not yet filled, a wire transfer already sent but not yet cleared. A rule that compares inventory on hand to a target, and ignores how much is already in transit, will keep ordering more every time it checks and finds the shelf still short, even though the shortage is already being fixed. That is functionally identical to a balancing loop with an added delay: the correction already under way plays the role of a result the controller cannot see yet.

John Sterman tested exactly this failure directly in a controlled setting: the Beer Distribution Game, a role-play MIT’s Sloan School had used in teaching since the 1960s, in which players take the roles of retailer, wholesaler, distributor and factory in a simple supply chain, each ordering from the stage above to keep their own inventory near a target, with a shipping delay between placing an order and receiving it. In a 1989 paper in Management Science, Sterman ran the game with real subjects — including experienced managers — against a customer demand pattern that was almost flat: one modest, one-time step up, then steady. The orders players placed did not stay flat. They swung widely, and the swings grew larger the further upstream — from retailer toward factory — a stage merely echoing what it had already received, amplified. Players who were asked why blamed the customer, the market, anything outside the game; the actual cause, visible in Sterman’s fitted model of their decisions, was that they were anchoring their orders on the visible inventory gap and systematically underweighting the orders they had already placed and not yet received — dropping the supply-line term, in effect, out of their own decision rule.

That amplification pattern, each stage of a supply chain over-reacting more than the stage before it, earned its own name once it showed up outside the classroom: the bullwhip effect, documented in real distribution data by Hau Lee, V. Padmanabhan and Seungjin Whang in a widely cited 1997 article. A small, real wobble in end-customer demand becomes a large one by the time it reaches raw-material suppliers, not because anyone along the chain is behaving irrationally by their own lights, but because each stage is running the same delayed, supply-line-blind correction rule Sterman’s subjects ran, one link further from the customer who actually generates the signal.

The deepest point in all of this is what Sterman’s subjects got backwards: they treated the oscillation as something happening to them, caused by an unpredictable world, when the oscillation was something their own decision rule was doing to itself. System dynamicists call behaviour that a system generates out of its own structure endogenous, to distinguish it from behaviour actually forced on a system from outside. A delayed balancing loop does not need an external shock to oscillate; the delay and the rule are enough on their own. That does not mean outside shocks never happen or never matter — only that a system can produce the appearance of being buffeted by the world while the real engine of its swings is sitting inside its own wiring the whole time.

Back to the story

That is exactly what Forrester found at GE’s Kentucky plants. Held constant on paper, the incoming orders were not driving the waves. The plants’ own production and hiring rules were reacting to inventory and backlog figures that were already somewhat dated by the time anyone acted on them, and those rules did not track how much correction — goods already in production, workers already hired — was already in the pipeline before adding more. Every correction landed on top of corrections still arriving, overshot, and had to be corrected back the other way a few quarters later. The business cycle outside the plant gates was not required to produce the three-year wave inside them; the plants’ own delayed balancing loop was enough.

Information delay
The lag between something changing and someone noticing it — a monthly inventory count that is already weeks stale the day it is read.
Material delay
The lag between deciding to act and the action actually taking effect — the weeks between placing an order and the goods arriving.
Supply line
The stock of correction already set in motion but not yet arrived — orders placed but not delivered, hires made but not started — distinct from the stock the decision-maker is actually watching.
Overshoot
Correcting a gap past the point where the gap has actually closed, because some of the closing is still in transit and unseen.
Bullwhip effect
The tendency of order swings to grow larger at each stage further from the end customer in a multi-stage supply chain, each stage amplifying the delayed, supply-line-blind correction of the stage below it.
Endogenous behaviour
A pattern a system produces from its own internal structure and decision rules, rather than one imposed on it by an event outside the system.

Example: The 2020–21 Car Chip Shortage

This sits at the financing-and-production link of the car’s own chain: when the pandemic struck in spring 2020, automakers, expecting a collapse in demand, cancelled their semiconductor orders, and chipmakers sold the freed capacity to laptop, phone and game-console makers riding a work-from-home surge. Car demand did not collapse; it rebounded within months. But semiconductor fabrication capacity cannot be reallocated on a few months’ notice — building out a new production line typically takes many months to years — so the automakers found themselves back of a queue they had voluntarily left, waiting behind customers who had moved in while the queue was open.

Estimates of the resulting lost vehicle production in 2021 vary by forecaster and by when during the year the estimate was made, which is itself a sign of a system still working through its own delays: AlixPartners raised its forecast three times over the year, from 2.2 million vehicles in January to 3.9 million in May to 7.7 million by September; S&P Global Mobility’s eventual tally for 2021 alone came to roughly 9.5 million units, and AutoForecast Solutions put the year’s loss at about 10.5 million. A real supply shock compounded the bullwhip rather than standing in for it: a fire on March 19, 2021 at Renesas Electronics’s Naka plant in Japan, which made roughly two-thirds of its output as automotive chips, knocked out a wafer line for months just as the rebound was accelerating.

1. Name the chain. Automakers cancel chip orders → chipmakers reallocate capacity to consumer electronics → car demand rebounds within months → chip fab capacity takes far longer than that to rebuild or reallocate → automakers wait behind the customers who took their old place in line.

2. Find the delay. The material delay between deciding to expand or reallocate chip production and that capacity actually existing runs to many months or years — far longer than the few months it took car demand to recover.

3. Find the supply-line error. Automakers and chipmakers alike were ordering and allocating against the demand they could currently see, not against the capacity already committed but not yet delivered — the same blind spot Sterman’s Beer Game players showed.

4. Read the amplification. A rebound in one quarter’s car sales produced order swings upstream, at the chip fabs, that outran the swing in actual demand — the bullwhip pattern, not a one-to-one pass-through.

5. Separate the endogenous part from the shock. The bullwhip itself was endogenous, generated by the ordering rules; the Renesas fire was a genuine external shock that landed on top of it and made the endogenous swing worse.

Equation / Model

orders = expected demand + (desired inventory − actual inventory) / adjustment time − supply-line correction

Orders placed equal expected demand, plus the gap between desired and actual inventory divided by how quickly the decision-maker tries to close that gap, minus a correction for how much is already on order and in transit.

expected demand
the decision-maker’s forecast of how much will be needed, usually built from recent observed demand
desired inventory − actual inventory
the gap between the inventory target and what is actually on hand right now
adjustment time
how many periods the decision-maker allows to close that gap; a shorter adjustment time means a more aggressive, faster correction
supply-line correction
a subtraction for orders already placed but not yet received — the term Sterman’s 1989 study found subjects systematically underweighted or dropped, which is what converts an ordinary delayed balancing loop into overshoot and oscillation

Why It Matters

Once you can name the information delay and the material delay inside a balancing loop, and ask whether its decision rule is actually tracking its own supply line, you gain a specific diagnostic: you can look at a boom-and-bust pattern — a factory’s hiring, a supply chain’s orders, a market’s price — and ask whether the swings need an outside story at all, or whether the system’s own decision rules, run through its own delays, are sufficient to produce them. That question reframes who is responsible for a cycle: not necessarily the people who get blamed for over-ordering or over-hiring in the moment, but the rule they are following and the delay it is running against.

Settled. That a balancing loop with a sufficiently large delay relative to its correction speed will overshoot and oscillate, and that people systematically underweight supply-line information when managing stocks under delay, are both well-established results in control theory and in Sterman’s widely replicated experimental work.

Debated. How much of any one specific real-world cycle is this self-generated oscillation versus a run of genuine outside shocks landing close together is frequently argued case by case; the 2021 chip shortage shows both operating on the same system at once, and separating their shares exactly is a matter of judgment, not a solved calculation.

Value judgment Whether a firm should accept higher costs to smooth its workforce and its ordering across a cycle its own rules helped create, sparing its workers and suppliers the swings, or treat that volatility as simply the price of running close to efficient, is a question about who should absorb a system’s instability — not one the stock-and-flow arithmetic can settle by itself.

Day 26 named the balancing loop — a closed chain with an odd number of negative links, pushing a system back toward a goal — and today adds the one ingredient that turns a clean balancing loop into a cycle: a delay between the action and its visible effect. Day 25 gave the stock-and-flow rule that a stock changes only through its flows; today’s supply line is a second such stock, usually tracked worse than the first, sitting between a decision and its delivery.

Across. J. W. Crowell, A. C. Guyton and J. W. Moore lengthened the blood vessel path between the heart and the brain in anesthetized dogs and reported the result in the American Journal of Physiology in 1956: the dogs developed Cheyne-Stokes breathing, a cycle of deepening and fading breaths separated by pauses. Stretching the delay between a change in blood carbon dioxide and the brainstem actually registering it was enough, on its own, to turn the body’s ordinary breathing-control balancing loop into an oscillation — one recognized mechanism behind the same breathing pattern that appears in some human heart-failure patients, whose slowed circulation lengthens that same delay.

Remember These
  • A balancing loop with a delay overshoots its goal, because the correction already under way has not finished arriving by the time more correction is added.
  • The supply line — correction already set in motion but not yet delivered — is a stock people reliably forget to track, and forgetting it is what converts a delay into oscillation.
  • The bullwhip effect is this same failure repeated at every stage of a supply chain, each stage amplifying the one below it.
  • Behaviour a system generates from its own structure is endogenous; it needs no outside shock to keep swinging, though real shocks can still land on top of it.
Apply It

In the late 1920s the German economist Arthur Hanau documented a regular “pig cycle” in German hog prices, where farmers expand breeding when prices are high but a decision to breed takes close to a year to become pork on the market — using today’s delayed balancing loop, why does that arrangement produce repeated price booms and slumps even with steady demand for pork?
