Morning Edition

Asia and Europe rebound as allies read the U.S. tariff plan as milder than feared

Thursday, November 26, 2026 · 30 min read

With U.S. markets shut for Thanksgiving, Taiwan rose 2.5%, Europe about 1.7% and U.S. stock futures half a percent as investors decided Washington’s tariff plan mostly keeps allies at rates they already pay, while South Korea raised interest rates, Houthi forces claimed a strike on Saudi Arabia’s largest refinery, and Pittsburgh settles in for a wet, windy holiday.

The Brief

If you read nothing else

  • The tape. U.S. stock and bond markets are closed for Thanksgiving. S&P 500 futures were up 0.55% at 7,235 at 6:28 AM, Nasdaq-100 futures 0.50%; they stop trading at 1 PM. Stocks reopen Friday for a half-day session ending at 1 PM.
  • Abroad. Taiwan’s market rose 2.50%, Hong Kong’s 1.19% (ending a seven-day losing streak), Tokyo’s Nikkei 0.86% and Korea’s Kospi 0.29% after Wednesday’s rout. The Stoxx Europe 600 was up about 1.6% this morning, with every sector higher.
  • The tariff read. Analysts and governments in Asia and Europe read the U.S. proposal as keeping the EU, Japan and Korea near the 15% they already pay, with nothing likely to take effect before early 2027. China called it “unilateralism” and said it is “firmly opposed”; Japan called it “regrettable.”
  • Rates and central banks. The Bank of Korea raised its policy rate a quarter point to 3.25%, its third rise this year. A Bank of Japan board member urged faster increases, with the yen near 159 to the dollar. Wednesday’s U.S. 10-year par yield of 4.85% stands until Friday; a Fed rise on December 9 remains priced at roughly 95–98%.
  • Biggest risk. The Gulf. Houthi forces say they struck Saudi Arabia’s Ras Tanura refinery and Iran claims two more tankers hit mines near Oman; neither damage claim is confirmed. Brent was little changed near $94.50 a barrel on the expiring contract.
  • Beyond markets. Macy’s Thanksgiving parade, in its 100th year, was postponed to Saturday because of forecast 60 mph gusts in New York, the first postponement in its history. Pittsburgh expects all-day rain, gusts near 40 mph and a high of 48.

Top Stories

A day after a chip rout, Asia and Europe recover as the tariff plan proves narrower than feared

The markets that sold off hardest on Wednesday bought back on Thursday. Semiconductor makers led the bounce, and bank shares followed in Europe.

Taiwan’s Taiex closed up 2.50% at 39,151.81, recovering most of Wednesday’s 4.8% fall; one recap put the gain at 3.1%, but the exchange-based figure most outlets carry is 2.50%. Hong Kong’s Hang Seng rose 1.19% to 22,226.88, its first gain in eight sessions, and Tokyo’s Nikkei 225 closed up 0.86% at 57,499.16 after being up about 1.5% earlier; the broader Topix rose only 0.11%. SoftBank Group, which fell almost 15% on Wednesday, rose 7.3%, and Advantest and other chip-equipment makers rose 6–7% during the session (AP, CNBC, as summarized). Korea’s Kospi briefly topped 4,800 before ending up just 0.29% at 4,711.21, as individual investors sold about 1.04 trillion won of shares into the rise and institutions bought; SK hynix rose 2.2% and Samsung 0.4% (Korea Times, JoongAng). Shanghai was flat at 3,476.88, holding its lowest close in more than a year.

Europe followed. The Stoxx Europe 600 was up 1.63% at 636.23 at 6:23 AM ET, with Germany’s DAX up 1.92%, France’s CAC 40 up 1.82% and London’s FTSE 100 up 1.60% (Yahoo Finance, delayed). Reuters reported every sector higher, led by banks and technology: Deutsche Bank rose about 3%, and the chipmakers Infineon and Soitec about 4%. The European technology index is still down about 12% this month, and volumes are thin with New York closed.

Outlets tied the bounce to two things: a recovery in chip shares after three days of heavy selling, and a reading of Wednesday’s U.S. tariff proposal as milder for allies than the Reuters report earlier in the week had suggested. As reported, the new rates for the EU, Japan, Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland would be capped at the roughly 15% their trade deals already set, and analysts quoted by AP and the law firm Baker McKenzie do not expect anything to take effect before early 2027. That is how markets described it; we cannot show it was the cause.

Why it matters U.S. futures pointing 0.5% higher suggest Friday’s short session starts from a firmer footing after Wednesday’s recovery from a 3.2% intraday fall. But most of the global gain is a rebound in the same chip stocks that fell hardest, and Shanghai, which is most exposed to the China tariffs, did not join in.
What’s next Tokyo’s November inflation figures come out at 6:30 PM Eastern tonight. U.S. stocks trade from 9:30 AM to 1 PM on Friday, bonds until 2 PM, with no major U.S. data.

Houthi forces claim a strike on Saudi Arabia’s biggest refinery; Iran claims two more tankers hit mines

Saudi Arabia says it shot down the drones. Neither the refinery damage nor the mine strikes has been independently confirmed.

The Houthi military spokesman, Yahya Saree, said on Wednesday that his forces attacked Ras Tanura, Saudi Arabia’s largest refinery and a main oil-export terminal, and caused fires; some reports add a claimed strike on Riyadh. Satellite images reportedly showed thick smoke over the site, and a Reuters-style report cites an unnamed source confirming an attack took place. The Saudi-led coalition said its air defences destroyed four drones over Ras Tanura and did not say the refinery was hit; Aramco and the Saudi government did not respond to requests for comment (CBC, The National, Quartz, US News, as summarized). Damage unconfirmed

On Thursday Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said two more oil tankers crossing without Tehran’s permission were “hit by sea mines” near Khasab, on Oman’s side of the Strait of Hormuz, after similar claims on Monday and Wednesday. No ships have been named. The maritime security agency UKMTO has attributed recent incidents near Khasab to unidentified projectiles rather than mines, and U.S. Central Command disputes the mine claims and says the main shipping lanes are clear. UKMTO did confirm that three tankers were hit on November 22, without casualties (CNBC, Marine Insight, ABC, as summarized).

Oil barely moved. Brent’s January contract, which expires at the end of the month, was down about 0.1% at $94.52 a barrel early Thursday, as reported by Reuters, and U.S. crude was at $84.44, up 0.9% from Wednesday’s settlement (Yahoo Finance, delayed). The next Brent contract, for February, traded near $91.

Why it matters A confirmed hit on Ras Tanura would be the first damage to Saudi export capacity in this war and a direct supply shock; so far the market is treating it, like the mine claims, as unproven. Shipping trackers put Hormuz traffic at roughly half of normal, and OPEC+ meets on Sunday.

Houthi and IRGC statements via CBC, The National, CNBC, ABC (claims) · Saudi-led coalition statement · UKMTO and CENTCOM as reported · Reuters and Yahoo Finance (oil, Delayed).

South Korea raises rates for the third time this year as Japan signals it may follow

Asia’s central banks are tightening while the yen sits near the level that has prompted intervention before.

The Bank of Korea raised its seven-day repo rate by a quarter point to 3.25%, the highest since November 2024, after increases in July and August; 32 of 34 economists in a Reuters poll had expected it. Two of the seven board members, Hwang Kun-il and Lee Soo-hyung, voted to hold at 3%. The bank raised its growth forecast for 2026 to 3.9% from 3.3% and for 2027 to 4.1% from 2.9%, and Governor Shin Hyun-song signalled more increases without a timetable. Korean consumer prices rose 2.8% from a year earlier in October (Reuters, CNBC, Korea Times).

In Tokyo, Bank of Japan board member Naoki Tamura urged faster increases and said the 1.25% policy rate is “some way from neutral,” after Governor Kazuo Ueda warned this week that the weak yen risks pushing inflation too high. Swaps price about an 80% chance of a rise at the December 17–18 meeting (FXStreet, as summarized). The dollar bought 159.29 yen this morning, just below the 160 level Japanese officials have warned about, with no report of intervention. Tokyo’s November consumer prices, the best early read on Japanese inflation, are due at 6:30 PM Eastern; October’s headline rate was 2.8%.

Why it matters With the Fed, the ECB, the Bank of Korea and probably the Bank of Japan all raising rates into December, the global cost of money is still going up. A Japanese rise matters most for U.S. markets: higher yields at home can draw Japanese savings out of U.S. bonds.

Bank of Korea decision via Reuters, CNBC, Korea Times · Bank of Japan remarks via FXStreet and others · Yahoo Finance (yen, Delayed).

China says it is “firmly opposed” to the new U.S. tariff proposals; Japan calls them “regrettable”

The plan is only a proposal. Washington’s trade chief says nothing is likely to take effect for months.

China’s commerce ministry called Wednesday’s Section 301 proposals “unilateralism,” said it is firmly opposed, and said it remains in close contact with Washington on extending the trade truce, as reported by Newsquawk and others; we found no sign of immediate retaliation. Japan’s chief cabinet secretary, Minoru Kihara, called the measures “regrettable” and rejected the finding of excess capacity, while noting that the rates are consistent with the two countries’ existing agreement. The EU lodged a formal protest, according to Korea’s SBS; we found no European Commission statement text, and no formal statement from Seoul.

The U.S. Trade Representative’s release proposes duties on 14 economies over what it calls “structural excess capacity.” As reported, China would face 20% on top of existing tariffs, rising to 40% if the truce is not extended past January. The EU’s primary rate would be 15%, with alternatives of 20% for the EU and Japan and 25% for Korea. India’s proposed 25% could fall to 17–20% with a deal. A public hearing is reported for January 25, and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC the tariffs likely would not take effect for months (USTR release; CNBC; Reuters via Investing.com; Baker McKenzie). Reported ceilings differ between outlets, and we have not read the Federal Register notice.

USTR release (as summarized) · China’s commerce ministry via Newsquawk · Japanese government via SBS · CNBC, Reuters, Baker McKenzie. Stage: proposed, not in effect.

Online holiday spending is running 9% ahead of last year, but fewer people plan to shop this weekend

The forecasts point to a season carried by higher spending per shopper, a pattern that fits stalled real consumer spending in October.

Adobe Analytics says U.S. shoppers spent $82.5 billion online from November 1 to 22, up 9.1% from a year earlier, including $6.1 billion through buy-now-pay-later plans. It forecasts $6.9 billion of online spending today (+8.5%, 63% of it on phones), $12.9 billion on Black Friday (+9.2%) and about $15.1 billion on Cyber Monday, with the season reaching $275.1 billion, up 6.7% (Adobe, via Digital Commerce 360). The National Retail Federation forecasts November–December sales up 4.2%, but expects 182.7 million people to shop over the five-day weekend, down from 186.9 million last year. These are nominal figures, not adjusted for prices.

Why it matters Wednesday’s data showed October spending falling slightly after inflation while incomes jumped on one-time payments. Online sales rising 9% with fewer shoppers suggests the money is there but concentrated; the first actual weekend figures arrive Friday and Saturday.

Lean’s developers certify an AI-produced proof of the Riemann hypothesis

It is the strongest endorsement yet of the claim. No one has yet written the argument down in a form a human can follow.

The Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement on November 21 saying, “We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct,” checked against Mathlib’s existing statement of the problem, according to Nature and Scientific American as summarized. The MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland independently compiled and rechecked the roughly 17-million-line proof with Lean’s kernel using only its three standard axioms; one end-to-end test was still pending as of November 23. Lior Alpöge, the Anthropic researcher behind the claim, says Claude did the technical work between October 6 and November 16. Our Wednesday edition described the proof as still being checked; this certification predates that report, and we had missed it.

Mathematicians are cautious about what has been shown. James Maynard called it “a historic milestone” if correct; Daniel Litt said it is “exceedingly likely to be correct… No human understands it yet”; Maksym Radziwiłł noted that it certifies the result without explaining why it is true. There is no peer-reviewed paper and no human-readable proof, and the Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers $1 million for a solution, still lists the problem as open.

Nature news, Scientific American, Smithsonian (as summarized) · Lean FRO and Mathlib joint statement (as quoted) · Clay Mathematics Institute problem list. Not peer-reviewed.

Wind postpones the Macy’s parade for the first time in its 100 years

An East Coast storm is bringing rain, high winds and coastal flooding on one of the year’s busiest travel days.

New York City, Macy’s and NBC moved the 100th Thanksgiving Day Parade to Saturday at 8:30 AM because of forecast gusts up to 60 mph and National Weather Service high-wind and coastal-flood warnings for Thursday into Friday. A spokesperson called it the first postponement in the parade’s history; it was cancelled from 1942 to 1944 during the Second World War. NBC will air a rerun of the 2025 parade this morning (CNN, AP, others). Officials in the Philadelphia area, under a high-wind warning, are discouraging travel today, and airlines including American, Delta, United and Southwest have issued travel waivers; about 500 flights were reported delayed at Miami and Dallas–Fort Worth (CNN, NPR, NBC Philadelphia, as summarized). The storm should clear most of the East by Saturday.

CNN, AP via OPB, SF Chronicle, WCVB · National Weather Service · NPR, Weather.com, NBC Philadelphia (travel, as summarized).

Risks & Scenarios

Friday’s half-session holds Thursday’s gains. We put the chance of the S&P 500 closing Friday above Wednesday’s 7,182.78 at 55–70%, on the basis that futures are 0.55% higher and overseas markets rallied, set against thin holiday trading and a VIX still near 28. It would be wrong if the Gulf claims are confirmed or a hot Tokyo inflation figure sends the yen and Japanese yields sharply higher.

Japan raises rates in December. We put the chance of a Bank of Japan increase at its December 17–18 meeting at 65–80%. Basis: swaps price about 80%, and a board member and the governor have both leaned that way this week; Japan’s central bank has surprised markets before. If it happens, expect pressure on U.S. long-term yields through Japanese demand for Treasuries.

The ECB raises rates on December 17. We put this at 65–80%: the move is widely expected after two increases this year, and the risk is that the October account or November inflation data shows a divided council.

The Gulf escalates. We do not put a number on whether Ras Tanura was damaged: the evidence is a claim, smoke in satellite images and one unnamed source, against a Saudi statement that the drones were shot down. If damage is confirmed, Brent would likely move several dollars higher.

The United States

Birthright citizenship. Judge Christopher Cooper of the federal district court in Washington issued a classwide preliminary injunction on Wednesday against President Trump’s August 2026 executive order on birthright citizenship, in a 67-page opinion, after a similar injunction in Maryland in September. Science

Heart disease genetics. A peer-reviewed study in Nature on Wednesday from Washington University in St. Louis maps, cell by cell, which genetic variants linked to coronary artery disease switch which genes on in artery tissue (DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-11275-z). Maps like this help researchers turn the long lists of risk variants from genome studies into specific genes that drugs could target; it is a research resource, not a treatment.
