1 · Refresher: Module 3’s spending and saving identities

Lesson 4 covered GDP counting; here is the identity today builds on.

Two ways to split the same income. Every unit of output is bought by someone: households (C), firms investing (I), government (G) or foreigners, net of what we import (X − M). The same income is also either consumed, saved (S) or paid in net taxes (T). Set the two splits equal and C cancels (pp.77–80).

C + I + G + (X − M) = C + S + T  ⇒  S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)

S
private saving, currency per year (p.80)
G − T
the fiscal deficit (positive when government spends more than it collects)
X − M
net exports; positive is a trade surplus

Private saving has three uses: firms’ investment, lending to a government in deficit, or lending abroad. It is an identity; it never says which piece moved first (p.80).

Clearing up: the sign of X − M

X − M is sales abroad minus purchases. Sell more than you buy and foreigners owe you: a positive number is a surplus, and you are lending abroad. Today’s CA = Sp + Sg − I says the same, with a government deficit as negative Sg.

Memorize
  • S = I + (G − T) + (X − M)
  • X − M > 0: surplus, lending abroad

2 · Today: trade, the balance of payments and the saving gap

Where output happens vs who owns it. GDP counts final output produced inside the borders, whoever owns the factories. GNP counts output from the country’s own citizens’ labor and capital, wherever they work. A foreign carmaker’s US plant is in US GDP and the foreign country’s GNP (p.190).

Terms of trade = export price index ÷ import price index: how many imports one unit of exports buys. A rise is an improvement (pp.190–191). Autarky is no trade, at home-only prices; an open economy trades at world prices (p.191).

Benefits and costs. Trade lets each country specialize, reach larger markets and more variety, and face more competition; the cost falls on workers and firms in industries that lose out to imports (Summary p.208). FDI is long-term ownership of productive assets abroad; FPI is shorter-term holding of foreign securities (p.193).

Opportunity cost, not productivity, sets trade. Absolute advantage is producing a good with fewer resources. Comparative advantage is a lower opportunity cost: giving up less of the other good to make it. A country better at everything still gains by exporting the good where its edge is largest and buying the other (pp.195–196).

Opportunity cost of A = output of B per worker ÷ output of A per worker

Units
units of B given up per unit of A; the lower figure has the comparative advantage (p.195)

Who gains. Both sides gain only if the trading price lies strictly between their two autarky prices. The closer it sits to the partner’s autarky price, the more of the total gain you keep (p.196). Comparative advantage moves over time with capital, skills, technology and policy (pp.197–198).

Two models of where advantage comes from. Ricardian: labor is the only input, and technology differences create the advantage. Heckscher–Ohlin: technology is the same everywhere; a country exports goods that use its relatively plentiful factor (capital or labor) intensively. Because demand for a factor is derived from demand for what it makes, trade raises the plentiful factor’s income and lowers the scarce factor’s. Only H–O shows this; the two models complement each other (pp.198–199).

The balance of payments is double-entry bookkeeping, so its accounts sum to zero. A debit is a rise in assets or a fall in liabilities, so an import is a debit; “debit” is not “bad” (pp.200–201).

Current account
goods, services, investment income, one-way transfers such as remittances; fees for services go here (pp.201–202)
Capital account
capital transfers and sales of non-produced, non-financial assets such as rights (pp.202–203)
Financial account
changes in assets owned abroad and foreign-owned assets at home (p.203)

A current account gap is a saving–investment gap. Start from Y = C + I + G + X − M, split saving into private and government, and the chain ends in one line (pp.204–206). A deficit means the nation invests more than it saves and borrows the difference abroad: inter-temporal trade, spending now and repaying later (p.205).

CA = Sp + Sg − I

Sp
private saving: disposable income minus consumption (p.205)
Sg
government saving: taxes minus purchases minus net transfers; negative in deficit (p.205)
I
investment; all in currency per year (p.206)

Quality of a deficit. Borrowing for productive investment can raise future capacity to repay; borrowing for consumption does not. Persistent deficits pile up foreign claims, so investors may demand a higher risk premium and the currency can weaken over time (p.206).

Assumptions and exceptions
  • The reading treats X − M as the current account, ignoring income and transfers.
  • CA = Sp + Sg − I is an identity: a fiscal deficit need not cause a current account deficit if private saving rises.
Memorize
  • GDP: where. GNP: whose.
  • Terms of trade = PX ÷ PM; higher is better
  • Export the good with the lower opportunity cost
  • H–O: plentiful factor gains, scarce factor loses
  • Import = debit; BOP sums to zero
  • CA = Sp + Sg − I
