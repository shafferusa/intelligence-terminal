1 · Refresher: the two tools Module 7 borrows

Lesson 4 refreshed Modules 1–3; here are the two ideas Module 7 leans on.

Elasticity decides whether spending rises or falls. Own-price elasticity is the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price (p.7). When it is above 1, buyers cut quantity by more than the price rose, so their total spending falls; below 1, spending rises (Summary p.28). Module 7 applies exactly this rule to a country’s import bill after its currency weakens.

The saving identity becomes the trade balance. From Module 3, S = I + (G − T) + (X − M) (p.80). Move terms and you get Module 7’s form: X − M = (S − I) + (T − G). A trade surplus is national saving left over after investment.

Clearing up: why elasticity is quoted as a positive number

Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so the raw ratio is always negative and the sign tells you nothing. Module 7 builds a minus sign into the definition so the number is positive: the same as the absolute value. Compare it with 1, never with 0.

Memorize
  • Elasticity > 1: a price rise lowers total spending
  • X − M = (S − I) + (T − G)

2 · Today: Modules 4–6 in brief, then currency exchange rates

Module 4, business cycles. Postponable spending (durables, investment, borrowed-for housing) swings the cycle (pp.138–142). Unemployment lags, and mild inflation is preferred because deflation raises real debt burdens (pp.145–154). Lesson 3 taught it in full.

Video recap: Business Cycles- Macro Topic 2.7 (Jacob Clifford, 5 min), the phases of the cycle and why recessions are hard to predict.

Module 5, money. Banks create deposits by lending; 1 ÷ reserve requirement is a ceiling, not a forecast (pp.174–176). M × V = P × Y is an identity; nominal rates track expected inflation, and surprise inflation moves wealth from lenders to borrowers (pp.178–183). Lesson 4 taught it in full.

Video recap: Fractional Reserve Banking and the Money Multiplier Made Simple (Marginal Revolution University, 7 min), how banks create deposits from reserves and why the multiplier is only a ceiling. Module 6, trade. Export what costs you least to give up (pp.194–199); CA = Sp + Sg − I (pp.204–206). Lesson 5 taught it in full.

Module 7 asks three questions: what is an exchange rate, how do people use the currency market, and when does a weaker currency actually improve the trade balance?

An exchange rate is a price. It is the cost of one currency in units of another (Summary p.231). A quote of “dollars per euro” is the price of a euro. The nominal rate is the number on the screen. The real rate adjusts it for both countries’ price levels: what your money buys abroad versus at home. The book only names it; the general rule:

Real rate (domestic per foreign) = nominal rate × (foreign price level ÷ domestic price level)

Nominal rate
units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency
Price levels
index numbers; a rising real rate means foreign goods are getting dearer for you

Percentage changes are not mirror images. If the quote is dollars per euro, the euro’s change is new quote ÷ old quote − 1. The dollar’s change against the euro uses the inverted quote, old ÷ new − 1, so a 10% euro rise is a dollar fall of a little under 10%.

Risk exists before any money changes hands. A firm that will receive euros in 60 days is exposed now, because the spot rate on that day is unknown; a forward fixes the rate today (pp.218–219). Hedgers use the market to remove that risk, speculators to take it on (p.219).

Spot
immediate exchange, usually settled two business days later (p.219)
Outright forward
rate agreed now for a later date; over the counter, so the other side can default (p.219)
Futures
standardized, exchange-traded, settled daily through margin, so default risk is lower (p.220)
FX swap
a spot trade plus the opposite forward; not the same as a currency swap (p.220)
FX option
the right, not the obligation, to exchange at a set rate, bought for a premium (p.221)

Rolling a forward settles only the difference. When a forward expires and the hedge must continue, the firm offsets it with a spot trade; the foreign amounts cancel, and one side pays notional × (contracted forward − spot on the roll date), in the quote currency, to whoever gained. A new forward carries the hedge on (p.220). Liquidity thins as contracts get longer and larger (pp.221–222).

A hedged foreign bond: convert at spot, earn the foreign rate, convert back at today’s forward (pp.221–223).

Elasticities approach: relative prices. A depreciation (market-driven) or devaluation (official, under a fixed rate) makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports dearer at home (p.223). Export prices are fixed in domestic currency, so export revenue can only rise, through volume. The import bill is the risk: its domestic price rises by the full depreciation, and it falls only if import demand is elastic.

%ΔR ≈ (1 − ε) × %ΔP   and   wXεX + wM(εM − 1) > 0

R
spending, price × quantity; the approximation holds for small changes (p.224)
wX, wM
exports and imports as shares of total trade; they sum to 1 (p.224)
εX, εM
foreign demand elasticity for exports; home demand elasticity for imports; unitless, positive (p.224)

That second line is the Marshall–Lerner condition: if it holds, depreciation moves the balance toward surplus. With balanced trade it reduces to εX + εM > 1. A deficit country has the larger import share, so import elasticity matters most (p.224).

The J-curve is a timing risk. Signed orders and slow habits keep elasticities low at first, so the balance worsens before it improves (pp.225–226).

Absorption approach: income versus spending. The trade balance is national income minus domestic spending, the same as saving minus investment, so a cheaper currency helps only if it raises income relative to spending (pp.226–227). With idle capacity, demand switches to home goods, output rises and part of the extra income is saved. At full employment, prices rise and the gain fades unless spending falls.

The wealth effect helps briefly: a weaker currency shrinks what savings buy, so households save to rebuild them, then stop (p.227). And the trade balance cannot move alone: a surplus is matched by a capital account deficit, so asset prices must shift until foreign assets look more attractive to investors (pp.227–230).

Assumptions and exceptions
  • Marshall–Lerner assumes export prices fixed in domestic currency and import prices in foreign currency, and ignores the J-curve (p.224).
  • Lasting change needs a shift in saving, investment or the fiscal balance; the currency alone rarely does it (pp.227–229).
Memorize
  • Real rate = nominal × (Pforeign ÷ Pdomestic)
  • wXεX + wM(εM − 1) > 0; balanced trade: εX + εM > 1
  • J-curve: worse first, better later
  • X − M = (S − I) + (T − G)
