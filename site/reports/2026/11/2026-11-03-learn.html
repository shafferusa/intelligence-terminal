Philadelphia Held a Parade During a Flu Epidemic. St. Louis Shut Its Doors. The Peak Death Rates Split Eightfold.

Tuesday, November 3, 2026 · 13 min read

A reinforcing loop amplifies whatever push it gets and a balancing loop pushes back toward a limit, and which one is steering a system at a given moment is written into the loop’s own wiring, not into anyone’s intentions.

Systems Thinking

Day 26 of 293 · Systems Thinking 2 of 12 · Feedback loops: reinforcing and balancing

A parade for 200,000 in a city already infected

On September 28, 1918, Philadelphia went ahead with a Liberty Loan parade down Broad Street that drew an estimated 200,000 people, to sell war bonds, even though influenza had already reached sailors at the Navy Yard days earlier. Within 72 hours the city’s hospitals were reporting they could not keep up with new admissions. Philadelphia’s health director did not ban public gatherings — including further Liberty Loan events — until October 3, five days after the parade, once the epidemic was already climbing fast.

St. Louis was hit by the same autumn wave at almost the same time. Its first reported civilian cases appeared around October 5. Two days later, on October 7, the city closed schools, theatres, churches and other places people gathered, citywide, while its case count was still small.

Decades later the epidemiologist Richard Hatchett, the physician Carter Mecher and the biostatistician Marc Lipsitch reconstructed both cities’ weekly mortality records for a 2007 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Philadelphia’s epidemic peaked at a weekly excess death rate of about 257 per 100,000 people. St. Louis’s peak, in its own worst week, reached only about 31 per 100,000 — roughly an eighth as high.

The same virus hit both cities within days of each other, in the same country, in the same autumn. Why did one city’s worst week kill at eight times the rate of the other’s?

The Big Idea

Today’s concept: a reinforcing loop amplifies whatever push it gets, feeding more of the same back into itself, and a balancing loop pushes back toward some limit or goal, cancelling a push rather than amplifying it. Most of what a system actually does over time — whether it runs away, levels off, or oscillates — comes down to which kind of loop currently has the upper hand, and for how long it keeps it.

A loop is simply a closed chain of cause and effect: A changes B, B changes C, and C changes A again, so the effect eventually feeds back into its own cause. Trace a reinforcing loop around once and a push on A comes back around as a further push on A in the same direction — growth that feeds itself, like compound interest. Trace a balancing loop around once and a push on A comes back around pushing A the other way — a thermostat correcting a room back toward its setpoint.

Nothing says a system runs on only one loop. Most real systems carry several at once, some reinforcing and some balancing, wired into the same chain of causes, and which one is actually steering the system’s behaviour at a given moment — a condition called loop dominance — can shift as conditions change, often without any single big external shock.

How It Works

A causal-loop diagram is a map of a loop’s links, each marked with its polarity. A link from X to Y is positive if pushing X up, all else equal, pushes Y up too, and negative if pushing X up pushes Y down. Polarity describes direction, not whether a change is good or bad — a negative link is not a bad thing, only a link that reverses direction.

The whole loop’s classification follows from one count: trace the chain all the way around, back to where it started, and count how many of its links are negative. A loop is reinforcing if that count is even — including zero negative links, which still counts as even — and balancing if the count is odd. Each negative link flips the direction of a push once; an even number of flips returns the push to its original direction, and an odd number reverses it.

Trace the epidemic’s simplest loop first. More infected people in a city produce more infectious contacts overall; more infectious contacts produce more new infections, because each contact is a chance to transmit; and more new infections directly adds to the count of people infected, closing the loop back to where it started. Each of those three links is positive, so the loop has zero negative links. Zero is even. This is R, the contagion loop, and it is reinforcing: the more people are infected, the faster new infections are produced, with nothing in the loop itself to slow it down.

A second loop runs through the pool of people who can still catch the disease. As more people become infected, the pool of remaining susceptible people shrinks — a negative link, since infected and susceptible move in opposite directions. As the susceptible pool shrinks, new infections per day shrink too, because fewer people are left to transmit to even at the same rate of contact — a positive link, since susceptible and new infections move the same direction, both falling. New infections then feed infected the same positive way as before. One negative link in a loop of three is an odd count, so this is B1, a balancing loop: contagion eventually runs out of fuel on its own, simply because an epidemic burns through the susceptible population it depends on.

A third loop runs through the response itself. More deaths push a city toward tighter closures — a positive link, deaths and closure-strength moving the same direction. Tighter closures cut the contacts people have with each other — a negative link, since closure-strength up means contacts down. Fewer contacts, as already traced, produce fewer new infections and eventually fewer deaths — two more positive links closing the loop back to deaths. One negative link again: an odd count, so B2, the deliberate closing of schools, theatres and churches, is also a balancing loop — but one that depends on someone actually pulling the lever, unlike B1, which runs on its own.

None of these three loops switches off while the others run; all three are always present, pulling against one another at once. What changes is which one has the most leverage at a given point — loop dominance. Early on, with almost everyone still susceptible and almost no one dead yet, B1 and B2 barely register, and R runs nearly unopposed: an epidemic that starts exponential stays exponential for a while. As infections mount, both balancing loops gain strength — the susceptible pool measurably shrinks, and deaths mount high enough to force a response — until their combined pull overtakes R’s, and growth bends over into decline. Where that crossover happens, and at what cost, is the whole difference between Philadelphia and St. Louis. The same three-step method — write the chain, mark each link’s polarity, count the negative links — works on any closed causal chain, from an epidemic to a bank run to the reward system of science itself, which is where the Example section below goes next.

Back to the story

In Philadelphia, R ran almost unopposed through a parade that handed the loop 200,000 fresh contacts at once, while B2 waited five days — until October 3 — to even begin pulling against it. By the time closures finally engaged, the epidemic had already grown large enough that B1, the slower loop of simply running out of susceptible people, was doing most of the work of eventually bending the curve — at a far higher peak. St. Louis engaged B2 on October 7, within two days of its first cases, while the epidemic was still small enough that a deliberate cut in contacts carried real leverage against R. The virus itself did not differ between the two cities; the moment each city let its balancing loop start pulling did.

Key Concepts

Reinforcing loop
A closed chain of cause and effect with an even number of negative links (zero counts as even); a push through it returns as a further push in the same direction, so it amplifies whatever change it gets.
Balancing loop
A closed chain of cause and effect with an odd number of negative links; a push through it returns reversed, pushing the system back toward some limit, ceiling or goal.
Loop polarity
Whether a single link is positive (the two variables move the same direction) or negative (they move opposite directions); polarity describes direction, not whether the effect is desirable.
Causal-loop diagram
A map of a system’s feedback structure: the variables, the links between them, and each link’s polarity, drawn as a closed chain.
Shifting loop dominance
The point at which the loop actually steering a system’s behaviour changes, because the relative strength of the loops present has crossed over — often without any single external shock.
Matthew effect
Robert Merton’s 1968 name for the reinforcing loop in which an already-eminent person or institution receives disproportionate credit for a contribution, which then makes further eminence more likely.

Example: The Reward System of Science

Robert K. Merton named this same reinforcing structure inside science itself, in a 1968 paper in Science titled “The Matthew Effect in Science” — after the Gospel of Matthew’s line that to those who have, more will be given. Drawing heavily on the sociologist Harriet Zuckerman’s interviews with Nobel laureates, Merton found that when scientists of very different standing collaborate, credit for the joint work tends to flow disproportionately to whichever collaborator is already the most eminent, regardless of who actually did the work or whose name appears first. Zuckerman’s own data showed something concrete behind the pattern: roughly two-thirds of the papers published by the Nobel laureates she studied had at least one co-author, which means most of a laureate’s output was never a solo-credit question to begin with — it was a credit-allocation question, decided again and again by the same tilt toward the already-famous name.

The loop is the same structure taught above, applied to a different system. Being already eminent brings a scientist more credit for new work; more credit brings more visibility, funding and access to talented collaborators; more of those resources make further prominent work more likely, which brings still more eminence. Margaret Rossiter named the system’s companion failure in 1993: the Matilda effect, after the nineteenth-century suffragist Matilda Joslyn Gage, who had herself pointed out that women’s scientific work was routinely credited to men — Rosalind Franklin’s contribution to the structure of DNA among the best-known cases. Where the Matthew effect concentrates credit among the already-famous, the Matilda effect can cut a contributor out of the loop before it ever starts compounding for her.

1. Name the chain. Eminence → credit for new work → visibility, funding and collaborators → further prominent work → eminence.

2. Mark each link’s polarity. More eminence → more credit (positive). More credit → more resources (positive). More resources → more prominent work (positive). More prominent work → more eminence (positive).

3. Count the negative links. Zero.

4. Apply the rule. Zero is even, so the loop is reinforcing: scientific eminence feeds itself with nothing in the loop’s own structure to slow it down — exactly like the epidemic’s contagion loop R.

Equation / Model

loop is reinforcing ⇔ (negative links in the loop) mod 2 = 0; balancing ⇔ mod 2 = 1

A loop is reinforcing exactly when the number of negative links it contains, divided by two, leaves no remainder — that is, the count is even, including zero. It is balancing exactly when that count is odd.

negative link
a link in the loop where the two variables it connects move in opposite directions
mod 2
the remainder after dividing by two; 0 means even, 1 means odd

The 1918 epidemic’s causal-loop diagram, in the same compact form:

R (reinforcing)
infected → contacts → new infections → infected. Three positive links, zero negative: reinforcing.
B1 (balancing)
infected → susceptibles depleted (negative link) → new infections (positive link) → infected (positive link). One negative link: balancing.
B2 (balancing)
deaths → closures (positive link) → contacts (negative link) → new infections → deaths (two more positive links). One negative link: balancing.

Why It Matters

Once you can trace a loop and count its negative links, you gain a specific kind of foresight: you can tell, from the wiring alone, whether a system is likely to run away or correct itself, often before enough data exists to see the trend directly. A system that looks calm can tip into runaway change — or a system that looks like it is spiralling can level off — without any new external shock at all, purely because the relative strength of the loops already present has crossed over. That is a more specific question than “is this getting worse”: it is “which loop currently has the leverage, and what would it take to shift it.”

Settled. The loop mechanics taught here, and the raw difference in peak weekly death rates between Philadelphia (about 257 per 100,000) and St. Louis (about 31 per 100,000), are not in dispute.

Debated. How much those early interventions reduced total deaths, rather than merely flattening and delaying the peak, is argued: Hatchett, Mecher and Lipsitch, and separately Howard Markel’s team, find real benefit from early, sustained closures, while the economist Robert Barro’s 2020 analysis of the same cities found the largely short-lived 1918 measures did little to cut total deaths over the full epidemic. How much the Matthew effect reflects real differences in merit, rather than pure visibility, is separately debated among sociologists of science.

Value judgment. Whether a reward system that channels credit toward the already-eminent is a tolerable cost of running science at all, or an injustice that should be actively corrected, is a question about fairness the loop diagram itself cannot settle — it can only show that the concentration is structural, not incidental.

Across. Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais described an identical structure in orbital mechanics in a 1978 paper in the Journal of Geophysical Research: debris in low Earth orbit collides with satellites and other debris, and each collision creates more fragments, which go on to cause more collisions — a reinforcing loop with the same zero-negative-link structure as the epidemic’s contagion loop R. Unlike B1 and B2 in 1918, the debris loop’s only natural balancing loop is slow atmospheric drag pulling fragments back down over years or decades, which is why the Kessler syndrome is treated as a live risk to low orbit rather than a self-correcting nuisance.

Remember These
  • A reinforcing loop has an even number of negative links (zero counts) and amplifies whatever push it gets; a balancing loop has an odd number and pushes back.
  • Trace a loop by writing the chain, marking each link positive or negative, and counting the negative links around the whole circuit.
  • Real systems usually run several loops at once; which one is steering behaviour — loop dominance — can shift without any new external shock.
  • Philadelphia let its contagion loop run five days unopposed after a 200,000-person parade; St. Louis engaged its balancing loop within two days of its first cases.
