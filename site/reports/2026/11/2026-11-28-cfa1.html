1 · Refresher: depreciation spreads a cost already paid

From the income statement module you finished on Nov 22; Lesson 8 gave the headline.

The principle. The cash left when the asset was bought (investing). Depreciation (tangible assets) and amortization (intangibles) only decide in which years that cost reaches the income statement, matched to the years the asset earns revenue. Method, life and residual value move profit between years, never total cost or cash (pp.46–50).

Straight-line takes the same slice each year: cost minus residual value, divided by the useful life (p.47). Diminishing balance applies a fixed rate to what is left: the acceleration factor times one over the life, times the opening net book value. Residual value is not subtracted from that base, but depreciation stops once book value reaches it; with little or no residual the balance never reaches zero, so firms switch to straight-line late in the life (pp.48–49).

Clearing up

One cost is being sliced, not added to. Accelerated methods cut thick slices early and thin ones late; the slices still sum to the same total. A longer life or higher residual thins each slice and lifts profit with no change in the asset, which is why analysts judge such policies more or less conservative.

Memorize
  • Straight-line = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life
  • Diminishing balance = (factor × 1 ÷ life) × opening net book value; residual is not in the base
  • Factor 2.0 = double-declining; stop at residual; method changes timing, never cash

2 · Today: who gains from trade, and what each currency regime gives up

Who wins and loses from trade and its restrictions, and how are currencies quoted and managed?

Trade grows the pie, not every slice. A country sells what it makes relatively cheaply at a better price abroad and buys the rest for less. Resources move into export industries and out of import-competing ones. The gains are national: winners could compensate losers, but usually nobody pays. Gains come from comparative advantage, scale, variety (exporting and importing within one category is intra-industry trade), competition and learning. The costs are adjustment costs: shrinking firms and workers whose skills fit only that industry (pp.195–197).

Tariffs. A small country is a price taker, not a small place: it cannot move the world price, so a per-unit tariff lifts the home price by the full tariff. Domestic producers gain area A; consumers lose A+B+C+D; the government collects C on the imports that remain. C is a transfer inside the country, so the national loss is only B+D: extra production at a higher cost than imports (B) and purchases no longer made (D) (pp.198–199). A large country can push the world price down and gain, if there is no retaliation and the gain exceeds B+D; the world still loses.

Quotas, VERs and subsidies. A quota caps imported quantity and gives the same price and quantity as the matching tariff, but area C becomes quota rent, profit from scarcity. Who holds the licenses decides the cost: auctioned by the government, the loss stays B+D; held by foreigners, it is B+C+D. A voluntary export restraint is the exporter’s own cap, so the exporter keeps the rent (pp.200–201). An export subsidy pays firms per unit sold abroad: they shift sales out, the home price rises, the budget pays, and a large country also hands part of the subsidy to foreign buyers through a lower world price. Importers can answer with countervailing duties (pp.201–202). Under all four policies producers gain and consumers lose. Motives: infant industries, jobs, security, revenue, retaliation (pp.197–198).

Trading blocs. Groups of countries cut barriers among themselves because it is faster than a global deal. The ladder, from least to most integrated: free trade area (no internal barriers, each keeps its own outside tariffs), customs union (adds a common external policy), common market (adds free movement of labor and capital), economic union (adds common institutions and policy) and monetary union (adds one currency) (p.204). Trade creation replaces costly home output with cheaper member imports; trade diversion replaces cheaper outside imports, still tariffed, with costlier member imports. The bloc helps if creation outweighs diversion (p.205).

Reading a quote. A/B means units of A, the price currency, per one unit of B, the base currency. When A/B rises, the base has strengthened. A direct quote puts your domestic currency as the price currency; an indirect quote is its reciprocal. Markets use fixed conventions instead (pp.216, 227). A dealer buys the base at the bid and sells it at the higher offer; the client pays the gap. Inverting a two-sided quote swaps them: the new bid is one over the old offer (p.229).

Nominal versus real. The nominal rate is what trades. The real exchange rate is an index adjusting for price levels: did foreign goods get dearer for a domestic buyer? It rises (less purchasing power) when the domestic currency weakens or foreign prices rise faster, and falls when domestic prices rise faster. It forecasts nominal moves poorly (pp.216–220).

R(d/f) = S(d/f) × Pf ÷ Pd

Who trades. Dealing banks (the sell side) quote to clients (the buy side), from companies and real money funds to central banks. Most volume is investment and speculation, and FX swaps (spot plus an offsetting forward) are the largest instrument (pp.221–226).

The regime conflict. With capital free to move and a credible peg, a rate cut drives money out; the central bank must sell foreign currency to hold the peg, which shrinks reserves and the money supply and pushes rates back up. So the peg costs it control of interest rates. Under a float, the same cut weakens the currency, which adds export stimulus. Controls or a looser peg buy back some control, with distortions (pp.231–232).

The regime ladder, least to most flexible: dollarization (using another currency), monetary union, currency board (a legal commitment with base money fully backed by reserves, so imbalances hit interest rates, not the rate), fixed parity (held with discretionary reserves, so it can be abandoned or attacked), target zone, crawling peg or band, managed float, independent float. Dollarization imports credibility, not creditworthiness (pp.232–242).

Assumptions and exceptions
  • The tariff areas assume a small country, straight-line curves and no retaliation.
  • A fixed parity has no legal backing, so it is less credible than a currency board.
Memorize
  • Tariff: producers +A, consumers −(A+B+C+D), government +C, nation −(B+D)
  • Quota rent to foreigners or a VER: nation −(B+C+D)
  • FTA → customs union → common market → economic union → monetary union
  • A/B up = base B stronger; bid < offer; inverting swaps them
  • Free capital, peg, independent rates: pick two
