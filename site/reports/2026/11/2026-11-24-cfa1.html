1 · Refresher: receivables, classification and why growth eats cash

Lesson 8 refreshed depreciation, OCI and the cash flow statement; these are finished Nov 22 points it left out.

Receivables are shown net of an estimate. Bad debt expense raises the allowance (a contra asset) and cuts profit with no cash moving. A write-off lowers receivables and the allowance equally, so net receivables and profit do not change. Trimming the estimate lifts profit without cash (pp.71–72).

Current means within the longer of a year or one operating cycle. A liability is also current if the firm cannot unconditionally defer it (pp.67–68).

Clearing up

Your workbook flags multi-period forecasting. Working capital is tied to sales at a fixed share, so when sales grow, that share of the growth is funded before customers pay: cash rises by less than profit and can fall. A days-sales-outstanding target fixes receivables once sales are set (pp.277–280).

Memorize
  • % uncollectible = allowance ÷ (net receivables + allowance), using gross, not net
  • Bad debt expense hits profit; a write-off does not
  • Δ cash = NI + non-cash items − Δ working capital − capex

2 · Today: measuring the cycle, its phases, credit and indicators

How do you tell where the economy is in its cycle, and which data tell you first? Lesson 3 covered how households, housing and jobs behave over the cycle; today adds how the cycle is measured and read.

Cycles recur but are not regular: one can last just over a year or more than a decade (p.51). There are three ways to measure them. The classical cycle tracks the level of output: a fall in output is a contraction. The growth cycle tracks output against potential, the output gap. The growth rate cycle tracks changes in the growth rate; it needs no estimate of trend and flags turns first. At peaks the order is growth rate, then growth cycle, then classical; at troughs it is growth rate, then classical, then growth cycle (pp.52–53).

Four phases, on the growth-cycle view. Recovery: output is below potential but the gap is closing; firms add overtime before hiring, so unemployment stays high. Expansion: the gap turns positive and spending, hiring and investment rise. Slowdown: the gap is at its widest, growth is slowing and inflation is still rising. Contraction: output is below potential; firms cut hours, freeze hiring, then lay off (p.54).

Jobs lag because people are costly to hire and train. Firms wait to be sure a turn is real, so they keep staff early in a downturn and use overtime early in a recovery. Output per hour peaks late in a recession (pp.60–61).

Markets lead. Risky assets rally 3–6 months before the economy bottoms. In a boom the riskiest assets gain most and government bonds fall; in contraction investors pay up for safe bonds, utilities and staples (pp.55–56).

Capex and inventories amplify the swings. Capex follows profits; short-lived items such as software are ordered first in recovery and cut first in contraction (pp.61–62). Early in recovery sales rise before production, so the inventory–sales ratio falls: normal, not weakness. In the slowdown sales fall faster than output and the ratio rises: that is the warning (pp.63–64).

Credit makes it worse. Lenders loosen in good times and tighten in bad. Tighter credit lowers property values and raises defaults, deepening the downturn. Credit cycles usually last longer than business cycles, and recessions with a credit and housing bust are longer and deeper. Macroprudential policy tries to damp the boom (pp.57–59).

Indicators, used together. Leading indicators turn before the economy (orders, permits, hours, market prices, expectations), coincident with it, and lagging after it (mostly labour costs, inflation, lending and household debt). One leading signal is not enough: a rising equity index is confirmed only if the broad leading composite also rises (pp.65–67). A nowcast estimates the current quarter before the official data arrive; the Atlanta Fed’s GDPNow is the example (pp.70–71).

A first look at fiscal policy. Monetary policy is the central bank acting on money and credit; fiscal policy is the government acting on taxes and spending. Both lean against the cycle (p.79). Lesson 4 introduced why both exist.

Output gap
actual output minus potential; positive when the economy runs above capacity (p.54)
Diffusion index
how many components of an index are rising: breadth, not size (p.71)

Diffusion index = (sum of component scores ÷ N) × 100

Score
1 for a component that rose by more than 0.05%, 0.5 for one that changed by less, 0 for one that fell by more (pp.71–72)
N
number of components; the index runs 0–100 and 50 is neutral
Inventory–sales ratio
inventories ÷ sales; in months of sales when sales are monthly (p.63)
Assumptions and exceptions
  • A slowdown is not a recession: the gap is still positive. The book’s text and its phase table disagree on slowdown inflation; go with accelerating.
  • Two quarters of falling real GDP is only a rule of thumb; dating committees call turns late (p.56).
  • Classify indicators from the book’s type table; the Conference Board index has a different membership.
Memorize
  • Peaks: growth rate → growth cycle → classical. Troughs: growth rate → classical → growth cycle
  • Unemployment lags; hours and overtime lead hiring
  • Inventory–sales ratio falls early in recovery, rises in the slowdown
  • Leading, coincident, lagging: turn before, with, after
