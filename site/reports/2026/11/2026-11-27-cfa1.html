1 · Refresher: the indirect method, from net income to operating cash

From the cash flow module you finished on Nov 22. Lesson 8 sorted cash flows into their three buckets; today is the bridge inside the operating bucket.

Why a bridge is needed. Net income is built on accruals; operating cash counts only money that moved. The indirect method starts from net income and undoes every accrual that involved no cash, showing why profit and cash differ. Direct and indirect give the same figure (p.99).

Three kinds of adjustment. First, add back non-cash charges such as depreciation: the expense lowered profit but no cash left this year (the cash went out when the asset was bought, in investing). Second, remove gains and add back losses on selling assets or retiring debt: those are not operating, and the full proceeds appear in investing or financing. Third, adjust for changes in operating working capital (pp.101, 108).

Clearing up

The working-capital signs follow one question: did this balance tie up cash or free it? A rise in receivables means sales were booked but the customers have not paid yet, so cash is lower than profit: subtract it. A rise in inventory means cash went into goods not yet sold: subtract. A rise in payables means you received goods or services and have not paid: cash is higher than expenses suggest, so add. Falls reverse every sign. An asset rising uses cash; a liability rising supplies it.

IFRS and US GAAP. Both allow either method. Under US GAAP, a firm using the direct method must also give the net-income reconciliation, and a firm using the indirect method must still disclose interest paid and taxes paid (pp.107, 109).

Memorize
  • CFO = net income + non-cash charges − gains + losses − increase in operating assets + increase in operating liabilities
  • Asset up = cash down; liability up = cash up
  • Only the operating section can be indirect

2 · Today: globalization, its tools, and pricing geopolitical risk

How do countries combine rules with open borders, what do they do to each other, and how should an investor size the risk? Lesson 11 covered why countries cooperate.

Globalization is the second axis. Cooperation is agreement on rules among governments; globalization is the flow of goods, capital, people and information across borders, driven mostly by private firms (p.152). They tend to move together but are separate: flows can continue without government support. The opposite of globalization is nationalism, putting one’s own economic interests first (p.154).

Why firms globalize, and what it costs. Firms go abroad for profit (more customers, cheaper inputs, scale), for access to resources and markets, and for non-financial gains. The costs: the gains are aggregate, so some workers and regions lose; standards can weaken if firms follow lax local rules; backlash builds; and interdependence spreads shocks. Capital comes in two forms: portfolio flows into securities, which can leave quickly, and foreign direct investment in productive capacity, which stays (pp.155–157). Facing rollback, a firm can bring essential production home (reshoring), rebuild its supply chain with duplicates (reglobalizing), or concentrate on key markets locally (doubling down). Full reversal is unlikely (pp.158–159).

The three institutions. The IMF watches member economies and lends to countries in balance-of-payments trouble, to keep the international monetary system stable. The World Bank funds long-term development in poorer countries: the IBRD lends to middle-income countries, the IDA gives low-cost credit to the poorest. The WTO sets and enforces the rules of trade between nations and settles disputes (pp.160–165).

Four archetypes. Put the two axes together and each country sits in a quadrant: multilateralism (cooperative and globalized), hegemony (globalized but non-cooperative, setting the rules for others), bilateralism (cooperative but nationalist, dealing one partner at a time) and autarky (neither: self-sufficiency). Interdependence makes a partner less likely to attack you but more likely to pass on its shocks. Watch movement: a change in the tools a country uses signals a change of quadrant (pp.167–171).

The tools. There are three families: national security (alliances at one end, armed conflict at the extreme), economic (trade pacts versus tariffs, quotas, nationalization) and financial (currency, capital access, sanctions). The test is simple: cooperative tools increase flows between countries; non-cooperative ones cut them. A dominant currency helps cooperation but exposes other countries to its issuer’s tightening (pp.172–175). Some tools mix purposes, such as cabotage, reserving domestic transport for national carriers (p.176).

Three types of risk. Event risk has a known date, such as an election. Exogenous risk arrives suddenly and unannounced, such as an invasion or a natural disaster. Thematic risk is known and evolves slowly, such as cyber threats or climate (pp.178–180). Knowing an event’s date lets you prepare; it does not make the event more or less likely.

Sizing the risk. Judge each risk on likelihood, velocity (how fast it reaches prices) and size and nature of impact. High velocity calls for short-term, tactical responses; medium velocity hits sectors; low velocity shifts asset allocation and long-run inputs. A likely but small risk needs little; an unlikely but large one deserves a scenario, not daily watching. Because outcomes are non-linear, plan with scenarios and signposts, indicators that a risk is rising or fading, and follow actual policy rather than political noise (pp.181–184).

How it reaches value. A persistent geopolitical risk raises the return investors require. A higher discount rate lowers the price of the same expected cash flows, which is why riskier markets trade at a discount (pp.177, 185–186).

Value of a level perpetual cash flow = cash flow per year ÷ required return

Assumptions and exceptions
  • Cooperation does not guarantee open flows, and open flows can survive without cooperation.
  • High velocity does not mean lasting damage: fast risks jolt prices; slow ones erode value over time.
  • Sanctions and nationalization are non-cooperative tools, even when announced as defensive.
Memorize
  • Multilateralism, hegemony, bilateralism, autarky: cooperative or not × globalized or nationalist
  • IMF = stability and crisis lending; World Bank = development; WTO = trade rules and disputes
  • Event = dated; exogenous = sudden; thematic = known and slow
  • Assess by likelihood, velocity, impact; respond with scenarios and signposts
  • Reshoring, reglobalizing, doubling down
