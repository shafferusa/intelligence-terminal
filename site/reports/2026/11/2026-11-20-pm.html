Closing Edition

Chip stocks crash and the S&P 500 falls 3.7% into a correction, its seventh straight loss

Friday, November 20, 2026 · 32 min read

The AI sell-off that began in Asia overnight hit Wall Street harder than futures had suggested: Nvidia fell 14% and the chip index 11%, the Nasdaq lost 5.3%, the VIX touched 40, and the only things that rose were Treasuries, consumer staples and a few drugmakers.

The Brief

If you read nothing else

  • The tape. The S&P 500 fell 3.70% to 6,910.39, its seventh straight loss, which outlets say is the longest run since February 2020. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% to 22,617.73, the Dow 1.93% (950 points) to 48,147.33 and the Russell 2000 2.76%. Several outlets report that the S&P 500 and the Dow are now in a correction, more than 10% below their records. For the week the S&P is down 8.6% and the Nasdaq 10.0%.
  • Chips. Nvidia fell 14.0% to $125.53, a new 52-week low, on 610 million shares, nearly four times its average. Micron fell 13.9%, AMD 13.3%, Broadcom 12.2% and the VanEck chip fund (SMH) 10.6%. Anthropic lost 8.5% in its first full session, to $88.91, after touching $83.05, five cents above its offer price.
  • Rates, the Fed and the Treasury. Treasuries had their best day in weeks. On the official curve, the 2-year fell 13 basis points to 4.63% and the 10-year 12 to 4.92%. Fed Governor Lisa Cook argued in Chile for further rate rises, and a quarter-point rise on December 9 is still priced at about 97%. Consumer sentiment rose to 51.5 in the University of Michigan’s final November reading.
  • Biggest risk. Volatility is now feeding on itself. The VIX closed at 35.89, up 26%, after touching 40.03, its highest in a year. The 9-day measure closed at 40.72, far above the 3-month at 30.94. Gold fell 4.4% and bitcoin about 5% alongside stocks, which points to forced selling rather than a rotation.
  • Next week. A short Thanksgiving week: markets shut Thursday and close at 1:00 PM Friday. October PCE inflation, the second estimate of third-quarter GDP and jobless claims are bunched into Wednesday, with $183 billion of 2-, 5- and 7-year notes for sale and Dell, Workday and Deere reporting.
  • Beyond markets. President Trump, back from Shenzhen, said he would restrict U.S. companies from building data centres abroad; no order or text has followed. A U.S. official told CBS of limited strikes on Iran’s Qeshm Island, which Central Command denied. In Pittsburgh, the transit authority approved its countywide bus-network redesign, effective August 2027.

Top Stories

Nvidia falls 14% as the chip sell-off drags the S&P 500 into a correction

The losses that began in Taipei and Seoul overnight reached Wall Street in full. The chip index had its worst week since 2001, one outlet reports, and the S&P 500 has lost 8.6% in five days.

The S&P 500 closed at 6,910.39, down 3.70%, after trading barely above Thursday’s close in the first minutes of the session (Yahoo Finance; CNBC and Reuters give the same close). The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% to 22,617.73, which outlets say erases its gain for the year, and the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ fell 5.67% on three times its usual volume. The Dow fell 1.93% to 48,147.33. It was cushioned by health care and staples: UnitedHealth rose 2.8%, Johnson & Johnson 1.3% and Walmart 1.5%. CNBC, NBC, CNN and Yahoo all report that the S&P 500 and the Dow have closed more than 10% below their records, the usual definition of a correction. On Yahoo’s data the S&P is 11.9% below its 52-week high.

Chips did the damage. Nvidia lost 14.01% to $125.53, Micron 13.89%, AMD 13.25%, Broadcom 12.19%, Marvell 11.69% and Arista 11.59%, and TSMC’s U.S. shares fell 9.48%. SMH, the chip fund, fell 10.60% to $400.06; it has lost 17.8% since last Friday, and Benzinga reports that the sector’s week was its worst since 2001. The technology sector fund fell 7.27%. The broader AI trade went with it: the Roundhill Magnificent Seven fund fell 5.18%, Meta 5.9%, Tesla 7.6%, Palantir 6.6%, and the AI-power names Vertiv (−10.1%), Vistra (−7.2%) and Constellation (−5.8%) all fell sharply.

The outlets we read gave no single catalyst. They pointed to doubts about the returns on AI spending, Anthropic’s weak second day and the hawkish Fed, much as they did on Thursday. NBC said Nvidia’s drop followed a Reuters report about questions over its accounting. We could not read that report, found no second outlet describing it in detail, and found no comment from Nvidia. The stock has been without a permanent chief executive since Jensen Huang resigned on November 12. Midday it was down about 7%; the second half of the fall came in the afternoon, and we cannot say which piece of news, if any, drove it.

Why it matters In a single week, the companies that carried the market for two years have become the source of its losses. Nvidia is down 48% from its 52-week high, and the S&P is now 6.0% below its 200-day average. Credit has not followed: high-yield bond funds fell only about 0.3%. While that holds, this is an equity repricing rather than a financial-stress event.
What’s next Losing streaks this long have usually ended with sharp rebounds, and the market is oversold on most short-term measures. But the S&P closed within 0.4% of its low for the day, and the next test is a holiday week with thin trading and a heavy data day on Wednesday.

Anthropic falls 8.5% on its second day, ending just above its offer price

The shares touched $83.05 in the afternoon, five cents above what IPO buyers paid, before recovering to $88.91.

ANT opened lower, as premarket trading had suggested, and was about $87 at midday (Fortune). It reached its low of $83.05 later in the session and closed at $88.91, down $8.26, on about 395 million shares, less than half Thursday’s volume (Yahoo Finance; CNBC also reports −8.5%). One data site gave a close of $84.54. That figure does not match the exchange-fed prices we use, so we set it aside. From the $111.50 first trade, the shares have lost 20% in a day and a half.

The bounce from $83.05 is the kind of move often attributed to underwriters supporting a new issue at its offer price. One crypto-news site reported that Morgan Stanley bought shares at $83. That is a single, unverified report. Banks do have a standard tool for this, an over-allotment option, but we have found no disclosure of its use.

Why it matters The offer price is now the line the market will watch. A close below $83 would mean IPO buyers had lost money within three sessions, in the largest IPO on record. Nvidia, which committed $10 billion to a private placement at the IPO price, is already showing a paper gain of only about 7% on it.

Treasuries rally hard; the 2-year yield drops 13 basis points

Bonds were the one big market that rose. Yields have fallen about a quarter of a point in a week, while the Fed is still expected to raise rates.

On Treasury’s official par curve, the 2-year yield fell to 4.63% from 4.76%, the 5-year to 4.71% from 4.86% and the 10-year to 4.92% from 5.04%, back below 5%. The 30-year fell 9 basis points to 5.31%. Short bills barely moved: the 3-month bill slipped to 4.32%. Since last Friday, the 2-year is down 25 basis points and the 10-year 23. TLT, the long-bond fund, rose 1.37%.

The rally did not come from a softer Fed. Governor Lisa Cook, speaking in Santiago, argued for more rate rises, said each additional day adds risk that inflation expectations move higher, and estimated October’s PCE inflation at 3.8% (Reuters, as carried by Investing.com and Yahoo). CME FedWatch still put a quarter-point rise on December 9 at about 97%, with the rest on a half point (FXStreet, Friday). So the market is pricing a rise next month and buying bonds anyway. That is consistent with investors starting to expect the Fed to stop sooner, or simply wanting safety, and today’s data cannot tell those apart.

Why it matters A curve falling across the board while the Fed talks tough is a bet that the Fed will not be able to keep raising rates into a falling stock market. If November’s payrolls and prices come in hot, that bet will be tested quickly.

Intuit falls another 13% after cutting its revenue outlook

The fall extended Thursday’s after-hours loss, and the stock is now 60% below its 52-week high.

Intuit closed at $270.44, down 12.77%, on three times its usual volume (Yahoo Finance). It had cut its full-year revenue guide to $23.08–23.37 billion and agreed to sell Mailchimp to Bending Spoons for about $3.68 billion including stock, while keeping its adjusted earnings guide of $22.88–23.12 a share. Reports this morning attributed the revenue cut to Credit Karma, not to the Mailchimp sale. Morgan Stanley and Bank of America cut their price targets to $310 and Jefferies to $445, as reported. Software fell broadly with it: Palo Alto Networks lost 5.1%, giving back most of Thursday’s gain.

Gold falls 4.4% and silver 8% as investors sell what they can

The haven metals fell with stocks for a second day. Gold is at its lowest since September 2025, according to Reuters as reported.

Gold futures ended near $3,523, down 4.4%, after a low of $3,502.90 (Yahoo Finance, delayed). The week’s loss is about 9%. Silver fell 7.9% to $40.84, and the gold fund GLD closed at $322.87, a new 52-week low. Bitcoin was about $58,800 late in the day, down roughly 5%, and ether fell nearly 8%. The dollar index was flat at 103.89, just below its 52-week high, and the yen gained about 0.5%.

Oil was the exception. The expiring December WTI contract settled at $83.98, up 77 cents, and Brent for January rose about 0.6% to $92.57 late in the day (Bloomberg, EnergyNow, as reported). Brent still lost about 3.4% on the week.

Why it matters When gold falls alongside stocks, it usually means investors are raising cash, for instance to meet margin calls, rather than moving into havens. That kind of selling tends to be sharp and short. It is also why bonds are the only hedge that worked this week.

Trump says he will restrict U.S. data centres abroad; no order has followed

The remark came at a news conference on his return from Shenzhen. It would cut across the Commerce Department’s new rule, which exempts American firms.

At the White House, the president said he would stop U.S. companies building data centres in other countries, and that he and President Xi had agreed on AI “guardrails” (CNN and NBC live coverage). No executive order, fact sheet or text has been published on either, and NBC reported that his own team had not been briefed on the data-centre idea. The Commerce Department’s interim rule of November 16 does something narrower: it requires licences, within 90 days, for overseas data centres that supply significant computing power to Chinese-headquartered companies, and it exempts U.S.-headquartered operators. The president also said Iran was negotiating in bad faith.

Why it matters Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Oracle are building abroad on a large scale. A restriction would hit their capital plans, the chip demand those plans support and the countries hosting the sites. Until there is a document, treat it as a statement.

The Economy

Today’s data. The University of Michigan’s final November sentiment index rose to 51.5 from the preliminary 51.0 and October’s 48.0, against a consensus of about 51.5–51.6 (university release, as reported by Bloomberg and Kitco). Expectations were 52.9 and current conditions 49.4. Year-ahead inflation expectations were reported at 3.7%, down from 4.7%, a large one-month drop. Survey director Joanne Hsu pointed to better expected business conditions after the election, and said sentiment is still 9% below February, before the conflict with Iran began. Interviews ran from October 20 to November 16, so they mostly predate this week’s market fall.

The Fed. Cook joins Waller (who floated a half point on Wednesday) and Musalem in arguing for more rises; New York Fed President John Williams said this week that conditions do not call for “dramatic action.” The policy rate is 3.75–4.00%. The question for December 9 is the size of the rise, and that turns on next Wednesday’s PCE prices and the November payroll and price reports due before the meeting. SOFR was 3.91% on Thursday (FRED). The Fed also published its survey of senior bank financial officers on reserve-management practices today.

Treasury. No auction today. Next week’s supply is heavy for a short week: $69 billion of 2-year, $70 billion of 5-year and $44 billion of 7-year notes, a $28 billion 2-year floating-rate note, and 6-, 13-, 26- and 52-week bills (TreasuryDirect). With yields down a quarter of a point in a week, the notes will be sold at much lower yields than investors expected a week ago. That tests whether buyers want duration at these prices or were only seeking safety.

Rates and credit. The curve rallied in parallel, so the 2s10s spread barely moved, widening a point to +29 basis points, while the 3-month/10-year spread narrowed 10 points to +60. The 10-year real yield was 2.88% and the 10-year breakeven 2.16% on Thursday (FRED), so most of the nominal yield is real yield rather than expected inflation. High-yield spreads widened for a fifth straight session, to 3.52 percentage points on Thursday (ICE BofA via FRED), and HYG closed 6 cents above its 52-week low. Spreads are wider by 32 basis points in a week but still low by history.

Abroad. European stocks fell more than on Thursday: Euro Stoxx 50 −2.81%, DAX −2.60%, CAC 40 −1.89%, FTSE 100 −1.02% (Yahoo closes). Emerging-market equities (EEM) fell 5.13%. The ECB’s reference rate put the euro at $1.0990.

The United States

A bipartisan bill needs 60 Senate votes, and both sides are weighing a stopgap into early 2027, when the new Congress elected on November 3 takes office. Science

A slow cosmic metronome. A team from ICRAR and Curtin University reports in Nature Astronomy that a radio source pulsing every eight minutes is a white dwarf orbiting a Sun-like star more than 5,000 light-years away. Astronomers have found a growing number of these “long-period radio transients” and have argued over whether they come from white dwarfs or from slow neutron stars. This is a peer-reviewed identification for one of them. It does not settle the question for the whole class.

Feeding the Milky Way’s black hole. Max Planck researchers link compact gas clouds near Sagittarius A* to winds from two massive nearby stars that may help supply it (ScienceDaily summary). It is a proposed mechanism from modelling and observations, not a measurement of the flow.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

A second daily train to New York is now running

Amtrak’s second daily Pennsylvanian round trip between Pittsburgh and New York, via Harrisburg and Philadelphia, began on Wednesday, the first added passenger service out of Pittsburgh in decades (PennDOT and the governor’s office; WPXI, Railway Age). Eastbound trains leave Pittsburgh at 7:10 AM and 1:00 PM, as reported. The extra train is funded by the state. Meanwhile, the city’s 2027 budget, with no tax rise and a draw of about $27–28 million from reserves, is before council, which must adopt it by December 31.
