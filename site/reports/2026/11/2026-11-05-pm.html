Top Stories

The AI selloff deepens into the close: S&P 500 −4.4%, Nasdaq −5.5%, chip index −12%

The afternoon brought no rebound. The S&P 500 had its worst day in 19 months, the Nasdaq closed in a correction, and the selling spread from chips to every sector.

At 2:20 PM the S&P 500 was down 3.9%. By the close it had fallen 4.36%, to 7,353.73. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% to 24,315.00, and the Dow fell 3.44%, or 1,833 points, to 51,523.06. That more than erased Wednesday’s 1,569-point post-election gain. The Russell 2000 lost 4.45%. CNBC and the AP said all three main averages had their worst day since April 2025. The S&P 500 is now 6.3% below its 52-week high and 4.4% below its 50-day average, and the Nasdaq 100 fund (QQQ) is 10.4% below its 50-day average.

Chipmakers took the worst of it. The PHLX semiconductor index fell 12.25% to 9,011.14. NBC News said that was its worst day since March 16, 2020, and its second-worst on record. The day’s largest declines in chips and AI hardware:

  • Micron −17.5% to $656.39
  • AMD −17.3% to $404.40
  • Marvell −16.4%
  • Vertiv −14.0%
  • Arm −13.1%
  • Quanta −12.3%
  • Arista −10.8%
  • TSMC −10.8%
  • Broadcom −10.4% to $291.53, a new 52-week low
  • Nvidia −9.3% to $181.60, on 300 million shares, about three times its average

NBC also reported SanDisk and Western Digital down about 17–22%. Tesla fell 10.8%, SpaceX 9.0% and Palantir 9.7%. The big buyers of AI capacity fell less: Microsoft −3.6%, Meta −4.0%, Amazon −2.2%, Alphabet −2.2%, Apple −1.5%.

Unlike Wednesday’s split market, Thursday’s selling was general. All 11 sector funds fell. Technology fell 8.0%, industrials 4.1% and consumer discretionary 3.8%. Staples, utilities and communications each fell less than 1%. The equal-weighted S&P 500 fund fell 2.9%, against 4.4% for the cap-weighted fund, so the largest companies did most of the damage. Volume in the S&P 500 fund was nearly three times normal, and Costco (+1.8%) was one of the few large gainers.

The explanation in coverage from the AP, CNN, CNBC, Reuters and Fortune is that a Democratic Congress, together with Democratic governors such as Texas’s Gina Hinojosa, who campaigned on a moratorium on new data centres, will slow, tax or regulate the AI build-out. Sen. Bernie Sanders posted “National data center moratorium now,” and Rep. Yassamin Ansari called for a “pause on advanced AI development” (CNBC). That is a market narrative: no bill was introduced and no company cut its spending plans. Qualcomm’s and Arm’s outlooks are a documented likely contributor for those two stocks. Benzinga also cites a report that China delayed approval of Nvidia H200 imports. Only one outlet reported that, and we could not confirm it.

Why it matters

This morning we asked whether the selloff was crowded positions unwinding or investors repricing AI growth. The close leaned toward a forced unwind. Selling accelerated late, volume was about three times normal, the short end of the VIX curve stayed sharply inverted, and the stocks hit hardest were the most crowded (memory, custom chips, networking) rather than those most exposed to Washington. Credit is the check. The high-yield bond fund (HYG) fell only 0.3%, and investment-grade bonds rose. A crash that stays in stocks is a repricing, not a funding problem.

What’s next

The jobs report at 8:30 AM, and whether chip stocks bounce once volatility eases. Nvidia reports later this month; analysts cited by Yahoo expect guidance of about $108 billion. Our call: the S&P 500 closes Friday above Thursday’s 7,353.73 (45–60%). Large one-day drops are often followed by a partial bounce, but a weak payroll number or a further rise in the VIX could extend the fall. Separately, we put the VIX closing Friday above 22 at 65–80%: it would take an unusually calm day to undo most of today’s jump.

Go deeper

Three things argue that this is a positioning flush and not yet a verdict on AI spending. The buyers of AI capacity fell far less than the sellers: Microsoft fell 3.6% while the memory makers that sell to it fell 17%. AppLovin, an AI-driven company that beat estimates, rose 31%, so investors were still paying for results. And the companies that might actually suffer from a data-centre moratorium, the power producers, fell less today (Vistra −6.2%, Constellation −2.5%) than the chip designers, whose link to US siting rules is indirect.

The case for a deeper repricing: a two-day fall of about 21% in the VanEck semiconductor fund (SMH) puts it 21% below its 50-day average and 14% below its 200-day average. That kind of damage usually needs more than a week to repair. Political risk to the build-out is real, if unquantified: Congress can now hold hearings and a Democratic Senate can block energy-permitting reform. Lam Research approved a new $25 billion share buyback after the close (company filing), the kind of support companies offer when they think their shares are cheap.

Treasuries and gold rally as investors seek safety; the 2-year yield falls 12 basis points

Bonds did their job. The front end moved most, pulling expectations for a December rate increase lower, even as two Fed presidents restated their case for one.

The Treasury’s official par curve shows the 2-year yield at 4.46%, down 12 basis points, and the 10-year at 4.84%, down 6. The 30-year fell 3 points to 5.25%, so the curve steepened: the gap between the 2- and 10-year yields widened to 38 basis points from 32. The 20+ year Treasury fund rose only 0.2%, while the 7–10 year fund rose 0.3%. Gold futures were near $4,100, up 0.7%, after trading as high as about $4,131 (Kitco). The dollar index was little changed at 101.60, and the yen gained about 0.5%.

The Fed’s hawks did not move. Richmond Fed President Tom Barkin said inflation is the main risk and a gradual pace of increases “makes sense,” and Cleveland’s Beth Hammack said policy needs to become restrictive along a gradual path (FXStreet). The Fed’s target range is 3.75–4.00%. Estimates of the chance of a December increase now range from about two-thirds (FXStreet, citing futures) to about four-fifths in other reports. We cannot reconcile them, so we give the range.

Why it matters

A 12-point fall in the 2-year yield on a day without bad economic data means investors are betting that a stock slide this sharp will make the Fed more cautious. The Fed has not said so. Its hawks spoke today and did not soften. Tomorrow’s jobs number will test that bet. This morning’s call that the 10-year would close below 4.90% was right.

AppLovin jumps 31% after a beat; Qualcomm and Arm fall on outlooks

Wednesday night’s results drew opposite reactions: investors punished hardware squeezed by memory costs and rewarded AI software that grew 55%.

AppLovin closed up 31.0% at $356.10, its best day in two years by Kiplinger’s count, on 22 million shares against an average of about 4 million. Third-quarter revenue was $2.185 billion, up 55% and above the roughly $2.07 billion expected, and its fourth-quarter forecast of $2.37–2.40 billion was well above consensus. Qualcomm fell 9.5% to $156.75: its revenue outlook beat estimates, but its earnings forecast did not, and it cited weaker handset chips and memory costs. Arm fell 13.1% to $166.72 although adjusted earnings of $0.52 a share beat the $0.47 expected; its guidance was only slightly above consensus. Expedia fell about 13% on its own guidance, as reported.

Company results as reported by Kiplinger, CNBC, Seeking Alpha and StockStory (search summaries) · closes: Yahoo Finance.

Oil rebounds 3% as Iran reports more tanker fires and the US weighs strikes or a deal

Part of Wednesday’s 8% fall came back. Deal odds are being cut, and the strait is still unsafe.

Rigzone reported that WTI for December settled at $90.52, up 2.7%, and Brent for January at $98.29, up about 4%; only that one outlet gave settlement prices. On Yahoo Finance, front-month WTI was at $90.92 and Brent at $98.69 at 4:27 PM. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said several tankers caught fire, some after hitting mines. Their counts vary from two tankers and a tug to four tankers, and no maritime authority has confirmed the mine claims. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre has logged at least five incidents in the Gulf since Sunday. Al Jazeera, citing the New York Times, reports that the US military is ready to resume major operations if ordered. Qatar is mediating, and Gulf News reports Vice President Vance is urging a deal. A research note cited by one broker put the odds of a deal at 10%.

Why it matters

Oil remains a political bet, and today it moved against the deal. Wednesday’s call that WTI would settle below $92 today was right, narrowly. Our call: front-month WTI settles above $88 on Friday (65–80%), given today’s rebound and new tanker incidents.

Bank of England raises its rate to 4.00% in an 8–1 vote

This is the bank’s first increase since August 2023. One member wanted a half-point, and the bank expects inflation to near 5% by mid-2027 because of energy prices.

The Monetary Policy Committee raised Bank Rate by a quarter-point. Catherine Mann voted for 4.25%. Markets had expected a 7–2 vote. The bank sees inflation, now 3.5%, peaking near 5% by mid-2027 because of energy prices tied to the Middle East. The next decision is Dec. 17. Sterling was steady near $1.35, and the FTSE 100 fell 0.4%. Australia, Sweden and Britain have now each raised rates within one week, and the Fed is weighing the same choice in December.

Jobless claims hold near a half-century low the day before payrolls

Claims fell to 189,000, productivity rose 3.6% and labour costs fell. The real test is tomorrow’s jobs report.

Initial claims for the week to Oct. 31 fell to 189,000, against about 196,000 expected (FRED, EOD official). That matches July’s low, the lowest since 1969. Continuing claims rose to 1.718 million. Third-quarter productivity rose at a 3.6% rate, and unit labour costs fell 0.7%, as reported this morning. Challenger’s October job-cut count could not be found this evening. Economists expect Friday’s payrolls to show a gain of about 65,000 (FactSet and Reuters, as reported by Morningstar), after September’s 29,000, with unemployment steady at 4.2% and hourly pay up 0.3%.

Johnson steps back and Jeffries launches his bid for Speaker as Mississippi and Alaska stay uncalled

The new majority starts organising. Its first deadline comes much sooner than January: government funding runs out Dec. 11.

Speaker Mike Johnson won his own sixth term but told staff he will not lead House Republicans in the next Congress, NBC and ABC report. Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries sent colleagues a letter formally seeking the speakership, and no challenger has declared. Senate Democrats expect to hold leadership elections in December. Mississippi’s Senate race, where Democrat Scott Colom leads Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by a narrow and disputed margin, and Alaska’s, where Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan, remain uncalled. Counts of uncalled House races differ widely among trackers. Al Jazeera reports that President Trump acknowledged the Iran war hurt his party but said he had to fight it; we could not find his full remarks.

What Moved Markets

Overnight and the open. Selling started in Asia: South Korea’s Kospi fell 5.3% (Samsung −7.6%, SK Hynix −9.4%), Taiwan’s Taiex 3.2% and the Nikkei 1.8%, while Hong Kong’s Hang Seng rose 3.7% (AP). In Europe, ASML fell 6.6% and Infineon 8.5%. US stocks opened lower after Qualcomm’s and Arm’s results. Likely contributor: the two outlooks, which every report on the open cited.

Morning and midday. Chip stocks fell further as the day went on, and the selling spread to Tesla, SpaceX, software and banks. Market narrative: Democratic calls for limits on data centres, cited by CNN, CNBC and Fortune. Politicians spoke, but nothing was introduced.

The close. The S&P 500 went from −3.9% at 2:20 PM to −4.4% at the close, and the VIX touched its highest level since March. No circuit breaker was triggered. Unexplained: no news explains the late selling, which is consistent with forced selling and hedging into the close.

Rates and gold. Treasury yields fell all day and gold rose. Likely contributor: investors moving into safer assets, with Treasuries rallying as stocks fell. Today’s claims and productivity data did not argue for lower rates.

Oil. Likely contributor: the tanker fires reported by the IRGC.

Crypto. Bitcoin fell 5.6% to about $75,900 and ether 7.8%. The Grayscale Bitcoin Mini Trust fund fell 5.0%, Coinbase 9.7% and Robinhood 10.3%. Market narrative: general risk reduction; there was no crypto-specific news.

The Economy

Today’s data. Jobless claims fell to 189,000, productivity rose 3.6% and unit labour costs fell 0.7% (see Top Stories). By level, layoffs are very low. By rate of change, labour costs are slowing: up 2.4% from a year earlier, below core PCE inflation of 3.1%.

The Fed. Barkin and Hammack both favour gradual increases. The target is 3.75–4.00%, and the next meeting is Dec. 8–9. The 2-year Treasury yield is now 29 basis points above the top of the target range, down from 41 a day ago. That narrowing reflects fewer expected increases, not a forecast of cuts. September’s headline CPI rose 0.65% for the month (FRED, index level); October’s CPI comes Tuesday, Nov. 10.

The Treasury. Thursday’s announcement sets next week’s bills: $100 billion of 6-week, $95 billion of 13-week and $82 billion of 26-week bills, all settling Nov. 12 (TreasuryDirect). The refunding auctions follow: $58 billion of 3-year notes, $42 billion of 10-year notes and $25 billion of 30-year bonds, settling Nov. 16. With yields lower after today’s rally, those auctions will show whether buyers still want duration. Results of today’s 4- and 8-week bill auctions were not retrieved.

Credit. The high-yield spread was 3.32% on Wednesday (FRED, ICE BofA), its tightest in a week, and the high-yield bond fund (HYG) fell only 0.3% today. Credit has not joined the equity selloff.

Global. Bank of England +25 basis points to 4.00% (see Top Stories). We found no report of a Norges Bank decision today.

The United States

The lame-duck calendar. Government funding expires Dec. 11 under the stopgap that passed the House 370–48 and the Senate 90–6 in September. The lame-duck agenda also includes full-year spending bills or another stopgap, the defense authorisation, a farm bill, a water-resources bill and permitting reform, with about three weeks of legislative days (Holland & Knight). We found no White House statement today on the deadline.

The Supreme Court. The court issued no merits ruling today. On Tuesday the justices heard St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Measles. The CDC counted 4,852 confirmed cases in 45 states and DC as of Oct. 29, more than double last year’s total. A child in Lewis County, New York, died this week, the state’s first measles death in 30 years (CNN, ABC).

The World

Iran. Tehran warned that countries helping US attacks are “legitimate targets” (Al Jazeera). Its foreign minister says talks through Qatar are “very intensive” and are based on Iran’s seven-point plan; there is no agreement. Market read-through: oil (see Top Stories).

Ukraine. US, Ukrainian and Russian negotiators are expected in Abu Dhabi on Nov. 10–11, though one earlier report gave Nov. 6–10. The Kremlin’s Yuri Ushakov said President Putin agreed “in principle.” Russia still demands all of the Donbas region, which Kyiv rejects. The EU paid Ukraine €4.36 billion under its support loan (Kyiv Independent).

Taiwan and chips. A Taiwanese legislative committee is weighing tighter export controls on chip materials and equipment bound for mainland China, with longer prison terms (Caixin, one source). Taiwan’s defense minister expects US congressional support for arms sales to continue after the midterms (Taipei Times). Taiwan’s market fell 3.2% today.

Europe. The European Parliament voted to loosen the 2035 car-emissions target from a 100% cut to 87.5%, according to Euronews. That report is single-sourced and does not say which stage of the process the vote was. Carmakers are the read-through.

AI & Technology

Politics reaches AI hardware. The day’s biggest AI story was the selloff (see Top Stories). The policy behind the fear is still talk. Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. That proposal predates the election, and its prospects with a president who can veto it are unclear. Texas’s governor-elect campaigned on a moratorium. Exposure (not direction): NVDA, AMD, MU, AVGO, VRT, PWR, VST, CEG, EQIX, DLR.

Nvidia and China. The Financial Times reported this week that Nvidia is requiring its data-centre partners to sell at least 51% of their services to customers outside China. Separately, Reuters reported that TSMC could face a US penalty of more than $1 billion after one of its chips turned up in a Huawei processor. Both reports are single-sourced.

Models. We could not confirm a major model release today. Release trackers list several, but none was confirmed by the companies.

Science

Ancient DNA and the Celtic languages. A study in Nature (Nov. 4) analysed genomes from 4,587 ancient people to test how Celtic languages spread across Bronze and Iron Age Europe. We have not read the paper, only its listing.

A robot lab. Nature’s News & Views describes an AI system that coordinates laboratory instruments to run multi-step molecular-biology experiments with little human help. It is a step toward automated experiments, not a replacement for scientists.

Mpox. A study in this week’s Science identifies the parts of mpox surface proteins that protective antibodies target, which could guide vaccine design. These are early, single-sourced listings.

Space

The company also unveiled a third stage, GS3, to place satellites directly into geostationary orbit, and a new engine (Space.com).
