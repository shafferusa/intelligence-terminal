1 · Refresher: the output gaps and stagflation

Nothing new is confirmed since Lesson 1, so this recaps the Module 3 ideas tonight’s Module 4 rests on. Lesson 1 and Lesson 2 teach them in full.

Potential is the anchor. Long-run aggregate supply sits at potential GDP: what the economy can produce with its labor, capital and technology when they are fully used. A business cycle is output swinging around that line (p.102).

Below potential: a recessionary gap. Spending falls short, firms cut output, unemployment rises and prices soften (p.103). Above potential: an inflationary gap. Labor and materials get scarce, wages and prices climb (p.107). Stagflation is the supply-side case: costs rise, so output falls while prices rise (p.108). Module 4 looks inside these swings, sector by sector.

Clearing up

Read the economy’s chart like any market chart: the price level up the side, real GDP along the bottom. The vertical LRAS line marks potential. Find where AD and SRAS cross: left of the line is a recessionary gap, right of it an inflationary gap. A demand shift moves output and prices the same way; a supply shift moves them in opposite directions.

Memorize
  • Recessionary gap: output below potential · inflationary gap: above · stagflation: output down, prices up

2 · Today: how the economy’s parts move through a cycle

Module 4 asks what actually rises and falls when the economy swings: which spending moves most, why jobs react late, and what inflation is, how it is measured and what causes it.

Households. Spending depends on income, job prospects and confidence. Durable goods such as cars and appliances swing most, because they last and the purchase is easy to postpone; services swing less, everyday non-durables least (pp.138–140). Baseline spending follows permanent income, what households judge they can sustain, while durables follow current disposable income. A rising saving rate signals caution now but stores spending power for the recovery (p.140).

Housing is a small sector that swings hard, because buyers borrow. Lower mortgage rates cut monthly payments, so sales and construction rise. Late in an expansion prices and rates outrun incomes, unsold homes pile up and building falls. Over the long run, new household formation sets the trend (pp.141–142).

Trade. Imports follow growth at home; exports follow growth abroad. A stronger home currency makes imports cheaper and exports dearer to foreigners, so imports rise and exports fall. Only lasting currency moves matter, and growth differences act faster (pp.142–144).

Unemployment lags. Firms hesitate to lay off, and in a recovery they first add overtime and temporary staff before hiring. Hours worked, temporary hiring and productivity turn earlier (pp.147–148). The unemployment rate counts only the labor force: people working or actively looking. A discouraged worker who stops looking leaves the labor force, so the rate can fall in a weak economy, and rise early in a recovery when those people start looking again (p.146).

Inflation is a sustained rise in the overall price level. Disinflation means inflation slowing while prices still rise; deflation means prices falling. Deflation hurts because debts are fixed in money: revenue shrinks while the debt does not, so firms cut spending and jobs. That is why central banks aim for low positive inflation, around 2%. Hyperinflation usually follows government spending paid for by printing money (p.150).

Measuring it. A Laspeyres index prices a fixed basket bought in a base period at today’s prices. It overstates inflation: shoppers switch to goods that got relatively cheaper, quality improves, and new products are missing from the old basket (pp.151–154). Headline inflation covers all items; core leaves out food and energy, which are noisy, not biased.

Everyday picture

Last year’s grocery list, priced at this year’s shelf prices, tells you how much more that same list costs. But you no longer buy exactly that list: when beef got dear you bought more chicken. The old list exaggerates what your groceries actually cost you.

Explaining it. Cost-push inflation starts with costs, mainly wages, forcing prices up; demand-pull starts with spending pulling prices up and wages following. Both come from demand pressing against capacity. For cost-push, watch unemployment against NAIRU (the rate below which wages start to accelerate) and unit labor cost; for demand-pull, watch output against potential and capacity use. Once people expect inflation, it can persist after its cause is gone (pp.155–159).

Laspeyres = Σ(q0 × pt) / Σ(q0 × p0) × 100 · inflation = Indext / Indext−1 − 1

q0
base-period quantity of each good, units (p.151)
p0, pt
price of each good in the base and current period, currency per unit
Unemployment rate
unemployed ÷ labor force; participation rate = labor force ÷ working-age population (p.146)
Unit labor cost
pay per hour ÷ output per hour, currency per unit of output; rises when pay outpaces productivity (p.157)
Assumptions and exceptions
  • Disinflation is not deflation: prices are still rising, only more slowly.
  • Underemployed people count as employed; discouraged workers are outside the labor force.
  • Real GDP is deflated with the GDP deflator, not the CPI (p.155).
  • Paasche (today’s basket) and Fisher (the geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche) appear only in the practice problems; with substitution, Laspeyres sits above Paasche and Fisher between them.
Memorize
  • Most cyclical: durables · then services · least: non-durables
  • Unemployment lags; hours, temps and productivity lead it
  • Laspeyres overstates inflation: substitution, quality, new products
  • Cost-push gauges: NAIRU, wages, unit labor cost · demand-pull gauges: output gap, capacity use
