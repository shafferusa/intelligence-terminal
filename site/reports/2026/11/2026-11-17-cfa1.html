1 · Refresher: reading a demand and supply chart

The module builds on one picture. Its introduction takes basic demand, supply and market equilibrium as known (p.3), and its learning outcomes then measure how strongly buyers respond. So the chart itself is the foundation.

Two curves, one crossing. Price runs up the vertical axis, quantity along the horizontal. Demand slopes down: at a lower price, buyers want more. Supply slopes up: at a higher price, sellers offer more, because more of their output now covers its cost. Where the curves cross, the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer: the equilibrium price and quantity. Below that price buyers want more than is offered (excess demand), and competition for the goods pushes the price up; above it, unsold goods push it down.

Clearing up

Every chart question starts with one test: did the good’s own price change? Then you slide along a curve that stays put. Did anything else change, such as income, another good’s price, or input costs? Then a whole curve moves: right means more at every price, left means less. Next, find the new crossing. Demand moving right raises both price and quantity. Supply moving right lowers price and raises quantity. Draw the old curve, draw the new one beside it, and read where they cross; never move a curve because its own price changed.

Memorize
  • Own-price change: movement along the curve · anything else: the curve shifts
  • Demand right: price up, quantity up · supply right: price down, quantity up
  • Price below equilibrium: excess demand · above: excess supply

2 · Today: what shifts the economy’s curves, and what happens next

Lesson 1 set out GDP, the three curves and growth accounting. Tonight’s core is the same chart logic as Part 1, applied to the whole economy: price level on the vertical axis, real GDP on the horizontal. A change in the price level moves along a curve; everything else shifts one (pp.83–101).

What moves aggregate demand. AD is total planned spending, so anything that changes spending at a given price level shifts it. Rising household wealth, from stocks or homes, lifts consumption: right. Confident businesses and firms running near capacity invest more: right. More government purchases or lower taxes: right. An easier money supply lowers interest rates: right. Faster growth abroad buys more exports: right. A stronger home currency makes exports dearer and imports cheaper: left (Exhibit 14, p.94).

What moves short-run supply. SRAS reflects firms’ costs. Higher nominal wages or dearer inputs such as oil squeeze margins, so firms offer less at each price level: left. A stronger currency cuts the cost of imported inputs: right. Wages matter net of productivity, which is what unit labor cost captures (p.96).

What moves long-run supply. LRAS is potential GDP, so only capacity moves it: more labor, more capital, better skills, more natural resources, better technology. Any LRAS shift moves SRAS with it; a wage rise alone does not touch LRAS (Exhibit 16, pp.99–100).

Four equilibria. In long-run equilibrium AD, SRAS and LRAS meet at potential (p.102). If AD falls, output drops below potential and prices soften: a recessionary gap, with rising unemployment. If AD overshoots, output exceeds potential and labor gets scarce; wages rise, SRAS moves left, and output returns to potential at a higher price level: the inflationary gap closes itself, through inflation. If SRAS moves left, output falls while prices rise: stagflation. Here policy has a hard choice: stimulating AD restores output but locks in higher prices; waiting means slow wage declines and lost output (pp.102–109).

When both curves move, one result is certain and the other depends on size. AD and SRAS both right: output rises, the price level could go either way. AD right and SRAS left: prices rise, output could go either way (pp.100–110).

Assumptions and exceptions
  • SRAS slopes up only because wages and input prices lag; once they adjust, output returns to potential.
  • A stronger currency shifts AD left but SRAS right: name both.
  • More money raises output only for a while; in the long run it raises only prices (p.93).
  • Stagflation is a supply problem; demand shifts make gaps, not stagflation.
Memorize
  • AD shifters: wealth, expectations, capacity use, fiscal policy, money, foreign growth, the exchange rate
  • Demand shift: output and prices move together · supply shift: opposite
  • Recessionary gap: below potential · inflationary gap: above · stagflation: output down, prices up
  • Wages shift SRAS, never LRAS; an LRAS shift moves SRAS too
