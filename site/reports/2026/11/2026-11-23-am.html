Top Stories

Futures point to a sharp rebound after the S&P 500’s 8.6% weekly fall

The bounce is led by the same chip and AI stocks that led the slide. It comes in a holiday-shortened week with the inflation data that matters most to the Fed due Wednesday.

S&P 500 futures were reported up 2.6% (Yahoo Finance), 2.79% (Seeking Alpha) and 2.9% (CNN) in the early hours, after AP had them up 2.1% earlier in the night. Nasdaq-100 futures were up 3% to 3.8%, and Dow futures by about 960 to 1,020 points. We could not take our own futures quotes this morning, so we give the range. Futures are not a guaranteed open, and last week the market reversed sharply by midday more than once.

The rebound follows the worst week for U.S. stocks since 2020. The S&P 500 fell 8.6% to 6,910.39, seven straight losses, and closed Friday in what outlets described as a correction. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% on Friday alone to 22,617.73. The Dow lost 1.93% to 48,147.33 and the Russell 2000 2.76% to 2,581.79 (Yahoo Finance closes). The semiconductor fund SMH fell 10.6% on Friday, and Nvidia, AMD, Micron and Broadcom each lost 12–14%.

Overnight, markets abroad were mixed rather than relieved. Japan was closed for a holiday. Korea’s Kospi fell another 3.31% to 4,708.54, its lowest close since January, triggering a sell-side “sidecar” curb on program trading (Korea JoongAng Daily, TradingKey). Taiwan’s Taiex rebounded about 2% by early afternoon after its worst week since 1991. Europe’s Stoxx 600 was up 0.5–0.7% in the morning (RTTNews, MarketScreener). Jonas Goltermann of Capital Economics called the U.S. bounce largely “mechanical,” meaning positioning rather than news (one report).

Why it matters A rally of this size after seven down days is common. What separates a bottom from a pause is breadth: on Friday 4,777 liquid U.S. stocks fell and 1,061 rose, and only 21.2% are above their 50-day average. A rebound that is broad, with small caps and the equal-weight S&P joining in, would say much more than one led only by Nvidia.
What’s next The 1 PM two-year auction, Williams at 4 PM, Dell and Workday on Tuesday evening, and Wednesday’s PCE inflation and revised GDP, all before Thursday’s holiday close.

Go deeper

The pattern last week was concentrated selling in the market’s largest, most AI-exposed companies, then a spread to everything on Friday, when gold, silver and bitcoin fell with stocks. Treasuries were the only haven that worked: the 2-year yield fell 13 basis points on Friday to 4.63%. This morning that is reversing, with yields up and gold up, which is what a relief move looks like when forced selling stops.

What would confirm a turn: the Russell 2000 and RSP gaining about as much as the Nasdaq, the VIX closing below 30, and high-yield bond funds (HYG closed Friday at $76.33, a 52-week low) stabilising. What would undo it: a fade through the afternoon, as on Thursday, when the S&P went from up 1.3% to down 2%.

Trump urges Nvidia’s board to bring back Jensen Huang; Nvidia is up about 8% before the open

The New York Times reports that the woman at the centre of Huang’s resignation was in contact with a Chinese state-security officer. Treasury Secretary Bessent says the administration still “needs answers.”

On Sunday night President Trump wrote on Truth Social that reinstating Huang “would be the best thing for the Company, and also, for the U.S.A.” (Forbes, Jerusalem Post, Stocktwits). Forbes called it his first direct public appeal to the board; on Friday he had said Huang deserved a “second chance.” Nvidia had not responded by early Monday. Huang resigned as chief executive on November 12 after reports of a relationship with a subordinate. Interim chief executive Colette Kress is running the company, a search firm is looking for a successor, and chair Harvey Jones has said the board does not intend to reinstate him.

The New York Times reported on Sunday that Alice Huang, of the chip buyer Megaspeed, was in contact with an officer of China’s Ministry of State Security from spring 2025 to this autumn, a period that overlaps her relationship with Huang and Megaspeed’s large purchases of Nvidia chips. AFP and ABC News carried similar accounts. All rest on anonymous sources, and it is not known whether any information was passed. Speaking on CBS’s Face the Nation, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the administration still “needs answers” on how Nvidia chips reached China and how high in the company knowledge went, but called the Alice Huang angle “kind of a canard.” His earlier ten-day request for answers runs out around Wednesday. One outlet reports Huang is due to be questioned by members of Congress on December 1; we could not confirm that.

Separately, the Wall Street Journal and Reuters reported last week that the SEC has opened an inquiry into whether investors were misled; the New York Times says it is at an early stage and could end without charges. The SEC has not commented. California’s attorney general served Nvidia with an investigative subpoena on November 19.

Why it matters Nvidia lost 26% last week, and as one of the index’s largest companies it moves the whole S&P 500. Its premarket gain, about 8.5% at 4:08 AM by CNBC’s count, would put it near $136, still far below its 52-week high of $243.37. The stock is now trading on governance and legal headlines, not results: its third-quarter revenue was a record $115.8 billion.

CrowdStrike agrees to buy Zscaler for about $44 billion in stock

It would be one of the largest cybersecurity deals on record. Zscaler holders get a 34% premium; CrowdStrike’s shares are falling.

CrowdStrike will pay 1.135 of its shares for each Zscaler share, worth about $260.41 at Friday’s prices and 34% above Zscaler’s close of $194.82, valuing the deal at about $43.9 billion including debt (CrowdStrike’s filing and announcement as reported by TheNextWeb, TipRanks/TheFly and GuruFocus). CrowdStrike shareholders would own 84.1% of the combined company. Both boards approved it unanimously, and Zscaler founder Jay Chaudhry would join CrowdStrike’s board. The deal needs shareholder and antitrust approval. Zscaler is up about 13–14% before the open, below the offer value, and CrowdStrike is down 5–7.5%.

The gap between Zscaler’s price and the offer is the market’s judgment on two things: CrowdStrike’s own share price, since the deal is in stock, and the antitrust risk of combining two of the largest security-software companies. Elsewhere, ABB agreed to buy Germany’s Ziehl-Abegg, a maker of fans and motors used in data-centre cooling, for about $2.65 billion (one report).

Treasury yields rise as investors leave havens; $69 billion of two-year notes go on sale at 1 PM

Friday’s rally took the 2-year yield down 13 basis points. This week’s $183 billion of note supply will test how much of that holds.

Early Monday the 2-year yield was up about 7 basis points to 4.689%, the 10-year up about 4 to 4.959% and the 30-year up 3 to 5.335% (CNBC). On Friday’s official curve the 2-year was 4.63%, the 10-year 4.92% and the 30-year 5.31%, after falls of 13, 12 and 9 basis points (Treasury). Over the week the 2-year fell 25 basis points. The 10-year breakeven inflation rate was 2.15% on Friday (FRED): most of last week’s fall in yields came from lower inflation expectations, not from bets that the Fed will cut.

The Treasury sells $69 billion of 2-year notes today, $70 billion of 5-year notes and a $28 billion 2-year floating-rate note on Tuesday, and $44 billion of 7-year notes on Wednesday, all settling November 30. The last 2-year auction cleared at 4.708% (one report), so today’s sale may come at a lower yield. A weak result would show up as a “tail,” the auction yield above the market yield just before the sale.

At least 15 killed in weekend Russian strikes on the Kyiv region

Most died when a five-storey apartment block in Dymer partly collapsed. Zelenskyy asked Washington to release Patriot interceptors that allies have already paid for.

Ukrainian officials say at least 15 people were killed across the Kyiv region, including a teenager, most in Dymer, about 45 km north of Kyiv, where a drone strike brought down part of an apartment block (Kyiv Post, eNCA). Two more people were killed and eight wounded when a supermarket in Vyshneve was hit. The toll has risen each day, from 6 on Sunday morning to 15, and may rise further. Moscow did not immediately comment. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy promised “fully justified responses” and urged the U.S. to approve Patriot deliveries financed by allies through NATO’s purchasing scheme. Ukraine says Russia fired about 2,700 attack drones, 1,460 glide bombs and 165 missiles in the past week; those are Ukraine’s figures.

On Saturday a drone attack set the Russian cargo ship Sparta II, which Finnish broadcaster Yle says is owned by a company run by Russia’s defence ministry, on fire in international waters in the Gulf of Finland, Finland’s defence ministry said. The crew was evacuated and the ship was being towed toward Russian waters on Sunday. Finnish and Russian officials both suggested the drones were Ukrainian; Kyiv had not commented. Diplomatically, the week ended badly: after U.S. envoy Jared Kushner said in Berlin that Russia would accept Article 5-style guarantees for Ukraine, Russia’s deputy foreign minister Sergei Ryabkov publicly rejected NATO-style guarantees on Friday (Al Jazeera).

Iran attacks U.S. positions in Bahrain as shipping attacks around Hormuz increase

Iran’s foreign minister says the strait stays closed to normal traffic until Washington meets its terms. Oil is little changed near $92 for Brent.

Bahrain says an Iranian attack on Saturday hit a civilian industrial site, causing a fire but no casualties; Iran’s Revolutionary Guards say they targeted U.S. Patriot sites and the Riffa and Awali bases (ABC News, Gulf News). The UK’s maritime security agency, UKMTO, reported two tankers hit by projectiles while leaving through Hormuz early Saturday and another incident “involving tanker and military forces” in the Gulf of Oman on Sunday. Iran says one tanker hit a mine; a claimed U.S. strike on a tanker is unconfirmed by either government. Gulf News reports more than 14 tankers gathering off Kharg Island to try to run the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports, now in its 130th day, and President Trump has threatened to sink vessels that try.

In Muscat, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said reopening Hormuz is “not difficult” if the U.S. accepts Iran’s “reasonable, lawful” conditions, reported to include sanctions relief and unfrozen assets, and called mediation through Qatar and Pakistan “extremely intensive” (Middle East Monitor, PressTV). Trump said Iran wants a deal but its terms are unacceptable.

Why it matters Brent was about $92 early Monday, little changed, and down about 20% in November despite the blockade. The oil market is treating the Gulf as a contained disruption. A direct clash at sea over the tankers off Kharg would test that.

Trump orders federal contractors to clear answers to Congress with the White House

Firms that do not comply could lose contracts. Democrats call the order unlawful; funding talks remain stuck three weeks before the December 11 deadline.

An executive order signed Friday requires federal contractors and subcontractors to coordinate with the White House before responding to congressional requests for information, and to follow its instructions on withholding material claimed as executive privilege, on pain of losing or being suspended from contracts (ABC News, CNN, Bloomberg). It follows a memo from White House Counsel Will Scharf requiring agencies to approve employee testimony. Rep. Jamie Raskin called it “unprecedented and blatantly unlawful.” Supporters say it protects executive privilege; no court challenge has been reported yet.

On spending, Senate Majority Leader John Thune says there have been “some good conversations” but “no deals yet” on extending funding past December 11. Sen. Tim Kaine says he will not back a stopgap that does not limit funding for the Iran war, and Republican Sen. Ron Johnson has called the war “illegal” (NPR, Fox News). Republicans are weighing a party-line bill for the White House’s $350 billion defence request. Congress returns November 30.

Science

A billion missing years. Researchers at the University of Wollongong propose in Nature that slow rising and sinking of continents, driven by hot mantle rock, wore away the rocks that would have recorded about a billion years of Earth’s history, the gap geologists call the Great Unconformity (ScienceDaily). It is a model of a long-debated puzzle, not a final answer.

Brains and AI models. A York University study in Nature Machine Intelligence finds that AI models which predict neurons’ activity well do not necessarily work like the brain: predicting in the other direction, from neurons to the models’ internal features, worked much worse (ScienceDaily, single report). It is a caution against treating predictive accuracy as evidence of a shared mechanism.

Fragile deep-sea populations. Genetic data from 486 specimens of five deep-sea species in the Gulf of Mexico showed effective population sizes below 500 for all of them, under a common threshold for long-term genetic health (Nature Communications).

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

PRT approves the first full redesign of its bus network since 1964

Pittsburgh Regional Transit’s board voted 10–1 on Friday to approve its Bus Line Refresh, which takes effect in August 2027 (WESA, PublicSource, WTAE, KDKA). Outlets count the route changes differently: WESA says the network goes from 95 routes to 87, and PublicSource says 20 routes are cut and 14 added. PRT projects 7–11% more riders, about 2 million trips a year, at the same operating cost, and says 99.4% of current riders will still be within a quarter mile of a stop. Board members warned the plan depends on multi-year state transit funding. We did not find a list of which South Hills routes change; PRT’s route maps are the place to check.

At the county, the full council must pass Executive Sara Innamorato’s 2027 budget, which has no tax increase, by December 6, after a committee added money for prosecutors and public defenders last week (about $1.37 million by WESA’s count; TribLive reports a larger figure). In the city, Mayor Corey O’Connor’s $872 million plan draws $28 million from reserves and cuts the city’s $10 million yearly payment to the Housing Opportunity Fund, which drew crowds to council hearings; council’s budget chair has ruled out a tax increase.
