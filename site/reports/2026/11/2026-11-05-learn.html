Jamaica’s Reefs Flipped From Coral to Seaweed in a Decade. Getting Them Back Has Taken Far Longer.

Thursday, November 5, 2026 · 14 min read

Some systems rest in either of two stable conditions separated by a threshold, so a push across it flips the system on its own — and because a different mechanism now holds it there, simply undoing the push does not flip it back.

Systems Thinking

Day 28 of 293 · Systems Thinking 4 of 12 · Thresholds, tipping points and hysteresis

The urchins that vanished from the Caribbean

In August 1980, Hurricane Allen tracked close along Jamaica’s north coast and tore apart reefs that had rarely faced that kind of wave energy before, flattening much of the staghorn coral that had built the shallow fore reef. A reef can usually absorb a hurricane and rebuild. This one had less margin than it looked: decades of overfishing had already stripped Jamaica’s north coast of most of the fish that graze algae off bare rock, and one grazer was doing most of that work alone — the long-spined black sea urchin, Diadema antillarum, clearing space fast enough that young coral could still find somewhere to settle.

Then, in early 1983, an unidentified waterborne pathogen swept the Caribbean basin. Within about a year it had killed more than 90 percent of the Diadema it found at most sites it reached; researchers who tracked the die-off on Panama’s reefs recorded urchin counts falling by over 90 percent in permanent survey plots. On Jamaica’s north coast, the pathogen removed the grazer that had been standing between the reef and the seaweed waiting to take it.

The marine ecologist Terry Hughes had already been running long-term surveys of those reefs. His results, published in Science in 1994, show coral cover that had held at roughly half the reef through the 1970s falling to under 5 percent by the time he wrote the paper. Fleshy seaweed, which had covered only a few percent of the reef before 1980, came to dominate more than 90 percent of it.

Reefs had weathered hurricanes before, and taken disease losses before, without this result. Why did a 1980 storm and a 1983 pathogen, landing three years apart on an already overfished reef, tip it somewhere neither shock alone had managed — and why, decades later, with the storm long past and some Diadema back, has the reef still not returned to coral at anything like its old extent?

The Big Idea

Today’s concept: some systems have two (or more) stable conditions they can rest in, for the same outside conditions, rather than just one — and they stay in whichever one they are already in until something pushes them hard enough, past a specific threshold, to flip into the other. Once flipped, reversing the original push does not flip the system back, because a different mechanism now holds it in its new condition than held it in the old one.

Picture the system not as a single point that always settles back to the same place, but as a landscape with two valleys, each a stable resting spot, separated by a ridge. A small disturbance just rolls the system back down into whichever valley it is already sitting in — that is ordinary stability, the kind a ridge-free system already has from a plain balancing loop. A disturbance big enough to push the system up and over the ridge, though, sends it rolling down into the other valley instead, where a different set of forces now holds it in place. Coral-dominated and algae-dominated are two such valleys for a reef. The ridge between them is the threshold, and crossing it is what the rest of this lesson explains.

How It Works

A system has genuine alternative stable states when, under the very same external conditions — the same water temperature, the same nutrient level, the same storm history — more than one self-sustaining configuration is possible, and which one you actually find depends on the system’s own recent history rather than on the conditions alone. That is a stronger claim than “this system changed when conditions changed”: it says the system can sit in either state with conditions held fixed, because each state defends itself once established.

A reinforcing loop, of the kind Day 26 named, is usually what does the defending. On a healthy reef, grazing fish and urchins crop algae off open rock fast enough that coral larvae keep finding bare surface to settle on; live coral shelters more grazing fish and invertebrates, which keeps grazing pressure high, which keeps rock open for more coral. That loop reinforces coral dominance. Push grazing pressure down far enough, though, and algae gets ahead of it: unchecked growth pre-empts the open rock coral larvae need, shades out the coral already there, and the loss of coral removes the structure that sheltered the fish that were doing the grazing — which drives grazing down further, which lets algae spread further still. The same kind of loop, reinforcing the opposite state. A threshold is the specific point, somewhere between those two loops, where the balance of forces flips sign — below it, grazing can outpace algal growth and coral holds its ground; above it, algal growth outpaces grazing and the system is drawn toward the algae-dominated valley on its own, with no further push required.

The ecologist Marten Scheffer and co-authors formalized the landscape picture in a widely cited 2001 Nature review: each valley is a basin of attraction, and resilience, in this technical sense, is not how healthy a system currently looks but how large a disturbance it can absorb without being pushed over the ridge into a different basin. That distinction matters because a system can lose most of its resilience — its basin can narrow and its ridge can lower — from a slow, easy-to-miss accumulation of stress, long before any single visible event reveals the change. A reef can still look like a healthy coral reef while decades of overfishing have quietly narrowed its basin, so that a disturbance it would once have shrugged off is now enough to tip it.

Hysteresis is the asymmetry that follows. Because a different mechanism holds the new state than held the old one, and that new mechanism can be stronger, simply removing the disturbance that caused the flip is not enough to reverse it. The storm passes; the pathogen runs its course; conditions return to roughly what they were before — and the system can still remain on the far side of the ridge, because algae actively defending its own dominance is a different, and sometimes larger, obstacle than the original loss of grazing ever was. Undoing the flip typically needs a much larger push the other way, sustained for much longer, than the push that caused it. Researchers who tracked a partial, localized recovery of Diadema on a Caribbean reef (Peter Edmunds and Robert Carpenter, writing in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2001) did find less algae and more juvenile coral where urchins came back in numbers — evidence that the loop can run in reverse. But the recovery they documented was local and partial, not the reef snapping back the moment the original pressure eased, which is exactly what hysteresis predicts rather than what it would predict if the system simply had one equilibrium all along.

Proving that a real ecosystem actually has two distinct stable states — rather than just sliding back and forth as conditions themselves slide back and forth — is genuinely hard, and ecologists disagree about how often it is true on real reefs. Peter Mumby, Alan Hastings and Nicholas Edwards, modeling Caribbean reefs after the 1983 die-off in a 2007 Nature paper, found that reefs did become susceptible to a true alternative stable state once most grazing fell onto parrotfish alone, with sharp thresholds of grazing and coral cover beyond which a reef loses the ability to recover. John Bruno and co-authors, surveying thousands of reefs worldwide for a 2009 paper in Ecology, found coral-to-macroalgae dominance considerably less common and less geographically widespread than the field had assumed — most degraded reefs they examined had simply lost coral, without seaweed rising to take its place at anything like the levels Jamaica showed. Both findings can be true at once: alternative stable states are real somewhere, Jamaica very likely among them, without being the universal fate of every stressed reef.

Back to the story

Hurricane Allen and the 1983 Diadema pathogen landed, three years apart, on a reef whose resilience had already been narrowed by decades of overfishing that had removed most of its other grazers. Either shock alone might have been absorbed. Together, they likely dropped grazing pressure below the threshold needed to keep pace with algal growth, pushing the reef over the ridge into the algae-dominated basin Hughes measured by the early 1990s. That the storm damage healed and some Diadema have partly returned, without the reef swinging back to anything near its old coral cover, is hysteresis doing exactly what the idea predicts: the push that flipped the system and the push it would take to flip it back are not mirror images of each other.

Alternative stable states
Two or more self-sustaining configurations a system can rest in under the same external conditions, with which one it occupies depending on its history rather than on current conditions alone.
Threshold (tipping point)
The specific point at which the balance of forces holding a system in its current state flips sign, so that crossing it draws the system toward a different stable state on its own.
Basin of attraction
The range of conditions around a stable state from which the system will return to that state on its own — pictured as a valley in a landscape, with the stable state at the bottom.
Resilience
In this technical sense, how large a disturbance a system can absorb without being pushed into a different basin of attraction — not how healthy the system currently looks.
Hysteresis
The asymmetry between what pushes a system past a threshold and what it takes to push it back, which arises because a different mechanism defends each stable state.
Threshold model of collective behaviour
Mark Granovetter’s model of decisions — joining a riot, a strike, a run on a bank — where each person acts only once enough other people already have, so the outcome depends on the whole distribution of individual thresholds, not their average.

Example: Granovetter’s Riot That Almost Didn’t Happen

The sociologist Mark Granovetter built the cleanest version of a threshold flip in 1978, stripped down to pure numbers, to make a point about crowds rather than reefs: a population’s average attitude can tell you almost nothing about whether it riots, because the outcome depends on the exact shape of the distribution of individual thresholds — the point Day 12’s lesson on distributions made from a different angle.

Imagine 100 people, each with a personal threshold for joining a riot: the number of other people who must already be rioting before that person joins in. Give them thresholds 0, 1, 2, all the way up to 99 — one person at each level, a perfectly even spread. The person with threshold 0 needs nobody else and riots immediately. That one rioter is now enough to trigger the person with threshold 1, who triggers the person with threshold 2, and so on in a chain, until all 100 people have joined. A population that looks, on average, moderately reluctant to riot (the average threshold is 49.5) riots completely.

1. Build the baseline population. 100 people, thresholds 0 through 99, one person per value — an even spread, average threshold 49.5.

2. Run the cascade. Threshold-0 person riots → 1 rioting, which meets or exceeds threshold-1’s requirement → 2 rioting, meeting threshold-2’s requirement → the chain continues unbroken through threshold 99 → all 100 riot.

3. Change one person’s threshold. Move the single person who had threshold 1 up to threshold 2, leaving everyone else unchanged. The population’s average threshold barely moves.

4. Re-run the cascade. Threshold-0 person riots → 1 rioting. The person who used to have threshold 1 now needs 2 rioters and there is only 1 → no one else has a threshold at or below 1 → the chain stops. Only 1 person riots.

5. Read the result. A change to a single person’s threshold, invisible in the population average, moved the system from one equilibrium (100 rioting) to a completely different one (1 rioting) — the same qualitative jump a reef makes crossing its own grazing threshold.

Equation / Model

r* = F(r*)

The equilibrium share of the population participating equals the cumulative share of the population whose threshold is at or below that same equilibrium share.

r
the running share of the population already participating (rioting, striking, joining a bank run), expressed as a fraction from 0 to 1
F(x)
the cumulative distribution of thresholds: the fraction of the population whose personal threshold is x or lower
r*
an equilibrium — a value of r at which exactly the right share of the population has a threshold low enough to be already satisfied, so participation stops changing

Start at r = 0 and feed the population’s own thresholds back into F repeatedly: each round, F tells you how many people are now willing to join given the last round’s participation, and that becomes the next round’s r. The process settles where the curve of F crosses the line r = r* — which can happen at more than one point. A population’s distribution of thresholds can have two stable crossing points, a near-zero one and a near-total one, with almost no outside push separating which one the population actually reaches — the social-science version of the same ridge between two basins that a grazing threshold creates on a reef.

Why It Matters

Once you can ask whether a system you are looking at — a market, an institution, a habit, a reef — has a real threshold hiding in it, you stop reading its current, calm-looking state as proof that it is safe from a large disturbance, and you stop assuming that reversing a disturbance will automatically undo its effect. Both assumptions fail exactly where alternative stable states are real: a system can look fine right up until it crosses its ridge, and getting it back can cost far more, and take far longer, than whatever pushed it over in the first place.

Settled. Threshold dynamics and hysteresis are mathematically well established and demonstrated in controlled and semi-controlled systems — shallow lakes flipping between clear and turbid states (Scheffer and colleagues, 2001) are among the best-documented real cases.

Debated. How common true alternative stable states are on coral reefs specifically is a live argument inside ecology, not a settled question: Mumby, Hastings and Edwards (2007) found evidence for them on Caribbean reefs; Bruno and colleagues (2009), surveying far more reefs, found coral-to-algae dominance shifts considerably less widespread than assumed, with most degraded reefs simply losing coral rather than gaining seaweed at Jamaica’s scale.

Value judgment Restoring a flipped reef, or heading one off before it tips, usually means limiting fishing that coastal communities depend on now, to protect a resilience whose benefit mostly arrives later and falls on different people — a cost-now, benefit-later, burden-shifted question about who should bear the price of keeping a threshold from being crossed, which the ecology alone cannot answer.

Remember These
  • A system has alternative stable states when more than one self-sustaining configuration is possible under the very same outside conditions.
  • A threshold is the point where the balance of forces holding a system in one state flips, drawing it toward a different state on its own.
  • Hysteresis means the push that flips a system and the push that would flip it back are not mirror images, because a different mechanism defends each state.
  • A population’s whole distribution of thresholds, not its average attitude, decides whether it tips — and whether it tips back.
  • Proving a real alternative stable state exists is hard; some apparent phase shifts turn out, on closer survey, to be simple decline rather than a flip to a new state.
