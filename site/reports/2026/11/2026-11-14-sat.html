Today’s News

Starship reaches orbit and deploys 26 Starlink satellites, but the booster aborts its tower catch

Flight 15 met its main goal. The first attempt to catch the ship itself with the tower is due about 4:15 PM ET today.

SpaceX launched Starship Flight 15 from Starbase at about 5:47 PM ET on Friday. The ship reached orbit and released all 26 Starlink V3 satellites (CNBC, Space.com and several spaceflight outlets). Super Heavy Booster 22, the first of the new V3 boosters to try a tower return, aborted itself late in its coast back. SpaceX spokesman Dan Huot’s phrase was that “the booster aborted itself”. It diverted to a splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico. CNBC reported that it exploded on impact. Single source One outlet reported that one of the 33 engines failed before the no-catch call; we found that in only one report.

The ship is due to come home about 22.5 hours after liftoff, around 4:15 PM ET today, for the first attempt to catch an upper stage with the tower arms. Cameras on three of the satellites are imaging its heat shield, and those images feed the go/no-go decision for reentry.

Why it matters Orbit, a full payload deployment and two catchable stages are the steps between today’s Starship and a rocket that is reused like an airliner. That reuse is the basis of SpaceX’s cost case and of NASA’s Artemis III lander. A failed booster catch does not change the payload result. It does mean the V3 booster has yet to show it can come back. SpaceX (SPCX) closed Friday at $139.11, up 3.6% on the week.

CNBC, Space.com, KeepTrack, Marsprogress and other spaceflight outlets, via search summaries · SpaceX statements as quoted.

A telecom failure grounds U.S. flights nationwide for more than four hours

It was the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023. Cancellations are spilling into the weekend.

The Federal Aviation Administration stopped departures across the country on Friday afternoon after telecommunications problems hit several of its regional air route traffic control centers. It lifted the stop at about 8:15 PM ET, nearly four and a half hours later. Reports put the start between 2:54 and 3:54 PM. More than 10,000 flights were delayed or cancelled (CNN, the Washington Post, CNBC, Al Jazeera, FAA statement). Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed overloaded routers. Homeland Security officials say the cause is still under investigation. The FAA and its contractor deny that the agency’s new automated traffic-management system was to blame.

What’s next Airline stocks open Monday after a week in which United fell 6% and Delta 5% on a single day over fuel costs. Watch for the FAA’s root-cause account and whether Congress ties air-traffic-control funding to the December 11 spending bill.

The Week’s Ten Stories

Nvidia’s week: a board inquiry, a founder’s resignation, and a chip-export fight

The stock fell 12.8% in five sessions to its lowest close of the past year, while its rivals rose. By Friday investors were treating it as a policy-risk story, not only a governance one.

How it started. On Sunday, November 8, several outlets reported that Nvidia’s board had opened an independent inquiry into Jensen Huang’s personal relationships. The reports tied one of them to Megaspeed, a Singapore data-centre company that U.S. officials suspect of passing Nvidia chips to China. The stock fell 4.3% on Monday and 5.5% on Tuesday. On Wednesday Morgan Stanley dropped it from its top-pick list and Baird downgraded it. The Justice Department’s national-security division took over the Megaspeed inquiry.

How it developed. On Wednesday night the Wall Street Journal reported that the board was preparing to remove Huang. On Thursday Nvidia said he had resigned as chief executive and director, “effective immediately.” CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO, and former Synopsys chief Harvey Jones became the company’s first independent chair. The shares fell as much as 14% and closed down 4.3%. Money went into the rivals: AMD rose 5.9% that day and Broadcom 5.5%.

How it ended. On Friday the selling spread. AMD fell 8.8%, Broadcom 4.8% and Nvidia another 2.0%, to $162.85. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said his department supports blacklisting Megaspeed. Speaker Mike Johnson spoke of a “growing consensus” on limiting chip exports to China, and Republicans are discussing attaching restrictions to the defence bill. China warned against misuse of export controls. Since Friday’s close, Reuters has reported a second relationship with an employee, and the Senate Banking chair has asked Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify voluntarily on December 3. Single source We found each of those in a single report. Nvidia says it found no evidence of chips diverted through Megaspeed.

What was misunderstood. Thursday’s headlines read the resignation as a threat to the whole AI trade. The week’s prices say otherwise. For the week, AMD rose 18.9%, Broadcom 18.2%, Micron 12.8% and the semiconductor index 8.1%. What investors priced was Nvidia’s own position, and then, on Friday, Washington’s export rules, which hit every company selling advanced chips to China.

What’s unresolved Who runs the company permanently; whether Huang, still a large shareholder, has any further role; what the Justice Department inquiry finds; and whether Congress writes chip rules into the defence bill. Tuesday’s results come first: consensus is about $109 billion of revenue and $2.48 a share (LSEG via IG), against company guidance of $108 billion, plus or minus 2%. Keep on the watchlist.

Hot jobs and inflation data turn a December rate hike into a near-certainty

Within five days the debate moved from whether the Fed hikes to whether it moves by half a point.

How it started. October payrolls, released Friday, November 6, showed 165,000 new jobs, against forecasts near 60,000. Unemployment fell to 4.1% and hourly pay rose 0.4%. How it developed. On Tuesday Governor Christopher Waller said a half-point move “should be considered” if core inflation printed 0.3% or more. Hours later October CPI came in at 0.4% headline and 0.3% core, each a tenth above forecast. Dallas’s Lorie Logan said she sees at least two more hikes. New York’s John Williams said more hikes are likely. San Francisco’s Mary Daly, Cleveland’s Beth Hammack and Chicago’s Austan Goolsbee also leaned toward tightening. How it ended. October producer prices on Friday rose 0.3%, a tenth below forecast. But they were up 6.5% from a year earlier, the most since 2022, and a narrower core measure rose 0.5%.

CME futures, as reported, ended the week with roughly a 90% chance of a quarter-point hike on December 9 and about 10% for a half point. A week earlier half a point was priced at zero. Bessent urged the Fed not to overreact to what he called an energy-supply shock from the Iran war. The Fed sets policy independently of the Treasury.

What was misunderstood Friday’s “cooler PPI” headlines. The headline fell because retailers and wholesalers absorbed costs in their margins. Goods prices still rose 0.5%. The report did nothing to weaken the case for a hike.

The bond market’s worst week in two years, and real yields did the damage

The two-year yield rose 37 basis points, its biggest weekly jump since 2024. All three refunding auctions tailed.

Treasury par yields rose across the curve from Friday, November 6 to Friday, November 13 (U.S. Treasury, EOD official). The 2-year went from 4.51% to 4.88%, the 5-year from 4.64% to 4.98%, the 10-year from 4.86% to 5.15% and the 30-year from 5.26% to 5.47%. The curve flattened: the gap between the 2-year and 10-year narrowed from 35 to 27 basis points. That is the shape of a market pricing more Fed hikes, rather than one worried about long-run inflation.

The inflation-protected curve confirms it. The 10-year real yield rose from 2.63% to 2.91%, 28 of the nominal 10-year’s 29 basis points. The 10-year breakeven inflation rate barely moved, from 2.23% to 2.24% (FRED). Supply added pressure. The $125 billion refunding sold 3-year notes at 4.627%, 10-year notes at 5.020% and 30-year bonds at 5.441%, and all three tailed, the first time since May. The new 30-year carries a 5.375% coupon, the highest since 2001.

Why it matters Real yields near 2.9% are a tighter financial condition than any Fed hike this year. They work through mortgages, through gold, which has no yield and lost 4.6%, and through the valuations of long-dated growth stocks. Existing-home sales fell to their lowest annual rate since 2010 in Thursday’s report. Keep on the watchlist.

What was misunderstood That the November 5 crash meant the AI build-out had peaked. A week later the companies that supply it were the market’s leaders. What the market now questions is which companies capture the spending, not whether the spending continues.

Ukraine talks gain a format: Abu Dhabi, the Kremlin, then a European team

Moscow’s core demands have not moved, but the talks now have a schedule of sorts and a European seat.

U.S.-mediated direct talks between Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi ended Wednesday with agreement in principle on what may be the largest prisoner exchange since 2022. On Thursday envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner spent about two hours with President Putin, then flew to Ireland to see President Zelenskyy. What Changed in the World

The Fed is no longer cutting, or pausing. A month ago the argument was about how long the Fed would hold. Now it is about how fast it hikes. The labour market has not cracked: payrolls, wages and claims all say so. Inflation is running at an annual pace near 3.7% on core CPI over three months, and wholesale prices are up 6.5% over a year. Higher policy rates are the market’s base case into 2027.

The cost of money is rising on its own. The 10-year real yield rose 28 basis points in a week while inflation expectations held still. That is tighter money, not an inflation scare. It is happening at the same moment Treasury auctions are drawing weaker demand at the highest coupons in a generation.

The AI trade lost its founder-king but kept its customers. Nvidia’s leadership crisis was the week’s biggest corporate event. Yet the companies selling into the same build-out had one of their best weeks of the year. The open question is now policy: who gets to sell chips to China, decided in Congress and at APEC.

Power in Washington is being reshuffled. Fifty-five Democratic senators, a contested majority-leader race, new Republican leaders and a funding deadline in four weeks. Fiscal and trade policy for 2027 starts being written in December, not January.

Scorecard

There was no Weekly Review last Saturday, so this scorecard covers two weeks: 67 calls whose deadlines have passed, from mid-October to Friday. 52 came out as written, 14 did not, and one could not be verified. Short-horizon calls on the day’s close, made with most of the information already in hand, did well. The misses cluster in three places, and those are what we have to learn from.

1. We kept underrating limited U.S. strikes on Iran. We gave 20–40% to strikes before November 3, and 15–30% (in two editions) to strikes between November 4 and 7. Both happened: the “defensive” strikes on Qeshm Island on November 2, then the strike near Hormuz on November 6. Verdict: wrong, three times. Why: we read the White House’s pre-election restraint as a broader pause, and we discounted strikes officials called defensive. Lesson: during a blockade, small strikes are routine, not exceptional. Define the event precisely and price it higher.

2. Our ranges were too narrow in a high-volatility week. The November 1 scenario matrix gave its base case, the S&P within 2% of 7,580, a 45–55% chance. The index ended the week 2.7% lower, in the bear case we rated 18–28%. We gave 15–25% to a VIX close above 25, and it hit 28.18 on November 5. We put 55–70% on payrolls between 25,000 and 100,000, and they came in at 165,000. We put job openings in a 6.8–7.4 million band, and they came in at 7.5 million. Why: we anchored on consensus and on the prior month. Lesson: when the VIX implies a weekly move near 2.7%, a ±2% base case sits inside one standard deviation and should not be the majority outcome. Data bands need to be at least as wide as the forecasters’ own range.

3. Single-session calls on fading moves. We expected WTI to settle above $96.46 after a morning spike on November 3; it settled 37 cents lower. The Nasdaq was meant to hold early futures gains on November 2, and did not. Vulcan was meant to launch on Friday, and a ground-equipment fault scrubbed it. October PPI was meant to come in at 0.4% or more, and it was 0.3%. Lesson: morning moves on headlines fade often enough that a 60–70% call to hold them is too high. Ground systems are a scrub risk separate from the rocket and the weather.

What went right. The rates calls: the 30-year auction clearing above 5.30% and the 10-year staying above 5% were both right. So were the Bank of England, RBA and OPEC+ decisions, Cisco’s revenue, Starship lifting off on its first day, and Treasury holding refunding sizes. Two were right for thin reasons: the Nasdaq trailed the Dow by three hundredths of a point on Thursday, and the S&P beat 7,550 by twelve points on Friday. We count them, but they are not evidence of skill. Unverifiable: whether Binance published its broader proof of reserves by November 4. We found its October 31 snapshot proof but no confirmation of the later one.

Science

A one-in-ten-billion decay. CERN’s LHCb experiment reported the first evidence of the neutral B meson decaying into two muons, at 3.2 sigma. The Standard Model predicts it about once in ten billion decays. Evidence at that level is not yet a discovery, which needs 5 sigma, but it is the first sign of a process physicists have hunted for decades.

Quantum simulation. A Nature paper used a neutral-atom simulator with up to 180 qubits to observe “prethermal” phase transitions: long-lived states that persist before a system settles into equilibrium. It is a physics instrument, not a general-purpose quantum computer.

Replication. A five-laboratory study in Nature Neuroscience found that psilocybin changed fear and avoidance behaviour in mice in the short term, but lasting effects were modest or inconsistent. That is a useful check on single-lab claims in a heavily promoted field.

Climate and astronomy. A Nature Climate Change projection has fossil-fuel CO₂ emissions falling about 0.4% in 2026, mostly because war-driven oil prices cut demand, not because clean energy grew faster. A Nature paper reports two concentric gas rings around the white dwarf WD J1328−1450. In early-stage diabetes work, researchers found raised levels of the protein Miro1 in the muscle of people with type 2 diabetes, and removing it restored insulin response in preclinical models.

Overhyped & Undercovered

Overhyped: “Nvidia’s crisis is the AI trade’s crisis.” The coverage was wall-to-wall. Over the same five days the chip index rose 8% and the S&P 2.5%. The lasting market risk in the story is export policy, which got a fraction of the attention the personal reporting did.

Overhyped: “Wholesale inflation cools.” October’s PPI headline was a tenth below forecast because margins shrank. The year-over-year rate rose to 6.5%.

Undercovered: real yields. A 28-basis-point jump in the 10-year real yield in one week, with inflation expectations flat and weak demand at all three auctions, is a large tightening of financial conditions. It got less attention than any of the Fed speeches that helped cause it.

Undercovered: the housing freeze. Existing-home sales at their lowest annual rate since 2010, with mortgage rates rising again, is where higher rates are already doing damage. Home Depot and Lowe’s report next week.

Undercovered: credit is unbothered. High-yield spreads tightened to 3.20 percentage points while Treasuries sold off. Either lenders see no recession, or credit is late to the story. That divergence is worth watching.

Factors

Equal weight vs cap weight: RSP +2.37% vs SPY +2.59%, so the advance was nearly broad. Small vs large: IWM +1.41%, which lagged. Growth vs value: QQQ +3.63% led the Dow’s +2.07%, with the growth lead coming from semiconductors. Defensives: staples, health care and utilities all rose about 2–2.5% and sit near one-year highs. That is unusual alongside a growth rally, and consistent with investors hedging higher rates.

Cross-asset

Stocks vs bonds: both moved, in opposite directions (S&P +2.5%, TLT −3.1%). That is the normal pattern when yields rise for growth and policy reasons rather than from fear. Gold vs real yields: textbook. Real yields rose 28 bp and gold fell 4.6%. Oil vs breakevens: oil −2.5%, breakevens flat. High yield vs equities: both firm. Semis vs AI capex: suppliers up, Nvidia down. The market is separating the build-out from its biggest single beneficiary. Bitcoin vs liquidity: bitcoin fell with tighter real rates while stocks rose, a divergence that has persisted for six weeks. Dollar vs EM: the Hang Seng fell 3.4% as the dollar rose 1.1%.
