Top Stories

Anthropic prices at $83 and raises $81 billion in the largest IPO on record

The deal was upsized by 155 million shares and priced $5 above its range. The shares trade on Nasdaq as ANT from this morning, and futures are already pricing a strong start.

Anthropic priced its initial public offering late Wednesday at $83 a share, above the $70–78 marketing range, and sold 980 million Class A shares against the 825 million first planned (CNBC, TechCrunch, CNN, CBC). Gross proceeds are about $81.3 billion, ahead of SpaceX’s offering in June as the largest IPO ever. A separately arranged $26 billion private placement with Nvidia, Google and Microsoft was done at the same price. One Seoul outlet’s $107.3 billion total adds the two together. Axios puts the initial market value at about $1.08 trillion; on a fully diluted basis, counting options and restricted stock, reports put it near $1.5 trillion. We found no company release confirming the final terms, though every major outlet now reports them without qualification.

Trading begins today on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under ANT. Nasdaq will run an opening auction to find a price, so the first trade will probably come well after 9:30. Bloomberg, citing grey-market indications, said shares could open 20–26% above the offer price. That is a single report of informal pre-trading, not an actual quote. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley led the deal.

Nasdaq-100 futures were up about 2.1% this morning, and Reuters attributed the move to hopes for a strong debut. That is a reasonable reading of the timing, but there is no way to show cause: futures had also bounced in after-hours trading on Palo Alto Networks’ results (below), and QQQ was already up 1.4% by 8 PM Wednesday.

Why it matters For two weeks the offering has been blamed for selling elsewhere: software stocks fell 6–9% on Monday as investors reportedly raised cash for allocations. A deal this size that trades well tells the market there is still appetite to pay for AI. One that breaks its $83 offer in the first days would hit the whole AI trade, which is already fragile after Nvidia’s reversal.
What’s next Watch the opening print against $83, and whether the money going into ANT comes out of other software and semiconductor stocks again. Index providers’ rules for fast entry of new mega-listings will become the next question; nothing has been announced.

The jump from $1.08 trillion to about $1.5 trillion between reports is the gap between basic shares outstanding and a fully diluted count; the outlets did not publish the share counts behind either figure. The $26 billion placement at $83 means three of the company’s largest suppliers and partners are also among its largest new holders, a circular arrangement that bulls call strategic alignment and sceptics call vendor financing.

What would undo the bullish read: a first trade below $90, about 8% above the offer, after futures priced a roughly 2% Nasdaq gain, would suggest institutions sold their allocations into the opening.

Fed minutes show “almost all” officials expect more rate rises; Waller floats a half point

October’s hold was closer than it looked, and a few officials put a bigger December move on the table.

Minutes of the October 27–28 meeting, released at 2:00 PM Wednesday, said “almost all” participants judged that further increases would likely be appropriate, and that “a couple” said a move larger than a quarter point could be considered in December if the data were unfavourable (Fed minutes as reported by InvestmentNews and others). The decision to hold was 10–2, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack voting for a quarter-point rise. Officials also discussed debt-financed AI investment as a financial-stability concern.

Governor Christopher Waller told CNBC on Wednesday the Fed may need to “shock people” with a half-point rise in December, or else pair a quarter point with a clear signal of a January move. Market pricing for December 9 is about 93–96% for a quarter-point rise and about 4–7% for a half point (CME FedWatch as reported); a week ago a rise was priced at about 87%.

Bonds took the minutes calmly. Wednesday’s official curve had the 2-year unchanged at 4.80%, the 10-year down 3 basis points to 5.07% and the 30-year down 5 to 5.41% (Treasury). A strong 20-year auction an hour earlier helped: it sold at 5.459%, below October’s 5.469%, with a bid-to-cover of 2.63 and indirect bidders taking about 60%.

Why it matters The market has priced a quarter point. Waller’s half point is the risk that is not priced. A half point would mean a policy rate rising faster than inflation is falling, and that hurts the most richly valued stocks first.

Palo Alto Networks beats, raises its outlook and buys Applied Compute for $4.5 billion in stock

The shares are up about 9% before the bell, on top of a 9.9% gain on Wednesday, and lifted other security names after hours.

Palo Alto Networks reported fiscal first-quarter revenue of $3.44 billion, up 39% and above its own $3.30–3.31 billion guide, and adjusted earnings of $1.05 a share against about $0.97 expected (company release, as reported by Benzinga). It raised its full-year revenue guide to $14.52–14.57 billion from $14.1–14.2 billion, and agreed to buy the AI start-up Applied Compute for about $4.5 billion in stock. The shares closed Wednesday up 9.90% at $339.92, traded at about $365–368 after hours and are indicated up about 9–10% before the open. CrowdStrike rose 4.4% and Okta 3.3% after hours.

Part of Wednesday’s gain came before the results, which suggests positioning ahead of the report; outlets did not identify a separate catalyst. Revenue growth of 39% is unusually fast for a company this size and will include acquisitions; the release as summarised did not split out organic growth.

Taiwan’s market falls more than 4% as chip and circuit-board stocks hit their limit

Asia did not share Wall Street’s optimism. Investors there worried about AI valuations and about the new U.S. curbs on chips in overseas data centres.

The Taiex fell 4.40% to 38,426.89, its lowest close since early November, and chip and printed-circuit-board stocks fell by the daily limit (Yahoo Finance close; Investing.com). AP reported TSMC down 3.5%. Hong Kong’s Hang Seng lost 1.95% to 22,841.62 as Alibaba fell after its results (below), and Shanghai slipped 0.28%. Japan’s Nikkei rose 0.71%. Korea’s Kospi is shown up 1.39% at 5,137.89 in Yahoo’s data, but two outlets report it lower on the day, so treat Korea’s direction as unsettled until the official close is confirmed.

The outlets we read cited worries over AI valuations and the Commerce Department’s interim rule of November 16. The rule requires licences for advanced chips going to data centres in several Southeast Asian countries and to any facility serving Chinese-headquartered firms. China’s commerce ministry has promised “necessary measures” in response. Taiwan’s export economy sits at the centre of both worries.

Why it matters TSMC’s Taiwan shares and its U.S. listing usually move together. A 4% fall in Taipei against a 2% rise in Nasdaq futures is a split that seldom lasts long. Either Taipei is wrong about the AI spending cycle, or the overnight optimism in New York rests on one IPO.

Trump, Xi and Putin meet in Shenzhen; state media report a “Peace Declaration”

The first three-way meeting ran about 90 minutes with no joint press conference. China and Russia describe a four-point text, and Washington has not confirmed it.

Presidents Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin met at the Kylin Villa in Shenzhen on Thursday, on the sidelines of APEC (ABC Australia, Al Jazeera, NBC live coverage). Chinese state media, TASS and RT report a “Peace Declaration” with four points: reform of the United Nations, stable energy flows among the three countries, a passage on the International Criminal Court, and a commitment to dialogue on nuclear risk, including the line that nuclear war “cannot be won and must never be fought” (Reuters, citing Chinese state media). Nikkei describes the energy point as a vow to raise oil supply. NBC reported the talks ended “with no immediate statement” from the U.S. side, and we found no White House readout.

Earlier, at their bilateral meeting, Xi called the talks “positive and constructive,” and China’s readout says the two sides agreed to build a “constructive relationship of strategic stability.” It gave no detail on extending the trade truce, which expires January 10, 2027, or on chips. The reported text contains no tariff cuts, no sanctions changes and nothing on a Ukraine ceasefire.

Why it matters For markets, tariffs and chips are what count, and nothing on either has been reported. An energy-supply pledge among the world’s largest oil importer and two of its largest producers is the item with a possible oil-price read-through. Without numbers it is a statement of intent, and Brent fell again overnight.

Today’s data. Risks & Scenarios

Anthropic’s first trade is above its $83 offer price. Probability 75–90%. Basis: the deal was upsized and priced $5 above range, grey-market indications point to a 20–26% premium (one report), and Nasdaq futures are up 2%. Large, hot deals rarely open below their offer. But at this size the supply of shares is unusual, and the AI group sold off after good news twice this week.

The S&P 500 ends its five-day losing streak (closes above 7,323.63). Probability 60–75%. Basis: futures imply a gain of about 1.2%, and Wednesday’s breadth was less bad than Tuesday’s. Against that, opening gains have faded twice this month, and Taiwan’s 4% fall shows hardware nerves are not settled. It is invalidated by a close in the red, led again by health care, financials and small caps.

A half-point Fed rise in December. Priced at about 4–7%. Our assessment is that the true risk is higher than the market price, because a governor has floated it publicly and the minutes show a couple of officials open to it. November’s consumer prices, due before the meeting, will decide it. We do not put a number on it this morning.

AI & Technology

Anthropic leads the paper this morning. Its listing makes the maker of the Claude models a public company worth about a trillion dollars on its first day.

Chips and export rules. The Commerce Department’s interim rule of November 16 closes what officials called the remote-access loophole. It bars high-end Nvidia and AMD chips from data centres anywhere when the operator knows the end user is a restricted party, and it requires licences for shipments to Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. There is a 90-day transition and 60 days for comment. Taiwan’s 4% fall overnight was partly attributed to it. Nvidia’s guide already excludes China data-centre sales.

We have not seen the companies’ own announcements for all three, so treat the details as unconfirmed. OpenAI published research claiming agents now complete 46% of research tasks that take a person two to four hours; that is the company’s own benchmark.

Science

Human origins. A paper in this week’s Nature by Jeffrey Rose and colleagues argues that early human settlement of Arabia’s Rub’ al Khali, the Empty Quarter, depended on where good tool-making stone could be found, not only on water and climate. It is an archaeological argument from site distributions, and it adds a factor to the climate-driven account of human dispersal rather than replacing it.

Parkinson’s. A study in Nature Communications reports that LRRK2, the gene most often mutated in inherited Parkinson’s disease, blocks the recruitment of RAB proteins and so reduces neuropeptide release in nerve cells. That is a possible mechanism, shown in laboratory models. It is relevant because LRRK2 inhibitors are already in clinical trials, but it is not a treatment result.

Memory. A second Nature Communications paper maps how a region of the human hippocampus, the subiculum, encodes spatial information used in episodic memory, from recordings in patients.

Space

A Falcon 9 carrying the classified USSF-317 mission is set for Vandenberg with a window from 9:46 PM to 1:46 AM Eastern, after several slips since November 12; the booster is to land on a droneship (SpaceX). With Vulcan grounded, every near-term national-security launch is a SpaceX launch.

Vulcan. ULA is examining low booster performance as a possible factor in Sunday’s failure about 10 minutes into flight, which destroyed a batch of Amazon Leo satellites. That is not a confirmed root cause, and all Vulcan launches are on hold. It was the rocket’s return to flight after February’s booster anomaly. An Ariane 64 is listed to fly Amazon Leo satellites from Kourou on November 23; we did not find a fresh confirmation of the date.

Artemis and Starship. NASA’s November 4 plan adds a crewed 14-day Orion mission in Earth orbit that docks with a Blue Moon test article before a Starship V3 test, aiming for a crewed landing in 2028; no lander has been chosen. SpaceX (SPCX) rose 1.27% to $143.50 on Wednesday, with no company news found. Rocket Lab fell 3.60% and AST SpaceMobile 7.61%, closing 33 cents above its 52-week low.
