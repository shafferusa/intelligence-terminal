1 · Refresher: inventory cost flows under rising costs

Lesson 8 covered what goes into inventory; this is the cost-flow part of Module 5 you finished on Nov 22.

A cost formula splits one pool of cost in two. Goods available for sale (opening inventory plus purchases) is either sold, and becomes cost of sales, or still on hand, and stays on the balance sheet. FIFO sends the oldest costs to cost of sales; LIFO, allowed only under US GAAP, sends the newest; weighted average uses a blend. It is an accounting assumption, not how the boxes leave the warehouse (pp.118–119).

When costs are rising, LIFO shows the cheaper picture on purpose. Newer, dearer costs hit the income statement, so gross profit and tax are lower; the only cash difference is the tax saved, which raises operating cash flow. The inventory left on the balance sheet carries old, cheap costs, so the current ratio is lower and debt-to-equity higher. FIFO is the reverse. When costs fall, every direction flips (pp.125–126).

Periodic or perpetual matters only for some methods. Periodic works out cost of sales at period end; perpetual updates at each sale. FIFO and specific identification agree either way; LIFO and weighted average can differ (pp.122–124).

Clearing up

Your workbook flags the balance-sheet inventory section. The balance sheet holds whichever costs the method did not expense: under FIFO the newest, close to replacement cost; under LIFO the oldest, possibly years stale. The bridge between them, the LIFO reserve, is on Dec 1 with your other flagged Module 5 items.

Memorize
  • Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − ending inventory
  • Weighted average unit cost = cost of goods available ÷ units available
  • Rising costs, LIFO vs FIFO: higher cost of sales, lower profit and tax, higher operating cash flow, lower inventory

2 · Today: fiscal policy, and what central banks are for

What can a government do with taxes and spending to steady the economy, how do you tell whether it is pressing the accelerator or the brake, and why is it harder than it sounds? Lesson 9 gave the first contrast; today fills it in.

Two actors, two levers. The central bank works through money, credit and interest rates; the government works through taxes and spending, which reach incomes directly. Both lean against the cycle (pp.80–82). Fiscal policy has three jobs: steadying total demand, shifting income between groups, and deciding what resources go to, such as roads or defence (p.83).

The demand chain. A tax cut raises disposable income or after-tax profit, spending rises, then aggregate demand and output. A government purchase is someone’s income at once. The chain breaks if people save the cut, or if there is no spare capacity, when prices rise instead. Keynesians see it as powerful with slack; monetarists as temporary (p.83).

Reading the stance: strip out the cycle. A larger deficit usually means looser policy, a larger surplus tighter (p.85). But in a recession tax receipts fall and benefit payments rise by themselves: automatic stabilizers widen the deficit with no decision taken. A deliberate change in tax rates or spending is discretionary. So judge the stance by the structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit, the one that would exist at full employment. A deficit that shrinks only because the recovery lifted tax receipts is not tightening (p.99).

Deficit versus debt. The deficit is one year’s gap, a flow; the national debt is the pile of past deficits, a stock (p.88). If the real interest rate on the debt exceeds real growth, the debt-to-GDP ratio tends to rise even with no new borrowing beyond interest, and a primary deficit makes it worse (p.89). Inflation erodes the real value of the debt, so cash interest overstates its real cost (p.99).

Does the debt matter? Calm: it is owed to citizens, may fund investment that raises future income, may use idle resources, may force tax reform. Worry: future taxes blunt work and investment; the central bank may be pressed to print money to fund it; borrowing can crowd out private investment by raising rates. Ricardian equivalence: if households expect a tax cut today to mean higher taxes later, they save it, and the stimulus vanishes (pp.89–91).

The tools. Spending is transfer payments (welfare, pensions: no output bought), current spending on running costs, and capital spending on infrastructure (p.92). Taxes are direct, on income, wealth and profit, or indirect, on spending (p.93). A good tax is judged on simplicity, efficiency, fairness (equals pay alike, the better-off pay more) and whether it raises enough revenue. The trade-off is speed against potency: indirect taxes act fastest; direct taxes and transfers need lead time; capital spending is slowest but adds to future capacity. Spending moves output more than a tax cut, except transfers to the poorest, who spend nearly all of it (p.95).

The multiplier. Each round of spending becomes someone’s income; part is taxed and part saved, and the rest is spent again. The rounds shrink, and their sum is the multiplier (pp.96–97). A balanced budget still expands output: the spending enters in full, but the matching tax removes only the part people would have spent, so the balanced-budget multiplier is 1, not 0 (p.97).

Fiscal multiplier = 1 ÷ [1 − c(1 − t)]

c
marginal propensity to consume: the share of extra disposable income spent, a decimal; c + s = 1 (p.96)
t
net tax rate: taxes minus transfers as a share of income, a decimal (p.96)
Result
change in output per unit of extra government spending; for example (hypothetical), c = 0.8 and t = 0.25 give 2.5 (p.97)

Why it is hard to execute. Three lags: recognition (seeing the problem), action (passing the law), impact (the effect arriving). Forecasts are poor, full employment is hard to measure, idle resources may have supply-side causes that spending cannot fix, markets may refuse to fund large deficits, and crowding out can offset the push (pp.100–102).

What central banks are for (Module 4). Modern money is fiat: valued because law and public accept it, not because it converts into gold, so its issuer must protect its value and trust (p.107). The central bank issues currency, banks for the government and the banks, oversees payments and often the banks, holds foreign reserves and runs monetary policy. As lender of last resort it can create reserves for a sound bank short of cash, which deters runs (pp.107–108). Its main aim is price stability; some, like the Fed, also have an employment goal.

Assumptions and exceptions
  • The multiplier needs spare capacity and constant prices; at full employment the push goes into prices.
  • With taxes, use c(1 − t), not 1 ÷ (1 − c).
  • Ricardian equivalence holds only if households foresee the future taxes.
Memorize
  • Deficit = flow, debt = stock; real rate above real growth pushes the debt ratio up
  • Stance: structural deficit, not the actual one
  • Speed: indirect taxes fastest, capital spending slowest
  • Fiscal multiplier = 1 ÷ [1 − c(1 − t)]; balanced-budget multiplier = 1
  • Lags: recognition, action, impact
