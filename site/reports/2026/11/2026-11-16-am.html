Top Stories

Asia’s chip stocks slide further before Nvidia’s results

Taiwan had one of its worst days of the year, Korea fell 3.6% and Tokyo closed below 60,000. Nasdaq futures point to a third straight losing day for U.S. chip stocks.

Taiwan’s Taiex closed at 41,040.08, down 6.0% from Friday’s 43,675.12 (Yahoo Finance close). TSMC fell between 3.4% and 5.7%, depending on the source and time of the quote (AP, MLQ); we could not confirm its exact close. Korea’s Kospi fell 3.59% to about 5,100. SK Hynix dropped 7.9% and Samsung Electronics about 3.6% (TradingKey). In Tokyo, the Nikkei 225 lost 2.42% to 59,662, and memory maker Kioxia fell 7.7%. Hong Kong was the exception, up 0.5%. Mainland China’s Shanghai Composite fell 0.9% after weak activity data.

Regional reports linked the selling to worries about the AI trade after a week in which Nvidia lost its founder-chief and fell 12.8%, and in which AMD, Broadcom and the memory makers gave back part of their November rally. AMD fell 8.75% on Friday and the VanEck semiconductor fund (SMH) 2.99%. In the U.S., Nasdaq-100 futures were 0.80% lower at 27,809 at about 6:27 AM, S&P futures 0.31% lower at 7,559, and Dow futures unchanged, so the selling is again concentrated in technology. The weekend’s new pressure on Nvidia from the Treasury (next story) came on top of this. No single confirmed catalyst explains Asia’s move. It followed both the weekend headlines and the soft Chinese data.

Why it matters Taiwan and Korea hold the factories that make the AI build-out: TSMC’s chips and the high-bandwidth memory from SK Hynix and Samsung. A 6% drop in Taiwan says investors are now questioning the whole supply chain, not just one company’s governance. Nvidia’s guidance tomorrow is the next hard data point.
What’s next Whether the U.S. open follows Asia. Watch SMH, TSM, MU, AMD and the Nasdaq-100. The S&P has been held up by equal-weight and defensive sectors; on Friday staples rose 1.4% and the equal-weight fund 0.2%. Nvidia reports Tuesday at about 4:20 PM, with the call at 5:00 PM.

Bessent asks whether Nvidia inflated sales through China-bound shell buyers

The Treasury Secretary gave the board 10 days and urged it to hire an outside chief executive. This is political pressure, not a finding.

Speaking on Fox News on Sunday, Bessent said there was a “culture of noncompliance” at Nvidia. He asked whether the company had “goosed” revenue and earnings through sales to apparent shell companies, many in Singapore, that channelled chips to China. He said he wanted answers from the board and its outside counsel within 10 days, and that the board should look outside the company for its next chief executive (Axios; NTD). Nvidia has not responded publicly that we could find. No regulator has made a finding. The Justice Department said last week it had reached “no conclusions,” and the Justice and Commerce inquiries into a Singapore buyer continue.

The comments land two days before Nvidia’s third-quarter report, the first under interim chief executive Colette Kress, who also remains chief financial officer. Board chair Harvey Jones has said the search will consider internal and external candidates. Consensus is about $109 billion in revenue and $2.47–2.48 a share. The company’s own guide was about $108 billion, plus or minus 2%, with a gross margin near 74% (FXStreet/Saxo; Motley Fool; company guidance). The options market has been pricing a move of roughly 9–11% either way.

Why it matters A cabinet official publicly questioning the quality of Nvidia’s revenue adds a new risk: that some sales could be restated or that export enforcement tightens. That matters beyond Nvidia, because its numbers anchor estimates for TSMC, the memory makers and the data-centre builders.
What’s next Nvidia’s results Tuesday, and whether management addresses the China-channel question on the call. Senate Banking has asked Huang and Commerce Secretary Lutnick to appear on Dec 3.

China misses on every count, and Japan’s economy stalls

October retail sales grew 0.4% and investment kept shrinking. Japan’s third-quarter output contracted for the first time in four quarters.

China’s National Bureau of Statistics reported October retail sales up 0.4% from a year earlier, against forecasts of 0.8% and September’s 1.1%. Industrial production rose 4.8%, against 5.3% expected and 5.5% in September. Fixed-asset investment fell 8.1% in January–October from a year earlier, against a forecast fall of 7.0%. Private investment fell 11.4% and property investment more than 21% (CNBC, FXStreet, investingLive). CNBC described the investment decline as without precedent outside the pandemic. Copper futures fell 0.7% to $6.04 a pound, silver 2.7%, and European basic-resources stocks about 1.5%.

Japan’s first estimate of third-quarter GDP showed output flat quarter on quarter, down 0.1% at an annualized rate, against a Reuters poll of +0.3%. Household spending fell 0.3%, its second straight decline. Net exports subtracted 0.6 percentage points as imports rose 2.5%. Business investment rose 1.6% (Euronews, MLQ). Economists quoted in the coverage still expect the Bank of Japan to raise rates in December. A second GDP estimate is due Dec 8.

Why it matters Weak Chinese demand days before President Trump meets Xi Jinping in Shenzhen gives Beijing a reason to want calm on trade. A flat Japanese economy makes a Bank of Japan hike harder to justify, and a hike is the main thing that would ease pressure on the yen at 160.

Vulcan fails on its return to flight, destroying 40 Amazon Leo satellites

It is ULA’s first total mission loss since the company was formed in 2006. National-security launches on the rocket will now wait longer.

United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan, flying for the first time with six solid boosters, lifted off from Cape Canaveral on Sunday, Nov 15. About ten minutes into flight it failed to reach orbit, and all 40 Amazon Leo broadband satellites aboard were lost (SpaceNews, Spaceflight Now, CBS News, GeekWire). Which part failed is not settled. ULA has said only that the problem came during the Centaur upper stage’s burn. Other accounts based on outside tracking say the first stage burned longer than planned and separated late, and Ars Technica’s sources, as relayed by other outlets, point to the booster stage. Northrop Grumman says its solid boosters worked and separated before the anomaly. Reuters, citing one person familiar with the matter, reports that Blue Origin, which builds Vulcan’s main engines, is helping ULA investigate. We found no FAA statement yet.

Amazon said ULA has been “a trusted partner” and that it expects ULA to “learn from this mission.” Before Sunday, Amazon had 398 Leo satellites in orbit, about 12% of a planned network of about 3,230 (Reuters, as cited). Under a conditional FCC waiver it must have the network deployed by July 2029. Its other heavy rocket, Blue Origin’s New Glenn, is not due to fly again until January (Reuters, Nov 5).

Why it matters Amazon’s satellite plan, its main challenge to SpaceX’s Starlink, now depends more heavily on rockets that are grounded or delayed. The Space Force has kept Vulcan off national-security missions since a February booster anomaly, and recertification was waiting on this flight. For investors the names to watch are AMZN, SPCX (SpaceX), Rocket Lab (RKLB) and Northrop (NOC); being affected does not tell you which way they move.

Investigators trace Friday’s flight stoppage to a new network router

The FAA says a contractor-installed router flooded its telecom network. The router’s maker says the device did not malfunction.

Officials say a router installed by contractor L3Harris during a copper-to-fiber upgrade flooded the FAA’s telecommunications network with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. That knocked out air-traffic facilities and forced Friday’s nationwide ground stop, from about 3:30 to 8:15 PM, which hit 17 of 22 en-route centres. The FAA has switched off the same function on about 60 similar routers and found no sign of a cyberattack. Whether the cause was faulty equipment, a configuration error or a person’s mistake is not yet known. HPE’s Juniper unit, which made the router, says it “did not fail or act improperly” (IBTimes). Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said on four Sunday shows that L3Harris “didn’t meet the moment” and blamed outdated systems he inherited (CNN). The disruption counts vary by outlet, but one tally put cancellations at more than 12,000 over two days.

CNN, CNBC, Scripps, Houston Chronicle, Travel Weekly (multiple) · HPE/Juniper statement via IBTimes (single).

Science

Electrons in graphene that should not jump, do. Researchers at Forschungszentrum Jülich report in Nature (vol. 659, p. 63) that electrons in graphene cross energy gaps that standard selection rules say are “forbidden,” some with transition probabilities near 100%. If it holds up, the result matters for ultrafast optoelectronics that switch light with electrons, because it widens what designers can make the material do. It is a single peer-reviewed study and has not yet been replicated. The same issue of Nature also carries work on programmable neutral-atom quantum simulators and on perovskite-silicon tandem solar cells.

Why lava fountains get so high. A University of Manchester team reports in Nature Communications that magma heated above its normal eruption temperature may drive the tallest lava fountains. That could improve hazard estimates at volcanoes such as Etna and Kīlauea. It is a modelling and petrology study, not a field confirmation.

Space

After Vulcan. ULA’s failure (Top Stories) means SpaceX’s Falcon rockets will keep carrying national-security missions on their own for longer, since Vulcan had been kept off them since February (as reported). Outlets expect Vulcan to be out of service for months. SpaceX flies two Falcon 9 national-security missions from Vandenberg this week: USSF-317, about 10 PM ET Wednesday, and NROL-136 on Saturday morning (Launch Library).

Starship’s catch. SpaceX’s Ship 42 was caught by the launch tower on Saturday after about 22.5 hours and 14 orbits, the first catch of an upper stage returning from orbit. It had deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites. Booster 22 cut off early on low propellant, aborted its catch and was lost offshore; Elon Musk blamed slight engine underperformance (NASASpaceFlight). A ship that can be caught and reflown is the core of SpaceX’s cost advantage over every other launch provider, including the one that just lost Amazon’s satellites. SpaceX shares (SPCX) closed Friday at $139.11, 6.6% below their 50-day average.

Elsewhere. Blue Origin’s New Glenn will not return to flight before January, after rebuilding its pad following a May hotfire accident (Reuters). Its next payload is also Amazon Leo. Rocket Lab raised the price of a Neutron launch to $70 million from $55 million and has 16 missions on the books, with a first flight no earlier than 2027 (company release). China plans launches on Nov 18 and around Nov 20 to build out its satellite internet constellations (Launch Library, tentative).

Also in the News

The U.S. loses its measles-elimination status. The Pan American Health Organization concluded at a closed meeting on Friday that measles has spread continuously in the United States for more than 12 months, ending the elimination status the country has held since 2000. The Wall Street Journal reported it first, and The Washington Post, CNN and CBS confirmed it; some outlets say a formal announcement is still to come. One report counts 5,412 cases this year, the most since 1991, including a long-running outbreak in Utah, and Maricopa County, Arizona, reports cases at a 30-year high. CDC scientists allege that reports were “intentionally shelved,” and the Department of Health and Human Services calls that false (IBTimes, single source). Sen. Bill Cassidy discussed the decision on CBS’s Face the Nation.

Pennsylvania

Counties finish the election audit this week, and the legislature’s session ends Nov 30

The Department of State began a statewide risk-limiting audit of the governor’s race on Nov 13, a statistical hand-count check of the machine tally. Counties must finish it by Thursday, Nov 19, and certify their returns by Monday, Nov 23 (Department of State). Unofficial results show Gov. Josh Shapiro re-elected over Treasurer Stacy Garrity, with Democrats taking the state Senate about 26–24 and keeping the House, their first full control of Harrisburg since 1993 (Spotlight PA, WHYY). The current session ends Nov 30, and every unpassed bill dies with it. Recreational cannabis, a ban on cash gifts to officials, skill-game regulation and repeal of data-centre tax breaks are among the bills KYW reports are not expected to pass. On utility bills, the PUC must rule by Dec 26 on Peoples Natural Gas’s $163.2 million rate request; judges’ recommendations are in the docket. Duquesne Light’s default electricity price, now 14.14 cents per kWh, resets Dec 1, and the new figure has not been published.
