1 · Refresher: the three statements, aimed at your flagged items

Module 1 was taught in Lesson 7; these are the points behind your Needs review marks.

Depreciation spreads a cost already paid. Method, life and residual value change only when the expense appears, never the cash. Double-declining front-loads it, with residual value kept out of its base (pp.46–49). OCI holds equity changes that skip net income: currency translation, hedges, securities at fair value through OCI, some pension items. Find it as the gap in the equity roll-forward (pp.54–56).

Balance sheet. Inventory sits at the lower of cost and net realisable value; IFRS lets a write-down reverse, US GAAP does not, and only US GAAP allows LIFO. A prepaid is cash paid for a future benefit, expensed as used. Deferred revenue is cash received before delivery, and it is settled by delivering, not by paying. Accrued expenses are costs incurred but not yet paid (pp.73–78).

Cash flow buckets go by purpose. Operating covers customers, suppliers, staff, taxes and trading securities. Investing covers long-term assets. Financing covers owners and lenders. US GAAP: interest paid and received and dividends received are operating, dividends paid are financing. IFRS lets each go either way (pp.96–98). A bond converted into shares moves no cash, so it goes in a note.

Profit is not cash. A growing firm ties up cash in receivables and inventory, so earnings can rise while cash falls (pp.277–280).

Clearing up

The indirect method rebuilds cash by undoing accrual entries. Start from net income. Add back depreciation: it lowered profit, but no cash left. Take out a gain on an asset sale, since the whole sale price shows under investing. Receivables up: some sales were not collected, so subtract. Payables up: some costs are unpaid, so add. Both methods give the same CFO, and only the operating section can be indirect (pp.99–101).

Memorize
  • CFO = NI + non-cash charges − gains + losses − Δ operating assets + Δ operating liabilities
  • OCI = ending equity − (opening equity + NI − dividends), with no share issues or buybacks
  • Straight-line = (cost − residual) ÷ life
  • US GAAP: dividends paid are financing; everything else on interest and dividends is operating

2 · Today: profit, shutdown, scale and the four market structures

When should a firm keep going at a loss, or quit? And how does the number of rivals change the price it can charge? Lesson 4 covered perfect competition and monopoly; today adds the cost decisions and the two structures in between.

Economic profit charges for the owners’ capital. Economic cost is accounting cost plus what the owners’ money could earn elsewhere at similar risk. So breakeven, where price equals average total cost (ATC), means a normal return, not zero accounting profit. Free entry pushes profit there; barriers let excess profit last (p.9).

Choose output with the margins, then read the price. Keep adding units while the next one brings in more than it costs: produce where marginal revenue equals a rising marginal cost. A price taker’s marginal revenue is the market price. A firm facing downward demand must cut its price to sell more, so its marginal revenue is below price, and the price is read off demand at that output, not off marginal cost (pp.6–8).

Ignore sunk costs when deciding to operate. A committed lease is owed either way. If revenue covers variable cost, every sale chips at the fixed bill, so operating loses less than closing. Short run: shut only if price falls below average variable cost (AVC). Long run every cost is avoidable, so exit if revenue falls short of total cost. Marginal cost cuts through the bottom of AVC first and then the bottom of ATC, so the shutdown point comes at a lower price than breakeven (pp.10–13).

Everyday picture

A food truck owes its parking fee all season. On a slow day it still opens if takings cover the fuel and the cook’s wages, because the fee is due either way.

Scale is a long-run choice. Each plant size has its own short-run cost curve, and the long-run average cost curve (LRAC) is their lower envelope. Falling LRAC means economies of scale (specialisation, better technology, bulk buying). Rising LRAC means diseconomies, mostly from coordination strain. Its lowest point is minimum efficient scale; smaller firms carry a cost handicap (pp.14–19).

Monopolistic competition: many sellers, differentiated products. Branding gives each firm sloping demand and short-run profit; entrants then take customers until profit is zero. Output stays below minimum-ATC output and prices above the competitive level, partly paying for advertising, in exchange for variety and innovation (pp.25–28).

Oligopoly: a few sellers watching each other. In the kinked demand model, rivals match a price cut but ignore a rise. Demand is elastic above the going price and inelastic below it. Marginal revenue has a gap, so costs can move without moving price. The model explains sticky prices, not where they settle. In Cournot, each firm picks output taking the rival’s as fixed; the result lies between competition and monopoly. A Nash equilibrium leaves no firm better off changing alone, yet joint profit is not maximised, which is why cartels tempt. Collusion is likelier with few firms, similar products and costs, and credible punishment. A dominant price leader tends to lose share as entrants arrive (pp.29–39).

Measuring structure. Elasticity is the best test but hard to estimate; concentration measures are easier proxies that ignore entry barriers (pp.40–44).

Shutdown point
minimum AVC: below it, closing loses less (p.11)
Breakeven point
total revenue equals total cost, which for a price taker is minimum ATC (pp.9, 11)
Minimum efficient scale
output at the lowest point of LRAC (p.17)

Profit = TR − TC = (P − ATC) × Q  ·  HHI = Σ si2

P, ATC
price and average total cost per unit, currency; Q is units per period (p.9)
si
firm i’s market share as a decimal; the HHI is 1 for a monopoly and 1/M for M equal firms (p.43)
CR(N)
sum of the N largest firms’ shares, in percent (p.42)
Cournot
with demand Q = a − P and equal constant marginal cost c, each of two firms makes (a − c)/3 (pp.32–33)
Assumptions and exceptions
  • The shutdown rule assumes fixed costs are truly sunk. Once a lease ends, the long-run test applies.
  • High concentration does not prove pricing power if entry is easy. A merger of two leaders barely moves CR but lifts the HHI sharply, because shares are squared.
Memorize
  • Short run: shut if P < AVC. Long run: exit if TR < TC
  • Breakeven: P = ATC (normal profit, not zero accounting profit)
  • Market power: MR < P; price comes from demand at the MR = MC output
  • Cournot is simultaneous; Stackelberg is sequential
  • HHI in decimals: four equal firms = 0.25
