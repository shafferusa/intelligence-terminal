Cape Town Set a Date for Its Taps to Run Dry. Then the Date Kept Moving.

Monday, November 2, 2026 · 13 min read

A stock changes only through its flows and holds steady only when inflow equals outflow — which is why a city could name the day its reservoirs would run dry months in advance, and why modest cuts in water use kept pushing that day back by months at a time.

Systems Thinking

Day 25 of 293 · Systems Thinking 1 of 12 · Stocks and flows: bathtub dynamics

A city counts down to Day Zero

In January 2018, after three dry winters, Cape Town’s leaders told the city’s roughly four million residents that the taps could run dry on a specific day. Officials called it “Day Zero”: the day the supply dams were projected to fall to 13.5% of capacity, the level at which the city planned to shut off most household plumbing and send residents to queue at collection points for a daily ration. That January the date stood at around 22 April; within days officials moved it forward again, to 12 April.

From 1 February 2018, households were restricted to 50 litres of water per person per day, as the city tried to pull its total draw down from the roughly 1.1–1.2 billion litres a day it had been drawing in 2015. As the cuts bit, the projected date did not creep forward by matching days. It leapt: by mid-February officials were naming June. By spring, citing a weekly dam decline that had slowed to half a percentage point, they pushed it to 9 July. By early April they said Day Zero probably would not arrive in 2018 at all, and moved their working estimate into 2019 — just as winter rains began refilling the dams, and the crisis ended without a single tap running dry.

How could officials name a specific day a city of four million would run out of water, months ahead of time — and why did a cut in daily use push the projected date back not by days, but by months?

The Big Idea

A stock is anything that accumulates and can be measured at an instant — the water sitting in a dam, the money in a bank account, the carbon dioxide in the sky. It changes only through its flows: an inflow adding to it and an outflow draining it, both measured per unit of time, like litres per day rather than litres. A stock keeps rising or falling for exactly as long as those two flows are unequal, and it stops only at the instant inflow equals outflow — a state worth its own name, dynamic equilibrium, because the stock holds still while water is still moving through it in both directions.

That sounds obvious stated plainly, and it is still routinely gotten wrong, because intuition tends to track the flow instead of the stock: if the inflow stops rising, people expect the level to stop rising too. It does not. A dam with no rain falling into it keeps falling for as long as anything is still being drawn out of it, no matter how small that draw has become — and a smaller draw does not hold the water level steady, it only slows the fall.

How It Works

Call the stock S and measure it at any moment t. Its rate of change is simply the difference between what is coming in and what is going out right now: dS/dt = inflow − outflow. That differential equation is not new vocabulary — it is Day 4’s derivative, the instantaneous rate of change, applied to an accumulating quantity instead of a position. Run the clock forward from a starting value S(0) and the stock at any later time is that starting value plus everything that flowed in minus everything that flowed out along the way: S(t) = S(0) + the integral, from 0 to t, of (inflow − outflow). That is Day 5’s integral doing exactly the job it was built for — accumulating a rate into a quantity — and the two equations say the same thing from opposite ends: the derivative tells you which way the stock is moving right now, the integral tells you where it has ended up.

Three consequences follow directly from that one relationship, and all three are where intuition goes wrong.

First, the stock’s direction depends only on the sign of inflow minus outflow, never on whether either flow happens to be rising or falling. A stock can be falling even while its outflow is shrinking, as long as outflow still exceeds inflow; it can be rising even while its inflow is shrinking, as long as inflow still exceeds outflow. People routinely expect a shrinking outflow to mean a stabilizing stock. It does not, until that outflow actually drops to match the inflow.

Second, a stock reaches dynamic equilibrium — holds a constant level — only at inflow = outflow, and that balance point can sit anywhere: full, empty, or in between. A bathtub with the tap open and the drain open can sit at a perfectly steady six inches of water for as long as the tap and the drain are matched, even though water is pouring in and draining out the entire time. Mistaking a steady level for a closed tap, or for no outflow at all, is common enough that researchers who study the error gave it a name: the bathtub heuristic — wrongly inferring the stock’s trend directly from a flow’s current level, as if the stock were tracking the flow one-for-one instead of accumulating the net difference between two flows over time.

Third — and this is the piece that explains Cape Town’s leaping dates rather than creeping ones — when net outflow (outflow minus inflow) holds roughly steady, the time left until a stock empties is not proportional to the cut in outflow; it is time to empty = S / net outflow, the stock divided by the net rate draining it. Halving a steady net outflow does not buy twice as many days before the old deadline arrives; it buys a deadline that sits roughly twice as far from today as the one you started with. Imagine a reservoir holding 100 units with no rain falling into it (inflow near zero) and a city drawing down 10 units a day: at that rate, the reservoir is 10 days from empty. Cut the draw to 5 units a day and the reservoir does not merely gain a few days — it is now 20 days from empty, double the runway, from the same cut in half. Cut it again to 2.5 units a day and the runway doubles again, to 40 days. Each proportional cut in outflow produces a proportional stretch in the time remaining, which is why a modest, fixed percentage reduction in water use can look, from the outside, like the deadline keeps leaping away rather than edging back.

Back to the story

Cape Town’s dams were the stock. Summer rainfall into them was close to zero, so the inflow side of the ledger was effectively off, and the whole system behaved like a reservoir draining on outflow alone — close to the exact case where time to empty = S / net outflow applies almost literally. When the city’s restrictions pulled total daily use down toward roughly half of 2015’s draw, the net outflow draining the dams fell by something close to that same fraction, and the time remaining before the dams hit 13.5% stretched out by roughly the same proportion — not by the number of days saved, but by a multiplying factor on however many days were left. That is why the date did not inch from 22 April to 23 April as cuts arrived; it leapt to June, then to July, as the usage cuts compounded. And it is also why the crisis ended the way it did: the inflow side of the ledger, switched off all summer, switched back on when winter rains finally arrived, pushing net outflow negative and the dams back up — not because the city’s restraint had somehow erased the deadline, but because the flow that had been near zero all along came back.

Key Concepts

Stock
Any quantity that accumulates and can be measured at a single instant — water in a dam, dollars in an account, carbon dioxide in the atmosphere.
Flow (inflow and outflow)
A rate that adds to or drains a stock, measured per unit of time; a stock changes only through its flows, never directly.
Net flow
Inflow minus outflow at a given moment; its sign, not its size or trend, determines whether the stock is rising or falling right now.
Dynamic equilibrium
A stock holding a constant level because inflow equals outflow, not because either flow has stopped; the level can sit anywhere, not only full or empty.
Bathtub heuristic (error)
The common mistake of reading a stock’s trend directly off a flow’s current level, instead of off the accumulated difference between the two flows over time.
Time to empty
Stock divided by a steady net outflow; it changes in proportion to the net outflow, so a modest cut in outflow can push the deadline away by a large multiple.

Example: The Atmosphere as a Bathtub

Atmospheric carbon dioxide runs on exactly the same accounting, and it is the case where the failure to read a bathtub correctly has been measured directly, in people who should know better. Fossil-fuel burning and land-use change are the inflow; the land and ocean together are the outflow, a planetary-scale carbon sink that currently absorbs a bit over half of each year’s emissions, leaving a little under half to accumulate in the air (the exact split varies year to year and is still actively studied, but “a bit over half absorbed” has held up across decades of measurement). The concentration is the stock, and like any stock it keeps rising for as long as inflow exceeds outflow — which it still does, even in a year when emissions themselves do not grow.

In a 2007 study published in Climatic Change, the systems researchers John Sterman and Linda Booth Sweeney gave a sample of roughly 200 MIT graduate students — most trained in science, engineering or economics, not people without quantitative background — a simple task: sketch what happens to atmospheric CO2 if annual emissions are held flat at roughly today’s rate instead of being cut toward zero. Most drew the concentration leveling off or falling, as though a steady inflow meant a steady or shrinking stock. It does not: the inflow has to drop below the outflow — below what the land and ocean can absorb — before the stock stops climbing, and it has to drop further than that before the stock actually falls. Holding emissions flat holds the tap flat; it does nothing to the drain, and the tub keeps filling.

1. Identify the bathtub. Stock S = atmospheric CO2 concentration. Inflow = annual emissions. Outflow = land and ocean absorption, currently a bit over half of emissions.

2. Write the stock’s rule. dS/dt = inflow − outflow. The concentration falls only when outflow exceeds inflow.

3. Test “hold emissions flat.” If inflow stays level while outflow is only a bit over half of it, inflow − outflow stays positive — dS/dt > 0 — so the stock keeps rising, just at a steady rather than accelerating pace.

4. Find the actual stabilizing condition. The concentration stops rising only once inflow falls to match outflow — which itself shifts as the sinks respond to a changing climate — not once emissions merely stop growing.

5. Read the implication. “Flat emissions” and “stable climate” are not the same claim; treating them as the same claim is the identical error Sterman and Booth Sweeney’s subjects made with a bathtub instead of a planet.

Why It Matters

Once you can see a system as a stock fed and drained by flows, you gain a specific defense against a specific error: mistaking a change in a flow’s trend for a change in the stock’s fate. A shrinking budget deficit does not mean a national debt is falling — it is still rising, just more slowly, for as long as the deficit (the flow) stays positive. A slowing rate of new admissions does not mean a hospital’s patient count has peaked, if admissions still outpace discharges. The question worth asking of any stock in the news is never “is the flow getting better,” but “has the flow crossed zero, or crossed the other flow it has to match.”

Settled. Stock-and-flow accounting is mass balance: a stock’s level at any time is fixed by its starting value and the history of its flows, and the bathtub heuristic error is well replicated across samples and decades of research.

Debated. How much of Cape Town’s avoided Day Zero to credit to household rationing, to cuts in agricultural and commercial allocations, or to the winter rains that arrived when they did is still argued locally; the flows themselves are not in dispute, only how to apportion the outcome among the people who changed them and the weather that did not ask permission.

Value judgment. Whether households should have absorbed the first and deepest cuts, ahead of agriculture and industry, is a question about fairness and priority that stock-and-flow arithmetic cannot answer on its own — it can only tell you how large a cut, wherever applied, buys how much time.

Connections

Back. Day 4 gave the derivative as the instantaneous rate of change; today’s dS/dt = inflow − outflow is that same tool pointed at an accumulating quantity instead of a moving position. Day 5 gave the integral as accumulation from a rate — the fundamental theorem of calculus linking the two — and today’s S(t) = S(0) + the running integral of net flow is that theorem doing the actual work of a bathtub, a dam or a national debt.

Across. Accounting — Logan’s own CFA ground — runs on the identical identity under a different name: the clean-surplus relation states that ending equity equals beginning equity plus net income minus dividends, which is exactly S(t) = S(0) + accumulated net flow, with equity as the stock and net income less dividends as the net flow. A balance sheet is a stock statement; an income statement is a flow statement; and the two are tied together by the same bathtub arithmetic a hydrologist uses on a dam.

Remember These
  • A stock changes only through its flows; nothing about it can change except by inflow adding or outflow draining.
  • A stock holds steady only when inflow equals outflow — dynamic equilibrium — not whenever a flow stops changing.
  • Time to empty is stock divided by net outflow, so a proportional cut in outflow buys a proportional stretch in time, not a matching number of extra days.
  • The bathtub heuristic error — reading a stock’s trend off a flow’s level instead of its accumulated net difference — shows up in trained scientists and engineers, not just casual intuition.
  • “The flow is improving” and “the stock has turned” are different claims; only the second one means the stock itself has stopped getting worse.
Apply It

The 1922 Colorado River Compact divided 7.5 million acre-feet a year to each of the river’s two basins based on flows measured in an unusually wet early-twentieth-century stretch, with Mexico later promised 1.5 million more by treaty, yet this century the river has averaged closer to 12–13 million acre-feet a year; using stock-and-flow reasoning, why would Lake Mead keep falling toward roughly a quarter of its capacity even in a year when the river’s flow was entirely ordinary by this century’s standards?
