Anthropic jumps 17% in its debut, but the S&P 500 falls 2% for a sixth straight loss

Thursday, November 19, 2026 · 33 min read

Anthropic opened at $111.50 and closed at $97.17, well above its $83 offer price, but it did not rescue the AI trade. An early 1.3% gain turned into a 2% loss in the S&P 500, the Dow fell 1,101 points, the VIX rose above 28, and after the bell Intuit fell 14% on a guidance cut.

The Brief

If you read nothing else

  • The tape. The S&P 500 fell 2.02% to 7,175.83, its sixth straight loss, after rising as much as 1.3% in the morning. The Nasdaq fell 2.25% to 23,879.20, the Dow 2.19% (1,101 points) to 49,097.06, its first close below 50,000 since June, and the Russell 2000 2.18%. All 11 sectors fell. The S&P is down 5.5% in a week and 8.5% from its 52-week high.
  • Anthropic. ANT first traded shortly before noon at $111.50, 34% above the $83 offer, peaked at $115.10, and then fell steadily to close at $97.17, up 17.1%, on about 847 million shares. The gain held, but the rest of the AI trade fell as it faded: AMD lost 7.7% and Nvidia 3.3%, to a new 52-week low.
  • Rates, the Fed and the Treasury. Treasuries rallied modestly. The 2-year fell to 4.76% and the 10-year to 5.04% (Treasury). The 10-year TIPS auction sold at a 2.903% real yield, reported as the highest at auction for that term since 2002. Jobless claims rose to 203,000 and the Philadelphia Fed survey fell to 6.3 from 20.9, its lowest since May. A December rate rise is still priced at roughly 90–97%.
  • Biggest risk. Volatility. The VIX jumped 15.8% to 28.44, and the 9-day measure, at 29.26, is above both it and the 3-month measure (27.03). An inverted VIX curve like this one means investors are paying most for protection over the next few days. Friday is the monthly options expiry.
  • After the bell. Intuit beat on the quarter but cut its full-year revenue guide and agreed to sell Mailchimp. Its shares fell about 14% after hours. Keysight rose about 3% on a strong outlook; Gap’s shares moved little on mixed reports.
  • Beyond markets. The United States, China and Russia released a four-point, non-binding “declaration toward peace” after the Shenzhen summit, with nothing on tariffs, chips or Ukraine. In Berlin, the U.S. envoys said Russia had accepted European participation in talks; Moscow has not confirmed that. Pennsylvania counties certify the November 3 election by Monday.

Top Stories

Anthropic closes 17% above its offer price, but its debut fails to rescue the AI trade

The largest IPO on record had a strong first day. The market around it had what was reported as its biggest intraday reversal in a year.

Anthropic’s Class A shares began trading on Nasdaq shortly before noon, after the exchange’s price-discovery auction. The first trade was at $111.50, 34% above the $83 offer price (Reuters, CNBC, CBS). The shares reached $115.10 in the first minutes and then drifted lower for most of the afternoon, touching $92.72 before closing at $97.17, up 17.07% (Yahoo Finance; CNBC, CNN and Forbes give the same close). About 847 million shares changed hands. CNBC compared that with 522 million on SpaceX’s first day in June; we found no second source for the comparison.

The value outlets put on the company at the close depends on the share count they use: about $1.27 trillion on basic shares outstanding (Forbes), and $1.75–1.78 trillion fully diluted (CNBC, CNN, Reuters). We found no after-hours move worth noting at press time.

Every buyer of the IPO made money on the day. But the debut came with the broad market moving the other way. Futures had been up 2% for the Nasdaq this morning on hopes for the listing. The S&P 500 was up 1.3% early, began to fade while ANT waited for its first trade, and closed down 2.02%. Reuters wrote that the listing “failed to revitalize faded enthusiasm over artificial intelligence, as an early spike in its share price ebbed.” A recap argued that investors sold existing tech holdings to buy ANT. That fits the timing but cannot be shown from the data, so we treat it as a market narrative, not a cause.

Why it matters A 34% opening premium that shrank to 17% by the close tells you buyers were willing to pay up, but not without limit. At $97 the company is valued at more than a trillion dollars on any count, before it has a quarter of results as a public company. If ANT closes below the $92.72 day low in the next few sessions, the debut will look like the top of the AI trade rather than its revival.
What’s next Friday is ANT’s second session and also the monthly options expiry. Research coverage from the underwriting banks normally begins about 25 days after the listing, in mid-December. No index provider has announced a fast-entry decision.

An early rally collapses; the Dow drops 1,101 points and the S&P falls for a sixth day

The S&P 500 swung from up 1.3% to down 2%, and more than 90% of its stocks fell.

The S&P 500 opened higher, reached 7,415.64 in the first hour, then fell to close at 7,175.83, its low for the day (Yahoo Finance). From top to bottom that is a 3.2% swing. Dow Jones Market Data called it the index’s largest intraday reversal since November 2025, as reported. The Dow closed down 1,101.11 points at 49,097.06, below 50,000 for the first time since June (CNBC), and CBS reported that more than nine in ten S&P stocks fell. AP counts six straight losses, the longest run since 2024.

The damage was broad. The equal-weight S&P fund (RSP) fell 1.76%, only slightly less than SPY’s 1.96%, and small caps fell as much as the big indices. The worst sectors were consumer discretionary (−2.79%) and communications (−2.67%); the mildest was energy (−0.65%). Chips led the losses among large stocks: AMD fell 7.72%, Marvell 4.86%, TSMC’s U.S. shares 2.90% and Nvidia 3.30%. Alphabet fell 3.46% and Amazon 3.87%. The few gainers were security software, where Palo Alto Networks rose 6.42% on its results, Okta 5.75% and CrowdStrike 2.71%, plus Intel (+1.92%) and Target (+0.92%).

The outlets we read offered no single catalyst. AP pointed to worries about the AI boom ending; Reuters to weeks of concern over valuations, AI infrastructure spending and Jensen Huang’s abrupt exit from Nvidia. The day’s weak factory survey (below) may have added to it. The safe-haven pattern was mixed: Treasuries rose, but gold fell to a 52-week low and bitcoin slipped, so investors sold almost everything except bonds and the dollar.

Why it matters The S&P 500 is now 2.3% below its 200-day average and 6.1% below its 50-day, with only bonds and the dollar holding up. A sixth straight loss with an inverted VIX curve is the profile of a market still looking for a floor. Six-day losing streaks usually end with a sharp bounce. That bounce is not a reason to think the decline has ended.

Intuit falls 14% after hours as it cuts its revenue outlook and sells Mailchimp

The quarter beat estimates. The full year did not, and a stock already down 54% from its high fell again.

Intuit reported fiscal first-quarter revenue of about $4.35–4.40 billion, up 12%, against about $4.31–4.39 billion expected, and adjusted earnings of $2.91 a share, up 56%, against about $2.75 (company release as reported by Benzinga; Finnhub consensus). It cut its full-year revenue guide to $23.08–23.37 billion, below the roughly $23.43 billion analysts expected. It agreed to sell Mailchimp to Bending Spoons for $3.31 billion in cash plus $368 million in stock, and raised its dividend 15% to $1.38. The shares closed the regular session at $310.02 and traded about 14% lower after hours, near $267 (Benzinga).

Intuit bought Mailchimp for about $12 billion in 2021, so the sale crystallises a large loss on that deal. The company has not said, in the coverage we found, how much of the guidance cut comes from removing Mailchimp’s revenue and how much from the core business. That split decides whether this is housekeeping or a warning.

Factory survey slumps and claims edge up, but rate-rise pricing barely moves

The Philadelphia Fed index fell to 6.3, its lowest since May, while prices paid jumped to 58.2.

The Philadelphia Fed’s November manufacturing index fell to 6.3 from 20.9, against forecasts near 20 (RTTNews, Admiral Markets). New orders turned slightly negative at −0.4, while the prices-paid gauge rose to 58.2 from 43.2. Together with Monday’s drop in the New York survey to 7.8 from 25.5, it is the second regional survey this week to show a sharp slowdown in activity alongside faster price rises.

Initial jobless claims rose 1,000 to 203,000 in the week to November 14, against about 197,000 expected; the prior week was revised to 202,000 (Labor Department via FRED). Continuing claims rose 70,000 to 1.763 million in the week to November 7, as reported. Claims remain low by history. But continuing claims rising faster than new ones means people who lose jobs are taking longer to find new ones.

Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said in prepared remarks to a Brazilian central bank conference that the AI investment boom is adding to inflation through demand now, with the productivity relief only coming “in the future” (Reuters via Kitco). He gave no view on rates. CME FedWatch readings for a quarter-point rise on December 9 ranged from about 91% to 97% through the day, and one reading put a half point at 9.3%, up from 6.5% on Wednesday. The spread reflects when each reading was taken.

Why it matters Weaker activity with faster price rises is the hardest mix for a central bank. Through these surveys the Fed is still being told that inflation is the problem, which is why a factory slump did not take a December rise off the table.

Walmart raises its outlook for a third time; the shares slip anyway

The raise only brought guidance up to where analysts already were. At about 46 times earnings, in line was not enough.

Walmart’s third-quarter revenue rose 5.2% to $188.8 billion, and adjusted earnings were $0.65 a share against $0.64 expected (company release as reported by CNBC). U.S. comparable sales excluding fuel rose 2.9%, in line, including a drag of about 1.25 points from a Medicare drug-pricing rule. U.S. e-commerce grew 23% and advertising 37%. It raised its full-year guide to sales growth of 4.9–5.2% at constant currency and adjusted earnings of $2.84–2.89. Analysts were already at about $2.88–2.89. The shares were up about 1% before the open and closed down 1.04% at $126.04 (Yahoo Finance).

Washington, Beijing and Moscow release a four-point declaration; trade is not in it

This morning only Chinese and Russian state media had described the text. All three governments have now published it.

The text, titled on the White House site as a U.S.–Russia–China heads-of-state declaration toward peace, was released by all three countries, The Hill reports. It covers reform of the United Nations, oil production and supply, the International Criminal Court, and a commitment to three-way dialogue on nuclear risk and non-proliferation. It is not binding and sets no date for further talks. ABC notes that neither Ukraine nor Iran is named. We could not open the White House page itself; the summary is from news reports.

Nothing on tariffs, chips, rare earths or the trade truce that expires January 10 was announced. President Trump is due to brief reporters on Friday on the way home (CNN, Fox). Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer called the trip a “historical embarrassment”; supporters of the trip point to the first three-way leaders’ meeting of its kind and the nuclear-risk pledge.

Why it matters For markets, the energy clause is the only item with a possible read-through, and it carries no numbers. Brent was about 2% lower late Thursday. The tariff and chip questions that matter for earnings remain open until the truce deadline.

The Economy

Today’s data are in Top Stories: claims of 203,000 (prior revised to 202,000; four-week average 197,750, as reported) and the Philadelphia Fed at 6.3 with prices paid of 58.2. Both are levels, not trends, and one week of claims is noise. But the regional surveys have now fallen sharply two weeks in a row, and the next readings are the S&P Global flash surveys and the Fed’s other regional surveys next week.

The Fed. A quarter-point rise on December 9 remains the base case in market pricing. Jefferson spoke of inflation pressure from AI investment and did not mention rates. Wednesday’s minutes showed “almost all” officials leaning toward more rises, and Governor Waller has floated a half point. The data that decide the size are November’s payrolls and consumer prices, both due before the meeting. The policy rate is 3.75–4.00%; SOFR was 3.90% on Wednesday (FRED).

Treasury. The $19 billion reopening of the 10-year TIPS sold at a 2.903% real yield, with a bid-to-cover of 2.43 (TreasuryDirect). Foreign and domestic investors bidding through dealers, known as indirect bidders, took about 69% of the competitive awards, direct bidders 25% and the dealers themselves 6%. Those shares point to solid end-user demand. Tipswatch reports that it is the highest real yield at a 10-year TIPS auction since July 2002 and puts the implied inflation breakeven near 2.15%. In other words, buyers are being paid a historically high return above inflation, and the market expects relatively little inflation. The Treasury announced next week’s auctions: $69 billion 2-year, $70 billion 5-year and $44 billion 7-year notes, a $28 billion 2-year floating-rate note, and 6-, 13-, 26- and 52-week bills. All will be squeezed into the short Thanksgiving week.

Rates and credit. The par curve on Thursday: 3-month 4.34%, 2-year 4.76% (−4 basis points), 5-year 4.86%, 10-year 5.04% (−3), 30-year 5.40% (−1). The 2s10s spread steepened a point to +28. High-yield spreads widened again to 3.47 percentage points on Wednesday (ICE BofA via FRED), their fourth straight widening. HYG closed 14 cents above its 52-week low.

Science

Sleep and the ageing brain. A study in Nature Aging from Laura Lewis’s lab at MIT, published last week, found that older adults show weaker slow waves of blood and cerebrospinal fluid flow during sleep. Those waves are thought to help clear waste from the brain. It is an association in imaging data, not proof that weaker flow causes decline, and the wider theory of how the brain clears waste is still debated. The lab’s next step is to test whether the flow can be strengthened.

Concussion. A Stanford Medicine team led by Jessica Towns fitted athletes in seven sports with motion-sensing mouthguards. They found that straight-line head acceleration predicted concussion better than rotational acceleration, with about 100 g carrying a 50% risk (Stanford, News-Medical). It reverses earlier work from the same group, and real impacts mix both kinds of motion, so expect argument before helmet standards change.

Space

Vulcan. ULA’s preliminary finding on Sunday’s failure, which destroyed 40 Amazon Leo satellites, is that the BE-4 engines lost thrust because the rocket did not supply them with enough fuel, not because of a fault in the engines themselves; the solid boosters performed normally (Spaceflight Now, Space.com). It is not a final root cause, and there is no return-to-flight date. Some headlines read it as clearing Blue Origin’s engine; that goes further than ULA did.

Tonight and the weekend. A Falcon 9 carrying the classified USSF-317 mission has a window opening at 9:46 PM from Vandenberg, after this edition closed, with an 11th flight for the booster. NROL-136 follows on Saturday, and an Ariane 64 is scheduled to carry 36 Amazon Leo satellites from Kourou on November 23 (Launch Library 2).
