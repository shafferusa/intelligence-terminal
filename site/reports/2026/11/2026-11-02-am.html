Top Stories

Oil falls more than 4% and WTI drops below $100 as Washington signals more time for Iran talks

The fall erases most of last week’s gain. What caused it is a report about the president’s intentions, not a change at sea: Iran still says the strait stays restricted until the US lifts its blockade.

At about 6:30 AM, January West Texas Intermediate crude was at $99.38 a barrel, down $4.74 (4.6%) from Friday’s settlement of $104.12, and January Brent at $108.46, down $4.97 (4.4%) from $113.43 (Yahoo Finance). WTI had closed Friday at a five-month high. The Associated Press, Reuters and The National all report a fall of about 4% and connect it to diplomacy with Iran.

Three developments came over the weekend. Axios reported that President Trump told Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu he wants to give diplomacy more time before any new strikes; Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the president prefers a diplomatic outcome and has not decided on strikes. Mr. Trump said the war would end “very soon, one way or the other.” And Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baghaei, said on Monday that “the ball is in America’s court,” that Iran’s demands had been passed through Qatari mediators and that a US delegation was expected in Doha.

Iran’s terms have not softened. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi says a plan worked out with Qatar could restore normal navigation within two days instead of seven: US measures on the first day, shipping on the second. As reported, Tehran wants the naval blockade of its ports ended, frozen assets released, crude sanctions waived and hostilities ended on all fronts. A senior Iranian official last week called an earlier Qatari draft “unacceptable.” At sea, the Revolutionary Guards claim two more tankers hit mines on an “unauthorized route”; US Central Command calls the claims false and says the main lanes are clear. No independent maritime authority has confirmed any mine strike.

Why it matters Oil above $100 has been the main source of this autumn’s inflation pressure. September’s PCE inflation was 3.6%, and that pressure is a big reason futures price a December Fed rate increase. A sustained fall would ease both. The risk runs both ways: several outlets report that the president will be shown strike options after Tuesday’s election, and Israel is pressing for them. Our call: front-month WTI settles below $101 today (60–75%), given it is about $99.4 more than seven hours before the 2:30 PM settlement and the news that moved it is diplomatic, not physical.

Treasury sets out its borrowing needs at 3 PM, two days before the refunding

The question is whether the Treasury keeps promising not to raise note and bond auction sizes for “at least the next several quarters.”

The Treasury will publish its marketable borrowing estimates for October–December and January–March at 3 PM. In August it estimated October–December privately held net marketable borrowing at $628 billion, assuming an end-December cash balance of $850 billion; for July–September it had estimated $739 billion (Treasury). Wednesday’s refunding statement sets coupon sizes for November through January and updates the buyback program, which the Treasury said it would revisit in November after doubling its long-end buybacks.

Most dealers expect coupon sizes to stay unchanged; JPMorgan expects no increase until about August 2027, according to one market preview. The 10-year Treasury yield fell about 35 basis points in October to 4.94% at Friday’s par close, its biggest monthly drop since December 2023, which makes long-term borrowing cheaper for the government going into the refunding.

Why it matters A larger borrowing estimate than August’s, or softer guidance on auction sizes, would put upward pressure on longer yields just as they have fallen. Our Sunday edition put the chance of larger coupon sizes or dropped guidance on Wednesday at 5–15%.

A judge bars federal pressure on election officials over noncitizen-voting claims; the government appeals

The order runs through Nov. 16 and covers every state. Separately, Los Angeles County election workers are due before a federal grand jury the day after the vote.

On Friday, US District Judge Trina Thompson in San Francisco issued a temporary restraining order, in a suit brought by election workers’ unions, barring the Justice Department and Homeland Security from pressuring officials in any state to segregate ballots, purge voter rolls or halt ballot processing on the basis of noncitizen-voting allegations. She found the tactics likely violate the Voting Rights Act’s ban on intimidation. The Justice Department appealed on Sunday, noting she ruled before it could respond; a hearing on the appeal is reported for Monday (CNN, Fox News).

In a separate California case, a Ninth Circuit panel last week denied, 2–1, the department’s emergency bid concerning about 20,000 ballots; the department has said it is likely to seek Supreme Court review, and we found no filing. A federal grand jury has subpoenaed the Los Angeles County registrar’s office, and at least two workers are summoned for Nov. 4. That is an investigation: nobody has been charged, and it is not public whether anyone is a target. The registrar, Dean Logan, calls the subpoenas intimidation; US Attorney Bill Essayli says officials who knowingly process noncitizens’ ballots risk legal jeopardy.

Binance reopens withdrawals and customers pull $3.6 billion on the first day

The exchange says it has covered more than 90% of the roughly $6.3 billion stolen from its own reserves. Its full proof of reserves is expected Tuesday.

Binance resumed withdrawals of bitcoin, ether and some stablecoins on Sunday, with other networks to follow in phases. Customers withdrew a net $3.64 billion on the first day (Reuters, CoinDesk). The company says it has replaced more than 90% of the stolen user assets from its user-protection fund, insurance-fund surplus and treasury; that is its own figure and has not been independently checked. About $3 billion of the stolen funds was frozen earlier, including more than $2.3 billion by the stablecoin issuers Tether and Circle. On Saturday the FBI said North Korean hackers it tracks as “TraderTraitor,” the group it blamed for last year’s $1.5 billion Bybit theft, carried out the attack and are converting the proceeds mainly into ether (NPR, AP, Bloomberg). Bitcoin is near $77,800, down 0.6% over 24 hours and well above last week’s low near $76,400.

Risks & Scenarios

Base case — a pause before the election (about 50–60%). Stocks hold a modest bounce, oil stays near $100 and yields drift, with most investors waiting for Tuesday night, Wednesday’s refunding and Friday’s jobs report. Our calls logged for grading: the Nasdaq Composite closes above 25,437.00 (60–72%), front-month WTI settles below $101 (60–75%), and the October ISM manufacturing index prints at or above 53.5 (55–70%), given September’s 54.5 and steady regional surveys.

Downside — the chip selling resumes (about 20–30%). The bounce fades by midday and semiconductors make new lows ahead of AMD on Tuesday. What would signal it: SMH below Friday’s $522.49 close, the VIX back above 20, and the high-yield spread above 3.4 points.

Science

Why crocodiles outlived the dinosaurs. A University College London-led study in Science Advances, based on more than 7,000 fossil records from over 400 species and climate models, finds the tropics stayed warm enough after the asteroid impact for crocodile relatives to survive there. The authors say climate is only part of the answer: body size, a semi-aquatic life, flexible diets and low energy needs probably mattered too.

Antimatter in a quantum trap. Physicists report in Physical Review Letters that they have held a single positron, the electron’s antimatter twin, in a controlled quantum state. The promise is more precise tests of whether matter and antimatter behave identically; those measurements have not yet been made.

Silicon qubits. RIKEN researchers report in Nature Physics about 99.9% control accuracy while running five silicon spin qubits at once. Silicon qubits could use existing chip factories; five qubits is still far from a useful machine.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

County voters decide tomorrow whether council may pay for staff and district offices

A question on Tuesday’s Allegheny County ballot would repeal the Home Rule Charter’s ban on using county money for council members’ personal staff, district offices and benefits. A yes vote allows council to fund them later; it does not spend anything now. A companion question to lift the cap on council’s own budget was reportedly withdrawn after criticism; we could not confirm that from the county. The vote comes as council takes up County Executive Sara Innamorato’s 2027 budget, about $1.25 billion in operating spending with no tax increase and no layoffs, but flat property-tax revenue that leaves most departments with less (WESA; Allegheny County Elections).

WESA; Allegheny County Elections Division ballot-question notice.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

U.S. Steel commits about $3 billion to a new rolling mill at Edgar Thomson, while Mon Valley towns lose tax revenue

U.S. Steel’s board has approved about $3 billion for a new hot strip mill at the Edgar Thomson works in Braddock, part of Nippon Steel’s $11 billion investment pledge. It would raise capacity from 2.2 million to 3.5 million tons a year, with first production in late 2029, after which the Irvin mill in West Mifflin would retire. There is no new money for Irvin or Clairton. Separately, under a property-tax agreement the company will pay “zero or very little” in 2026–27, and Mon Valley municipalities and school districts are cutting budgets to cope (Mon Valley Independent and PublicSource; Nikkei).

Mon Valley Independent / PublicSource; Nikkei via Mining Weekly.
