1 · Refresher: the tricky points from Modules 1–3

Demand equations. In quantity = 80 − 2×price + 0.5×income, each coefficient is a slope: units of quantity per one-unit change in that variable; the constant gathers everything held fixed. Slopes depend on units, elasticities compare percentages. So first solve for quantity, then elasticity = coefficient × that variable’s value ÷ quantity. Own-price: judge by size (above 1 is elastic). Income: positive is normal, negative inferior. Cross-price: positive substitutes, negative complements.

Cost curves. MC bottoms out first, then crosses AVC and ATC at their lowest points. ATC’s minimum comes later because fixed cost per unit keeps shrinking.

Firms. One wheat farmer cannot move the price, so it can sell any amount at it: its demand is flat and equals MR, while the market’s slopes down. Choose output where MR = MC, then read the price up on the demand curve. Economic profit draws entrants, supply shifts right, and profit shrinks to a normal return. Consumer surplus is willingness to pay minus price; an entry fee plus a per-use charge captures some of it. A natural monopoly’s costs keep falling with scale, so a regulator pricing at average cost covers costs plus a fair return on capital.

GDP. Count only new, final production (or value added); used goods, transfers and capital gains produce nothing now (pp.71–73). The deflator divides a year’s nominal GDP by that same year’s real GDP, at whatever base year the question names (pp.74–77). Compound across years: 100 to 121 in two years is 10% a year.

Saving and spending. In S = I + (G − T) + (X − M), positive X − M is a surplus (p.80). Falling house prices make existing owners poorer, so they spend less. Rate cuts in a slump signal weakness, not strength. Potential growth ≈ hours growth + productivity growth, with past periods weighted by their years: a measure of capacity, not a GDP forecast (pp.121–123). Lower inflation, lower yields, higher prices for existing bonds. Capital consumption allowance means depreciation.

Clearing up: reserves

Reserves are money a bank actually holds; the reserve requirement is the rule for the minimum share of deposits it must hold. Lending room is the gap. More reserves or a lower rule widens it and credit loosens; fewer reserves or a stricter rule narrows it. Module 5 shows the mechanism.

Memorize
  • Elasticity = coefficient × variable ÷ quantity
  • Annual rate over n years = (end ÷ start)1/n − 1

2 · Today: the business cycle, then money

Module 4 in brief (Lesson 3 has more). Durables swing most, services less, everyday goods least (pp.138–140). Housing swings hard because buyers borrow (p.141). Imports follow growth at home, exports growth abroad; a stronger currency raises imports and cuts exports (pp.142–144). Unemployment lags: firms use overtime and temps first, and discouraged workers leave the count (pp.146–148). Disinflation is slower rises; deflation is falling prices, painful because debts stay fixed (p.150). Fixed-basket indexes overstate inflation, which comes from costs or from demand outrunning capacity (pp.151–159).

Video recap: Business Cycles- Macro Topic 2.7 (Jacob Clifford, 5 min): the phases of the cycle and why recessions are hard to predict.

Why policy exists. Only government can push a whole economy. Monetary policy is the central bank steering money and credit; fiscal policy is taxing and spending. Both aim to smooth the cycle and keep inflation stable; tools come later (pp.171–172).

Money fixes barter, which needs a partner who wants exactly what you have. Money is a medium of exchange, a store of wealth and a unit of account; it must be accepted, of known value, divisible, compact and hard to fake (pp.173–174).

Banks create deposits by lending. A bank keeps a fraction of a deposit as reserves and lends the rest; the borrower spends, the seller deposits it elsewhere, and that bank repeats the step. Rounds shrink, and their total is set by the reserve requirement. The central bank starts the chain by buying securities or lending reserves (pp.174–176).

Multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve requirement · maximum deposits = new deposit ÷ reserve requirement

Reserve requirement
a decimal; 20% gives a multiplier of 5 (p.176)
Deposits
currency units, original included; a ceiling (p.175)

Narrow money is notes, coins and the most liquid deposits; broad money adds other liquid assets. A credit card only defers payment, so it is not money (p.177).

Quantity theory. M × V = P × Y holds by definition (V is how often each unit is spent). Assume steady velocity and neutrality (money cannot change real output long term) and more money means higher prices. Monetarists call money growth the cause of inflation; causation may also run the other way (p.178).

People hold money for transactions and precaution, both rising with income, and to speculate: less when other assets pay more, more when they look risky (pp.178–179).

The money market clears through bonds. The interest rate is the cost of holding money. Supply is fixed by the central bank, a vertical line; demand slopes down. Above equilibrium people hold surplus money, buy bonds, and yields fall; below it, the reverse. More money lowers rates now and raises prices later (pp.180–181).

Fisher effect: nominal rate = real rate + expected inflation

All three
percent per year, same horizon; the real rate is assumed stable (p.182)

Who inflation hurts. Expected inflation gets built into wages and loan rates, so it costs little. A surprise shifts wealth: a lender who priced in 3% and gets 9% is repaid in cheaper money, and the borrower gains. Uncertainty adds a lender premium and blurs price signals (pp.183–184).

Assumptions and exceptions
  • The multiplier is a maximum: excess reserves and cash held outside banks shrink it.
  • The identity alone proves no cause; Fisher keeps the real rate stable, not the nominal.
Memorize
  • Multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve requirement
  • M × V = P × Y · nominal = real + expected inflation
  • Rate above equilibrium: excess money, bonds bought, yields fall
  • Only unexpected inflation transfers wealth
