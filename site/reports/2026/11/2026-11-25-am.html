Top Stories

The AI sell-off resumes: Nasdaq futures down 2.7% after Korea and Taiwan tumble

Monday’s rally was almost entirely undone in Asia overnight. Several pressures arrived at once: chip-stock valuations, hardware margins, Dell’s reaction and a tariff report, with inflation data two hours before the open.

At 6:29 AM, December S&P 500 futures were at 7,090.25, down 1.76% from Tuesday’s settlement, and Nasdaq-100 futures at 25,563, down 2.71% (Yahoo Finance, delayed). Dow futures were down 1.08% and Russell 2000 futures 1.10%. The fall deepened through the night: Investing.com had S&P futures down 1.3% and Nasdaq futures 2.2% at 5 AM. Premarket, Nvidia was at $147.41 (−5.1%), AMD −6.4%, Micron −6.0%, TSMC’s U.S. shares −4.0% and Microsoft −2.5% (Yahoo Finance, about 6:40 AM). Futures are not a guaranteed open.

The fall began in Asia, where Tuesday’s record-sized chip rally reversed. Korea’s Kospi fell 6.16% to 4,697.51, and the Korea Exchange briefly halted programme trading after Kospi futures dropped more than 5% (AFP). Taiwan’s Taiex fell 4.80% to 38,195.43, with TSMC down 3.6% and Hon Hai 8.8% (Central News Agency, Al Jazeera). Japan’s Nikkei fell 2.78% to 57,011.62, with SoftBank reported down about 10%; Shanghai fell 2.03% and the Hang Seng 1.14% (Yahoo Finance closes). Outlets and dealers cited doubts about AI spending and the debt financing it, the fading of the rally that followed Jensen Huang’s return to Nvidia, and the tariff report. No single confirmed catalyst explains the size of the move; investors appeared to focus on all of them at once.

Tuesday had already shown the strain beneath the index. The S&P 500 fell 0.50% to 7,201.09 and the Nasdaq 1.48%, while the Dow rose 0.85% to 50,018.70; the semiconductor fund SMH fell 7.26%, Micron 11.0%, Dell 11.8% and HPE 16.3%, after HPE warned that memory costs would squeeze server margins. Below the surface, the average stock did better: in our whole-market count, 2,865 stocks rose and 2,515 fell, though more volume went into the decliners, and the equal-weight S&P fund RSP rose 0.54%. The share of stocks above their 50-day average rose to 29.8% from 26.8%.

Why it matters A two-day round trip of this size, up 4.7% then down again, is what an unsettled market looks like, and it keeps the S&P 500 below both its 50-day and 200-day averages. The difference from last week is the rotation: money left chips for retailers, banks and health care on Tuesday rather than leaving stocks altogether. Whether that holds today, with Nasdaq futures down twice as much as the Dow, is the test.
What’s next PCE, GDP, claims and durable goods at 8:30 AM; weekly oil inventories at 10:30; the 7-year auction at 1 PM; the Beige Book at 2 PM. Markets close Thursday for Thanksgiving and at 1 PM on Friday, so today is the last full session of the week.

Dell reports a record $132 billion AI backlog and raises its forecast, but its shares fall about 7%

The quarter answered the demand question emphatically. The market’s question has moved to what that demand earns, after HPE’s margin warning on Tuesday.

Dell’s third-quarter revenue was $54.94 billion, against about $49–50 billion expected, and adjusted earnings $9.45 a share against about $6.60. It took $60.2 billion of AI-server orders in the quarter and ended with a record AI backlog of $132.3 billion, up from about $95 billion three months earlier. It guided fourth-quarter revenue to $56.3 billion and adjusted earnings to $8.40 a share, raised its full-year revenue forecast to about $202 billion, and authorised a new $25 billion buyback (CNBC, The Next Web). One outlet reports that management expects the operating margin of its server and storage unit to fall in the fourth quarter; we could not confirm that elsewhere.

The shares rose 4–6% after hours, then reversed: at about 6:40 AM they were at $274.21, down 7.1% from Tuesday’s $295.28 close (Yahoo Finance), which itself was down 11.8% on the day. Newsquawk summarised it as “lower despite beat & raise.” The pattern matches HPE’s on Monday night: strong orders, then selling on margins.

Why it matters Dell is the clearest read on how much of the AI build-out reaches the companies that assemble the servers. A record backlog says the spending is still coming; the share price says investors now care more about memory and component costs, and how much of each dollar the assemblers keep, than about order books.

October PCE inflation, revised GDP and jobless claims arrive at 8:30, the Fed’s last big read before December 9

A quarter-point increase is all but priced. The data decide whether markets start to price a half-point, or a run of increases into the spring.

Forecasters expect the PCE price index, the Fed’s preferred inflation measure, to have risen 0.3% in October, or 3.8% from a year earlier after 3.6% in September, and core prices, which exclude food and energy, to have risen 0.3%, or about 3.2% from a year earlier after 3.1% (XTB, RBC, Dow Jones via CNBC). RBC forecasts core at 3.3%. Personal income and spending are each expected to have risen about 0.4%. In September, core PCE rose 0.32% from August on our calculation from the published index (BEA via FRED). The October CPI already showed core consumer prices up 0.30% on the month, and most of the CPI-to-PCE translation is known in advance, which is why forecasters are tightly grouped.

The second estimate of third-quarter GDP is expected to show annualised growth of about 3.5%, up from 3.4% in the advance estimate; Goldman Sachs expects 3.7%. Initial jobless claims for the week to November 21 are expected near 200,000, after 203,000 the week before, and continuing claims near 1.74 million. October durable-goods orders are due at the same time; we found a core forecast of +0.5% but no reliable headline consensus.

The Fed raised its target range to 3.75–4.00% in September. Futures price about a 97% chance of a quarter-point increase on December 9 (Investing.com, citing fed funds futures; we did not check CME directly). New York Fed President John Williams said on Monday that rates will likely rise “over time” but has been reported as saying “dramatic action” is not called for; St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem has said several more increases are likely before spring. The Fed’s pre-meeting blackout begins Saturday, so any official who wants to shape expectations has three days.

Why it matters Real growth near 3.5% with core inflation above 3% and claims near 200,000 is an economy the Fed does not need to protect, which is why it is tightening. A 0.4% core reading would make a half-point move, which Governor Waller has floated, a live debate; a 0.2% reading would ease pressure on the long end and on the rate-sensitive growth stocks that are falling this morning.

Reuters: the administration may announce new tariffs on 16 trading partners as soon as this week

The list covers most of America’s largest suppliers. No rates, product lists or official notice have been published.

The administration may unveil new duties under Section 301 of the Trade Act, which allows tariffs against unfair foreign practices, on 16 trading partners as soon as Tuesday or Wednesday, following an investigation into excess industrial capacity launched in March, Reuters reported (via Korea Times and others). The 16 are China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, India, Mexico, Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Cambodia, Bangladesh, Singapore, Switzerland and Norway. The duties would partly replace the tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February and come on top of the forced-labour duties of 10–12.5% imposed in July on 60 partners. One former Commerce official speculated about rates of 15–25%; that is a guess, not reporting. We found no notice from the U.S. Trade Representative or in the Federal Register.

Why it matters Korea and Taiwan, whose markets fell most overnight, are both on the list, and dealers there cited it. For the Fed, tariffs are another upward push on goods prices just as it is raising rates against inflation above 3%; for companies such as Dell and HP, which assemble hardware from Asian components, they are another cost on margins already in question.

Reuters via Korea Times, TBS News (as reported; one report) · no official document found. The timing and rates are unconfirmed.

Nvidia falls again as Treasury’s deadline for its board arrives; Trump says a pardon for Huang is “always an option”

The stock has given back most of Monday’s 31% jump. The questions about chips reaching China have not been answered in public.

Nvidia closed Tuesday down 5.86% at $155.38 and was at $147.41 before the bell, down another 5.1% (Yahoo Finance), against Monday’s $165.05 close and a 52-week low of $124.50 on November 20. Treasury’s ten-day request for answers from Nvidia’s board and outside counsel about how its chips reached China through shell companies runs out today (Politico, as reported); some outlets note it is a requested window, not a formal deadline with a penalty. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Sunday that no answers had yet arrived but that Nvidia’s committees were “moving very quickly.” We found no response from the company.

In an interview with the New York Post, President Trump said a pardon for Huang is “always an option” but that he does not think it will be necessary, that Huang should not need to invoke the Fifth Amendment at his closed-door House interviews next week, and that a government stake in Nvidia is “probably not too late” (CNBC, NBC News, Forbes). Huang is scheduled for interviews with three House committees on December 1–2 and as the sole witness at a Senate Banking hearing on December 3. Reuters reported on November 20, citing two sources, that the SEC’s inquiry concerns possible accounting issues linked to Nvidia’s 2025 change in how it reports revenue by region and to AI financing deals; the SEC has not commented, and we found nothing new on it.

Iran’s mine claims remain unconfirmed as oil stays near its lows before OPEC+ meets

The strait is effectively shut to normal traffic, yet the market is pricing a surplus.

Shipping trackers describe the Strait of Hormuz as effectively closed to commercial traffic, and the Joint Maritime Information Center rates the threat as severe. Iran’s navy and Revolutionary Guards, via Press TV, now claim that several tankers, including a “fourth,” hit naval mines on November 23–24; the counts are inconsistent, no ships have been named and no independent source has confirmed any of it. The last independently reported strike was a tanker hit by an unknown projectile on November 22 (UKMTO). The Treasury on Monday sanctioned more than two dozen Iranian exchange houses and front companies in Iran, Dubai, Hong Kong and Turkey, saying it had identified about $14.7 billion of possibly linked transactions (JNS, ANI; the Treasury release itself was not retrieved).

Brent was about $88 at 6:29 AM and U.S. crude about $81.40 (Yahoo Finance, delayed); Reuters, a few minutes later, had Brent at $90.96 and U.S. crude at $81.43, so different contract months are probably being quoted, and we do not read a direction into Brent this morning. U.S. crude is down about a third from its 52-week high. Analysts cite Libya’s restarted Sharara field, the IEA’s estimate of a 1.7 million barrel-a-day surplus this quarter, and OPEC+’s meeting on November 29 on 2027 output, where the UAE and Iraq want higher quotas. The industry group API reported a 126,000-barrel crude build last week; the government’s figures come at 10:30 AM.

The United States

Funding. Congress has left for Thanksgiving with the government funded only through December 11 and no agreement. The House has passed three of the twelve full-year spending bills and the Senate none, and appropriators expect another stopgap (The Hill). The sticking points are Democratic demands to restrict money for the Iran war, which Majority Leader John Thune has called “not a realistic ask,” and the president’s request for about $350 billion in new Pentagon spending; a Senate bill needs at least seven Democratic votes. Thune says Democrats have told him they will not negotiate until their leadership contest is settled. Congress returns November 30.

Senate Democrats choose a leader on December 1. Sen. Chris Van Hollen is challenging Chuck Schumer in a secret ballot, the first contested Senate Democratic leadership race since 1994 (AP, The Hill). After the midterms, the winner is expected to become majority leader in the next Congress. A Washington Post count as of November 20 had 16 senators publicly backing Schumer, 6 Van Hollen, 3 more not backing Schumer and about 30 undeclared. Supporters of a change argue the party needs new leadership; Schumer’s backers, including Sen. Brian Schatz, cite his record of holding the caucus together.

Homelessness funding. Federal Judge Mary McElroy in Rhode Island has vacated HUD’s plan to steer $1.3 billion of its roughly $4 billion Continuum of Care program to transitional and services-only projects, ruling that HUD exceeded its statutory authority, and found that a scoring rule rewarding cooperation with encampment clearing violates the First Amendment; some incentives, such as points for sober housing, survived (NPR, Philadelphia Inquirer). HUD Secretary Scott Turner said the ruling “disrupts $4 billion” days before awards. No appeal has been announced.

Memorial arch. Federal Judge Leonie Brinkema blocked construction of the planned Trump memorial arch near Arlington on November 20, finding the challenge likely to succeed; the Justice Department said it would appeal to the Fourth Circuit at once (NPR, Axios, AP). A separate pause by Judge Tanya Chutkan in Washington runs to December 3.

Immigration. DHS reported 483,200 deportations and more than 491,000 ICE arrests in fiscal 2026, a record (Washington Post). Massachusetts has sued ICE over arrests at state courthouses (as reported by immigration-lawyer news clips; we did not see the filing). We found no new Supreme Court orders on Monday or Tuesday.

AI & Technology

Safety worries inside the labs. Axios reports, citing more than two dozen people it did not name, that some employees at Anthropic and OpenAI fear that models they have not released are too risky to release, and that there has been internal debate about keeping models internal or pausing training. An Anthropic spokesperson said keeping a model internal does not by itself make it safe and that the company may slow or hold back releases; OpenAI pointed to new safety requirements that could slow its releases. It is one outlet’s reporting on anonymous sources, since widely repeated. Market read-through: Anthropic, now public, fell 6.5% on Tuesday and is lower again this morning; we cannot say how much of that is this report.

Chips and policy. Huang’s return is adding momentum to export-control bills in Congress, including the Chip Security Act, which would require location checks on advanced chips (The Hill). Bessent said last week that no further curbs are needed on older Nvidia chips sold to China after the administration cut off China’s remote access to top chips, and Nvidia says it has stopped H200 shipments to China (PBS). President Trump’s statement last week that U.S. companies would not be allowed to build AI data centres abroad still has no order or legal mechanism behind it (CNBC). Market read-through: hyperscalers with large overseas builds (Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta) carry the policy risk if it becomes real.

Cyber. Radware disclosed that attackers used an exposed internal management interface for its cloud firewall service to reach customer account details and cloud access keys; it has rotated credentials (BleepingComputer, one report).

Science

A machine-checked claim on the Riemann Hypothesis. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced on November 20 a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, formalised in the Lean proof assistant and produced with the company’s Claude models. MIT’s Andrew Sutherland reports that the code compiles, uses only standard axioms and is accepted by an independent checker, with some checks still pending; James Maynard told Nature he suspects the proof will turn out to be correct (Nature news, Al Jazeera). It is a claim, not yet an accepted result: the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as open, and verifying that the formal statement matches the hypothesis exactly is part of what remains. If it holds, it would be the first of the Millennium Prize problems settled with AI.

Shorter tuberculosis treatment. A phase 2b trial in the New England Journal of Medicine, run by the UNITE4TB consortium, identified two 16-week regimens built around the experimental drugs ganfeborole and BTZ-043 as candidates to shorten treatment for lung TB, which usually takes six months. It is a mid-stage trial; phase 3 results are needed before any change in care.

Space

Ariane 6 delivers for Amazon. Tuesday’s Ariane 64 deployed all 36 Amazon Leo satellites at about 460 kilometres; they will raise themselves to about 630 (Spaceflight Now, Amazon). It was Ariane 6’s tenth flight, and Amazon’s first launch since ULA’s Vulcan failure on November 15 destroyed about 40 of its satellites. Amazon says it has more than 400 in orbit. Vulcan remains grounded while the investigation, which points to an inadequate fuel supply to its BE-4 engines rather than the engines themselves, continues.

China. Shenzhou-24 launched on Tuesday evening, Eastern time, and docked with the Tiangong station, carrying Tang Hongbo, Wu Yi and Pakistan’s Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, the first foreign astronaut in China’s programme (Launch Library 2; docking per Korean broadcaster SBS, not yet confirmed by China’s space agency in what we saw).

Also. A Falcon 9 launched the classified USSF-266 mission from Vandenberg on Tuesday at 11:08 AM Eastern, its booster landing on its 29th flight (SpaceX). Rocket Lab’s Electron is scheduled to fly a StriX radar satellite for Synspective on Thursday at about 7:05 PM Eastern, and a Falcon 9 carries a Cygnus cargo ship to the space station on Sunday (Launch Library 2). Space stocks fell with tech on Tuesday: Rocket Lab −7.4%, AST SpaceMobile −8.1%, Intuitive Machines −8.4%.

Pennsylvania

Electricity prices rise December 1; Duquesne Light’s default rate goes up about 3%

Duquesne Light’s residential “price to compare,” the default supply rate for customers who have not chosen a supplier, rises to 14.56 cents a kilowatt-hour from 14.14 on December 1, about $2.49 a month on a typical 600-kilowatt-hour bill; the utility cites higher demand, including from data centres (WESA). West Penn Power and Penn Power also change their rates, and PECO and PPL customers face larger increases, reported at about 10%. The Public Utility Commission is urging customers to compare suppliers on PAPowerSwitch and to check assistance programmes before winter.

In Harrisburg, Democrats will hold the governorship and both chambers next year for the first time since 1993. Allegheny County’s Sen. Jay Costa is to become president pro tempore and Maria Collett majority leader, while Joanna McClinton and Matt Bradford remain the House’s top two leaders (Spotlight PA). The current session ends November 30 with little floor action expected.

Pittsburgh · Allegheny County

County budget goes to the full council; the city debates reserves and pensions

Allegheny County Council’s budget committee advanced the roughly $3 billion 2027 budget with five amendments worth about $2.9 million, including more than $1 million for staff in the district attorney’s and public defender’s offices and $1 million of opioid-settlement money for EMS agencies; the property-tax rate is unchanged, and the full council must vote by December 6 (TribLive, WESA). WESA put the justice-office money at $1.37 million, so the breakdown differs by outlet.

In the city, Mayor Corey O’Connor’s $872.6 million plan has no tax increase or layoffs but draws about $28 million from reserves, reduces pension contributions by $10 million over five years and ends transfers to the Housing Opportunity Fund; Controller Rachael Heisler has questioned the pension cut, and the city projects deficits through 2031 (WESA, PublicSource). Council must adopt a budget by year-end.
