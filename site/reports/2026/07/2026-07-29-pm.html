Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 3/71 · Dimensional analysis (continued)

This morning introduced dimensional analysis as tracking the physical dimensions — length, mass, time — that a quantity is built from, independent of specific units. The core working rule is dimensional homogeneity: every term added together in a valid physical equation must carry the same dimensions, and both sides of an equation must match. Written compactly, if [v] denotes "the dimension of velocity," then [v] = [L][T]⁻¹ (length over time), and any equation claiming to compute a velocity must reduce to exactly that combination once every constant's dimensions are tracked through.

A concrete tie-in: dimensional analysis can catch a wrong formula, but it cannot supply the numeric constants inside a correct one. The kinetic-energy formula, KE = ½mv², has dimensions [M][L]²[T]⁻² on both sides once you check it — but dimensional analysis alone would be equally satisfied by KE = mv² or KE = 3mv², since the dimensionless prefactor doesn't affect the dimensional bookkeeping. That is the common misconception worth correcting: dimensional analysis is a powerful error-catching sanity check, not a derivation engine — it tells you an equation is dimensionally plausible, never that it is numerically correct.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 3/75 · Thrust (continued)

This morning introduced thrust as mass flow rate multiplied by exhaust velocity. Written out, the simplified thrust equation is F = ṁv_e, where ṁ (m-dot) is the propellant mass flow rate in kilograms per second and v_e is the effective exhaust velocity. A more complete version adds a pressure term for the nozzle exit: F = ṁv_e + (p_e − p_0)A_e, where p_e is the exit pressure, p_0 is ambient (atmospheric) pressure, and A_e is the nozzle exit area — which is why the same engine produces more thrust in the vacuum of space (where p_0 = 0) than at sea level.

Real-world tie-in: today's Long March 7A launch (confirmed successful, Tianlian 3-01 payload) and tonight's scheduled Long March 6A both rely on exactly this relationship scaled up to orbital-class engines. The common misconception: a "more powerful" engine (higher thrust) isn't necessarily a "more efficient" one. Thrust tells you how hard an engine pushes right now; specific impulse (a later topic in this sequence) tells you how efficiently it converts propellant into that push. A large first-stage engine is optimized for high thrust to lift off the pad against gravity, while an upper-stage engine is often optimized for high efficiency over a longer burn — different design goals, not a simple better-or-worse ranking.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 3/118 · k-th Central Moment / Expected Value (continued)

This morning introduced expected value as the probability-weighted average, and central moments as measures of spread and shape around that mean: E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx, with the k-th central moment μ_k = E[(X − μ)^k]. The second central moment (k=2) is variance, the probability-weighted average squared deviation from the mean; its square root is the standard deviation, the everyday measure of how spread out returns are.

Today's actual market data is a clean real-world tie-in: the VIX — itself a market-implied estimate of the standard deviation (annualized) of S&P 500 returns — jumped 10.10% today to 20.05 as the S&P fell 1.51%. A one-day move of that size sits well outside a "typical" day for an index whose annualized volatility was priced around 18–19% coming in, illustrating variance/standard deviation in action: today was a multi-standard-deviation event relative to recent history, not an ordinary fluctuation. The common misconception: mean and variance alone fully describe a return distribution. They don't — skewness (the third central moment, asymmetry) and kurtosis (the fourth, tail thickness) matter enormously for risk, especially on days like today. Equity returns are well known to have negative skew and excess kurtosis ("fat tails") versus a normal distribution, meaning large down-moves like today's are more common, and cluster more, than a mean-and-variance-only model would predict.
