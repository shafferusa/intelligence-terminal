Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 2/71 · Units

Every physical quantity is a number paired with a unit, and the unit is not decoration — it is half the information. Saying an object’s speed is “60” means nothing until you know whether that’s 60 miles per hour, 60 meters per second, or 60 furlongs per fortnight; the same number describes wildly different physical situations depending on the unit attached to it. Physics mostly works in the SI (International System) base units: the meter for length, the kilogram for mass, the second for time, and a handful of others (the ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela) that combine to build every other unit you’ll encounter — a newton of force, for instance, is just kilogram-meters-per-second-squared in disguise.

This builds directly on yesterday’s topic, scientific measurement: a measurement is only meaningful once it carries both a number and a unit, and yesterday’s discussion of precision and significant figures only makes sense once you’re tracking a specific unit consistently. A number without its unit is not a smaller piece of information — it is not physics at all, just arithmetic.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 2/75 · Newton’s Laws Applied to Rockets

A rocket flies for the same reason a fired gun kicks back into your shoulder: Newton’s third law, every action has an equal and opposite reaction. A rocket engine violently accelerates propellant mass out of the nozzle in one direction; by that same law, the vehicle itself is pushed in the opposite direction with equal force. Crucially, this works identically in the vacuum of space as it does in an atmosphere — a rocket does not need anything to “push against” the way a car pushes against the road or a swimmer pushes against water. It only needs to throw mass one way to go the other way, which is exactly why rockets, unlike jet engines, work in space at all.

This builds directly on yesterday’s topic, rocket anatomy: yesterday named the parts (engines, propellant tanks, the nozzle that shapes and accelerates the exhaust); today explains why those parts, arranged that way, actually produce motion. Today’s Story 10 on Starship’s booster landing burn is a live example: the engines relighting (or, Monday, not fully relighting) are the mechanism that lets the booster reverse and control its own momentum for a landing, using exactly this action-reaction principle in reverse.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 2/118 · Probability from a PDF over an Interval

A probability density function (PDF) doesn’t give you the probability of any single exact outcome — for a continuous quantity like a stock return, the chance of landing on any one precise value is effectively zero. Instead, a PDF describes probability as an area: the chance that a random quantity X falls between two bounds a and b equals the area under the PDF’s curve between those two points, written formally as an integral. Think of the PDF as a shape where taller sections mean “more likely to land somewhere near here,” and the total area under the entire curve always sums to exactly 1 (100% probability of landing somewhere).

This builds directly on yesterday’s topic, simple and log returns: once you’re treating a stock’s return as a random variable rather than a fixed known number, the natural next question is “how likely is a return in some range?” — and this equation is the formal tool for answering exactly that, which underpins every risk measure (like Value-at-Risk) used later in this track.

Real-world tie-in: today’s FOMC-odds figures in Story 10 (roughly 64–66% hold, 34–36% hike) are exactly this kind of probability, just discretized into two buckets rather than expressed as a continuous PDF — prediction markets and options-implied probabilities are, under the hood, built from exactly this area-under-the-curve logic applied to a continuous distribution of possible outcomes. Common misconception: a PDF’s height at a point is not a probability and can even exceed 1 — only the area under a range of the curve is a probability, never a single point’s height.
