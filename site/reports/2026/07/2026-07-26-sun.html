Section 21 · learning · no advance on weekendsSpaceflight Week Preview

Spaceflight 1/75 · starting position: Rocket anatomy

The spaceflight track opens with the physical building blocks before the equations: Rocket anatomy (the major structural elements — tanks, engines, avionics, payload — and how they're arranged), Newton's laws applied to rockets (why a rocket doesn't need air to push against), Thrust (the force a rocket engine actually produces, and what generates it), Mass flow (how quickly propellant leaves the vehicle, and why that rate matters as much as the thrust number), and Specific impulse (the efficiency measure — effectively "fuel mileage" — that ties thrust and mass flow together). This week's real-world tie-in is immediate: SpaceX's Starship Flight 13 on July 24 exercised exactly this anatomy at extreme scale — 33 first-stage engines, a mass-flow rate high enough to produce roughly 74 meganewtons of combined thrust, and a heatshield that survived reentry intact for the first time on this vehicle generation.

Section 22 · learning · no advance on weekendsQuant/ML Week Preview

Quant/ML 1/118 · starting position: Simple Return and Log Return

The quant/ML track opens with the basic unit every later model is built from: the difference between a simple return r_t = (P_t - P_{t-1})/P_{t-1} and a log return R_t = ln(P_t / P_{t-1}). Prices are not the modeling unit in quantitative finance — returns are, because they are closer to stationary and, in log form, add cleanly across time. The next four topics build directly on this foundation: probability from a PDF over an interval, the k-th central moment (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis), the null/alternative hypothesis framework for testing a mean, and the t-statistic used to test whether an apparent strategy edge is real or noise.

Real-world tie-in: Friday's VIX close of 18.58 versus Thursday's implied level is exactly a simple return calculation; the report itself computes percentage changes this way throughout. Common misconception: simple returns compound multiplicatively, not additively — a +10% day followed by a -10% day does not return you to the starting price (it leaves you down 1%); log returns fix the additivity problem but introduce their own small approximation for large moves.
