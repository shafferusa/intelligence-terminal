Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 4/71 · Significant figures

Significant figures are the digits in a measured (or calculated-from-measured) quantity that carry real information about its precision — every reliably-known digit plus one estimated digit, no more. A ruler marked in millimeters lets you read a length to the nearest millimeter with confidence and then estimate one further digit by eye; reporting more digits than that overstates how precisely you actually know the value, while reporting fewer throws away real information you do have. This builds directly on yesterday's topic, dimensional analysis: dimensional analysis checks whether an equation's structure is even sensible, while significant figures govern how honestly you report the specific numerical answer that equation produces once you plug in real, imperfectly-known measurements.

The common misconception worth correcting: trailing zeros after a decimal point (as in 4.20) are significant and meaningfully different from writing 4.2 — the former claims precision to the hundredths place, the latter only to the tenths — while trailing zeros in a whole number like 4200 are ambiguous without more context (scientific notation, 4.2 × 10³ versus 4.200 × 10³, removes the ambiguity). Today's market data offers a live example: this run's own Treasury fetch reports the 10-year yield as 4.67% and the 30-year as 5.20% — that trailing zero in "5.20" is doing real work, distinguishing it from a hypothetical 5.2%, which would imply a full order of magnitude less precision than the Treasury's own published curve actually carries.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 4/75 · Mass flow

Mass flow (often written as an m with a dot over it, ṁ) is the rate at which a rocket engine expels propellant mass — measured in kilograms per second — and it is one of the two quantities, alongside exhaust velocity, whose product gives an engine's thrust. A bigger engine, or one running at a higher throttle setting, achieves greater thrust in part by increasing its mass flow: shoving more propellant mass out the nozzle every second. This builds directly on yesterday's topic, thrust itself: thrust was introduced as mass flow multiplied by exhaust velocity, and today's lesson isolates the first of those two factors to look at it on its own.

A common misconception worth correcting: a higher mass flow rate is not automatically "better" engineering — it is one lever among several, and pushing it up without also managing exhaust velocity and propellant supply just burns through fuel faster for a given thrust level, shortening burn time. This morning's NROL-95 Falcon 9 launch (see Space & Spaceflight above) is a concrete example: the same Merlin engines that produced enough combined thrust to lift the rocket off the pad also had to sustain that mass flow rate consistently enough, for long enough, to reach orbital velocity — mass flow and burn duration trade directly against each other for a fixed propellant load.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 4/118 · Null and Alternative Hypotheses for a Mean Test

A hypothesis test starts by framing a question as two competing claims: the null hypothesis, H0, which states a specific baseline value for some true (unobservable) population mean, and the alternative hypothesis, H1, which states that the true mean differs from that baseline. This builds directly on yesterday's topic, expected value and central moments: a hypothesis test about a mean is really asking whether the first central moment (the expected value) of some underlying distribution actually equals a claimed number, using only a finite, noisy sample drawn from it.

Real-world tie-in: today's Q2 GDP release is itself an implicit hypothesis test in the public discourse around it — forecasters' clustered estimates (roughly 1.0–2.5% across sources, see Top Stories) function like a null hypothesis about the "true" growth rate, and the actual 8:30 ET print either falls comfortably inside that range (failing to reject the baseline story) or outside it (evidence for a different underlying growth regime). A common misconception worth correcting: failing to reject a null hypothesis is not the same as proving it true — a test with limited data, or a genuinely small underlying effect, can easily fail to detect a real difference that exists, which is exactly why a single GDP print is treated as one data point, not decisive proof of any specific growth narrative.
