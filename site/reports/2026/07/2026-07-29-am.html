Iran fires ballistic missiles at a US base in Jordan for the first time since Trump’s pause; all intercepted; US and Saudi forces strike back in Iraq

Event: 17:45 ET Jul 28 (attack), overnight (retaliation) · Published: rolling since Jul 28 evening · Last checked 06:00 ET

What happened
At 5:45 PM ET Tuesday, forces of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) launched multiple ballistic missiles in what US Central Command (CENTCOM) called an “attempted surprise attack” on US forces and a CENTCOM location in Jordan. CENTCOM says all of the missiles were intercepted; Jordan’s military separately said its own air defenses shot down five missiles launched from Iran. Saudi Arabia’s Defense Ministry confirmed its forces coordinated with CENTCOM. In response, CENTCOM and the Saudi Arabian Armed Forces struck “multiple terrorist logistics and weapons sites” in eastern Iraq, targeting Iran-aligned groups that the US says the IRGC has directed to carry out more than 30 drone attacks on US forces and Saudi energy infrastructure over the preceding 72 hours. Iran’s state-run IRNA news agency carried a Revolutionary Guard statement confirming its Aerospace Force fired missiles at “targets in the region,” an implicit confirmation of the attack. This is the first Iranian ballistic-missile attack on a US base since Trump paused US military action against Iran on Friday, Jul 25, to give diplomacy another chance — a pause this report has tracked holding through a run of drone (not missile) attacks. No casualties have been reported from this specific attack; CENTCOM’s own statement frames all missiles as intercepted. (This is a distinct, separate incident from the Jul 18 Iranian strike on Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base that killed two US service members — that earlier attack is not being conflated with tonight’s.) Separately overnight, oil jumped: this run’s own Yahoo Finance fetch shows WTI crude (CL=F) at $81.91, up 3.59% versus Tuesday’s own confirmed $79.07 close (Yahoo’s raw contract-roll-affected previous-close field is unreliable again, as flagged in recent editions); CNBC separately reports Brent crude near $86.97 (+3.42%) and WTI near $82.09 (+3.58%), corroborating both direction and magnitude. Trump told Axios on Monday, before this attack, that the US was in “very deep talks” with Iran but warned he would return to “very strong military action” if diplomacy failed: “Not much time…Either it goes fast or not at all.”
Why it matters
This is the most significant test yet of whether Friday’s pause survives: a direct ballistic-missile attack on a US base is a materially different order of escalation than the drone attacks this report has tracked through the pause, and it lands hours before today’s FOMC decision (oil is a direct input to inflation expectations) and tonight’s Microsoft/Meta/Arm/Qualcomm earnings.
What is confirmed
CENTCOM’s own statement (primary, US military) is corroborated by Jordan’s military statement and Saudi Arabia’s Defense Ministry (both primary, foreign government), and by Iran’s own IRGC/IRNA statement (primary, Iranian state media) confirming the missile launches. Wire and outlet coverage (CNN, Axios, Fox News, The Hill, AllSides, JPost) is consistent on timing, target and outcome (all intercepted).

Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 3/71 · Dimensional analysis

Dimensional analysis is the practice of tracking the physical dimensions (length, mass, time, and so on) that a quantity is built from, independent of which specific units you happen to measure it in. Every physical quantity has a dimension — speed is length divided by time, force is mass times length divided by time-squared — and those dimensions must match on both sides of any correct physical equation, and must combine consistently when quantities are multiplied, divided, added or subtracted. This builds directly on yesterday’s topic, units: where units are the specific labels you choose (meters vs feet, seconds vs hours), dimensions are the more fundamental, unit-independent categories those labels belong to. You can convert a speed from meters-per-second to miles-per-hour, changing its units, without ever changing its dimension of length-over-time.

The common misconception worth correcting: people often use “units” and “dimensions” interchangeably, but dimensional analysis is strictly more general than unit-checking — it lets you verify an equation’s structural correctness (do the exponents and combinations of length/mass/time make sense at all?) even before you’ve committed to specific units, and it lets you derive the functional form of physical relationships (like the period of a pendulum depending on length and gravitational acceleration in a specific combination) using nothing but dimensional consistency, before doing any detailed physics. Today’s market data offers a parallel: a Treasury yield curve mixes several distinct dimensions of information (a rate, a maturity, a date) that must all be tracked together — quoting “4.61%” without its 10-year maturity and its Jul 28 date is as dimensionally incomplete as quoting a speed without saying whether it’s meters or miles per second.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 3/75 · Thrust

Thrust is the force a rocket engine produces, and at its simplest it comes from two things multiplied together: how much propellant mass the engine expels per second (the mass flow rate), and how fast that propellant leaves the engine (the exhaust velocity). A bigger, more forceful push comes from either throwing more mass out the back every second, or throwing the same mass out faster — both increase thrust by exactly the same multiplicative relationship. This builds directly on yesterday’s topic, Newton’s laws applied to rockets: thrust is the concrete, measurable force that Newton’s third law describes in the abstract — it’s the number you’d actually put on a rocket engine’s spec sheet, in units of force (newtons, or pounds-force).

A common misconception worth correcting: people often assume a rocket’s engine thrust alone tells you how “powerful” or capable it is, but thrust by itself says nothing about efficiency — two engines can produce identical thrust while one burns propellant far faster than the other to do it, meaning it will run out of fuel much sooner (this is the setup for the specific-impulse concept later in this track, which measures thrust per unit of propellant consumed, not thrust alone). Today’s Starship Flight 13 recap (see Space & Spaceflight above) is a live example: the Super Heavy booster’s 33 engines igniting nominally is a statement about total thrust and redundancy, not by itself a statement about how efficiently that thrust was produced.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 3/118 · k-th Central Moment / Expected Value

The expected value of a random variable, E[X], is the probability-weighted average of all its possible outcomes — for a continuous variable it’s an integral of x times its probability density function, building directly on yesterday’s topic (finding probability as an area under that same density function). Central moments go a step further: they measure the shape of a distribution around its mean, by taking powers of the deviation (X minus the mean) and averaging those. The first central moment is always zero by construction; the second central moment is variance (how spread out the distribution is); the third relates to skewness (how lopsided it is); the fourth relates to kurtosis (how fat its tails are).

Real-world tie-in: today’s disputed FOMC hike-odds readings (see Top Stories) are themselves implicitly a statement about the first moment (expected value) of the market’s distribution over possible rate outcomes — when CME-style pricing says “38% hike odds” versus another venue’s “62% hold,” the two venues disagree about the shape of that underlying distribution, not just its mean. A common misconception worth correcting: variance (the second central moment) is not the same thing as risk in every practical sense — two return distributions can share identical variance while one has a much fatter left tail (higher kurtosis, more crash risk) than the other, which is exactly why quant risk models look beyond the second moment alone.

Data-quality note (equity chartPreviousClose bug, newly identified this run): Yahoo Finance's chartPreviousClose metadata field was found to be unreliable not just for continuous-contract commodity futures (a recurring issue in prior editions) but for the entire equity watchlist when fetched via a one-month history range.
