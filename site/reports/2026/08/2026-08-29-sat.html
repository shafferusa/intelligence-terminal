Today’s News

Russian strike near Kyiv kills at least 37 overnight, the deadliest this report has tracked in weeks

A drone-and-missile barrage hit an ammunition and drone-component storage site in a residential village outside the capital, with secondary explosions doing most of the damage.

Russia struck the village of Myla, roughly five kilometers west of Kyiv, around 8:10 PM local time Friday with drones and missiles that hit what Russia's defense ministry described as a warehouse storing long-range drone components; the resulting fire and secondary detonations tore through nearby housing. At least 37 people are confirmed dead and 42 injured, including four children, per multiple wire reports; roughly 50 residential buildings and a care facility for elderly and disabled residents were damaged, and 381 people were evacuated. The head of the local Dmytrivka community, Taras Didych, was among those killed. Ukrainian officials separately described a wider overnight barrage of roughly 267 drones plus Oniks and Iskander missiles across multiple cities — Myla was the deadliest single site.

President Zelensky said ammunition should never have been stored this close to homes and called for accountability, a rare public acknowledgment of a Ukrainian storage decision rather than only Russian conduct. This is the scale of strike this report's own Sunday outlook flagged as plausible this week (a forecast that a strike at or above Kryvyi Rih's 16-dead casualty scale would land before Aug 28 graded correct, in this week's Scorecard below) — and it exceeds that benchmark by more than double.

Why it matters This is the deadliest single strike either side has produced in the weeks this report has been tracking the war's tempo, and it lands during a week when Ukraine separately disclosed it is down to roughly 1% of its US Patriot interceptor stock — a shortage that helps explain why intercept rates against exactly this kind of mixed drone-and-missile barrage have been falling.

Nepal's flood toll passes 600, still unreconciled between the country's own agencies — and a second lake is forming

Nepal Police now count 626 dead; the Prime Minister's Office had put the figure at 469 confirmed with 977 missing as recently as Friday. Neither side has explained the gap.

Nepal Police's count reached 626 dead by Saturday morning, up from 553 earlier in the week, with roughly 2,500 people still missing across Nepal and Tibet combined and 2,301 rescued so far. That figure sits well above the Prime Minister's Office's own Friday count of 469 confirmed dead with 977 missing — the two domestic Nepali institutions have not reconciled their numbers, let alone squared them against China's separate, much lower Tibet-side count (which has drifted only slightly, to roughly 5–7 dead and 554–558 missing). This report treats the toll as still in dispute rather than settling on either figure.

The immediate danger has not passed: the first landslide-dammed lake that formed after the initial glacier collapse overflowed Friday, briefly halting rescue operations before they resumed, and officials are now watching a second, newly formed lake on the China side as more weekend rain is forecast. Nepal is reportedly preparing mass burials for unidentifiable remains.

What’s next A joint or reconciled Nepal-China toll remains, per this report's own forecast logged last Friday, a low-probability event in the near term (15–25%, graded open, horizon Sept 4) — the gap has widened, not narrowed, since Wednesday.

Gaza's ceasefire overseer warns the process risks collapse, as strikes continue in Gaza and a rare West Bank airstrike kills three

Nickolay Mladenov's warning follows his Wednesday UN Security Council criticism of Israel — the second public break between the ceasefire mechanism's own overseer and Israel in three days.

Board of Peace director-general Nickolay Mladenov said publicly Friday that the Gaza ceasefire risks collapsing over the same disarm-first-versus-parallel-track sequencing dispute this report has tracked for weeks, criticizing both Israel's restrictions on shelter supplies and continued strikes, and Hamas's non-fulfillment of its own disarmament commitments. Israeli strikes Friday killed five in Gaza — a family of three near Khan Younis and two people in Gaza City — and a separate, rare Israeli airstrike in the West Bank city of Jenin killed three named Palestinians (Qais Bitawi, 22; Basel Turkman, 26; Moamen Jaradat, 22); the Israeli military said it targeted a Hamas operative and two associates. This is the first West Bank airstrike since February 2025, and the second West Bank strike in as many weeks this report has recorded.

Single source A Times of Israel liveblog headline separately references settlers "reportedly attacking" the West Bank village of Qusra on Saturday; this report found no corroborating detail or second source and is not treating it as confirmed.

Why it matters Mladenov's Friday warning is a sharper escalation than his Wednesday UN criticism — the mechanism's own senior official is now using the word "collapse" publicly, not just registering disagreement.

The Week’s Ten Stories

Nvidia's best quarter in months and Fed Chair Warsh's hawkish debut pulled the market in opposite directions — and the Fed won

A beat that should have defined the week instead lasted about eighteen trading hours.

Nvidia reported Wednesday after the close: revenue of $96.221 billion against roughly $92–93 billion consensus, EPS of $2.22 against roughly $2.10, Data Center revenue up 117% year over year, a third-quarter guide of $108 billion (plus or minus 2%) that explicitly assumes zero China data-center revenue, and a fiscal-2028 growth guide of roughly 70% against the Street's roughly 44% estimate. The stock closed Thursday up 8.74%, breaking a streak of falling after each of its last four earnings reports, and dragged the Nasdaq to its best session in weeks alongside Salesforce (+22.6%) and CrowdStrike (+20.5%), both also reporting Wednesday night. Broadcom, the broader semiconductor index and AI-power names (Constellation Energy, Vistra) all rose with it — a forecast betting the print would instead trigger a broad AI-infrastructure pullback graded wrong, in this week's Scorecard.

Then Friday, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivered his first Jackson Hole keynote as chair, and the tape reversed inside a single session. Warsh emphasized above-target inflation (July's PCE price index printed 3.7% year over year, above a roughly 3.6% consensus) over forward guidance, calling guidance a practice that has "overstayed its welcome." September rate-hike odds roughly doubled by some measures, from about a third to the mid-50% range; the Treasury curve moved 2Y +14bp, 10Y +6bp, flattening 2s10s from roughly 47bp to 39bp. Equities gave back Thursday's gains (S&P −0.25%, Russell 2000 −1.39%), leadership flipped from technology and semiconductors to financials and communication services, gold fell 3.24% and the dollar index rose 0.52% — all in one session.

Why it matters A hawkish five-minute framing shift from one speaker moved more of this week's net market outcome than a historically strong earnings beat from the world's most valuable chipmaker did. That is itself informative about where markets currently weight risk.
What’s next One of this week's own forecasts, logged Friday, now asks whether the roughly doubled September hike odds partially unwind over the coming week absent a confirming data point — that forecast is graded next Saturday.

Anthropic beats the Pentagon in court — a narrower win than the headline suggests

A federal judge called the "supply chain risk" designation retaliation for protected speech. A separate, differently framed Pentagon rule still designates Anthropic the same way.

U.S. District Judge Rita Lin (N.D. Cal.) ruled Thursday that the Defense Department's February 2026 designation of Anthropic as a national-security supply-chain risk — imposed after Anthropic declined to let the military use Claude for mass surveillance or autonomous weapons — was arbitrary and capricious and violated the company's First and Fifth Amendment rights. The 59-page order directs the Pentagon to withdraw the directive; the government has not yet filed a formal notice of appeal as of Saturday morning, though its litigation posture in the case (arguing the designation stemmed from a contractual dispute, not viewpoint retaliation) and its pattern of appealing adverse AI-policy rulings this year both point toward one.

What has not been widely reported, including in this outlet's own Thursday coverage: a separate, narrower case is still pending before the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals, involving a different Pentagon rule that also designates Anthropic a supply-chain risk. Anthropic has not actually shed that designation entirely — Thursday's win removes one instrument, not the underlying label, until the D.C. Circuit case resolves.

Why it matters This is the first court win for an AI lab against the administration's use of national-security authority as a policy lever, arriving the same week Congress's own AI-security deadline passed with only a joint industry letter in hand — but the fuller picture is more contested than a single ruling implies.

U.S. District Court, N.D. Cal. opinion (primary) · NPR · CNBC · TechCrunch · Forbes · The Hill · Axios.

Meta settles with 47 states for up to $17.1 billion over youth mental health — structured to pressure the whole industry, not just Meta

$5.3 billion of the total is contingent on whether TikTok and YouTube adopt matching rules.

Meta agreed Wednesday to pay roughly $12.7 billion regardless, with a further $5.3 billion contingent on whether TikTok and YouTube adopt matching minor time-limit and age-assurance rules — a structure explicitly designed to set an industry standard rather than resolve a single company's liability. Florida, New Mexico and Texas are not part of the settlement. Required changes include a two-hour daily soft cap for minors, a "Productive Pause" prompt after fifteen minutes, hidden like-counts for minors, banned beauty filters for teens and a feed opt-out.

Why it matters This is the largest youth-safety settlement of its kind, and its contingent-payment structure is a novel enforcement mechanism worth watching if other states try to replicate it against other platforms.

The mail-ballot litigation whipsawed again: a compliance finding, a fresh injunction, and a Sept 3 hearing still to come

Judge Talwani found USPS "feigned compliance," then reinstated protection anyway with a narrower order.

Judge Talwani ruled Tuesday that USPS "feigned compliance" with her earlier injunction by finalizing the mail-ballot rule, but declined to block its Federal Register publication, since USPS itself said it could not implement the rule while her injunction stood. Separately, the parallel 23-state-plus-D.C.-plus-Pennsylvania merits lawsuit has not yet produced a fresh injunction of its own.

The AI industry answered Congress collectively — 116 companies, one joint letter, still no individual response from either frontier lab named in the original inquiry

OpenAI and Anthropic signed a group statement on AI cyber risk. Neither has yet answered Congress's original questions about their own disclosed security incidents.

More than 100 AI and cybersecurity companies, including OpenAI and Anthropic, signed a joint letter this week urging closer industry-government collaboration on defending against AI-enabled cyberattacks. What Changed in the World

Three threads ran under this week's individual stories. First, the week's two largest scheduled catalysts — Nvidia's earnings and Warsh's Jackson Hole debut — pointed in opposite directions, and the macro one won: a single hawkish speech undid more of the market's gains than a historic beat-and-raise added, a reminder that idiosyncratic good news does not automatically compound into a market-wide trend when a policy signal cuts the other way. Second, "beat the number and still sell off" struck a second company in a second consecutive week — Marvell after Walmart — suggesting markets are now consistently pricing something beneath the headline (margin guidance, comparable-sales growth) rather than the earnings-per-share line itself, at least for richly valued names. Third, three separate disaster and conflict tolls (Nepal-Tibet's flood, the Myla strike, and the ongoing Hormuz standoff's competing claims) all got harder to pin down this week rather than easier, even as each event itself grew more consequential — a pattern this report is watching rather than forcing into a single resolved number before the underlying facts settle.

One forecast — whether any federal civilian agency missed the Aug 21 VMware vCenter patch deadline — is left open rather than force-graded: this kind of individual-agency compliance failure typically surfaces later via GAO or CISA reporting, not same-week press coverage.

The week's clearest miss: this report's own Sunday scenario matrix, again. All four of last Sunday's scenarios (base, bull, bear, shock) graded partial or wrong. The base scenario got the index-level outcome right — the S&P closed the week within half a percent of the assumed band — but for the wrong reasons: it assumed Warsh would read as measured and Nvidia would merely meet consensus, when in fact Warsh was more hawkish than expected and Nvidia beat decisively, with the two surprises partly canceling out rather than the week unfolding calmly as described. The bull and bear scenarios both assumed Nvidia's earnings and the Fed's tone would move markets in the same direction as each other; instead they moved in opposite directions, breaking both scenarios' central premise. The shock scenario correctly stayed low-probability and none of its triggers fired. This is the second time in three weeks this report's own scenario matrices have gotten the market-level number right while getting the causal mechanism wrong — a pattern worth naming rather than treating as three unrelated misses.

Nvidia's own forecasts held up better than the scenarios around it. A standalone forecast that Nvidia's post-earnings move would exceed 5% in either direction graded correct (three separate versions of essentially this same call, issued across three editions, all graded correct together) — the zero-China framing and the scale of the FY28 guide were enough to break the stock's four-report losing streak decisively. A forecast that the print would trigger a broad AI-infrastructure pullback graded wrong: the opposite happened, and the cohort rallied with Nvidia rather than against it.

Geopolitical escalation kept clustering, mostly as expected. A forecast that Hormuz would see further escalation within the week graded correct (a further tanker strike and fire, both within days of the prior fatal incident); a narrower forecast asking specifically about a full closure, sinking, or direct US-Iran exchange graded only partial, since the actual escalation (a fatal but more limited strike) didn't match those exact trigger conditions — a reminder to name broader trigger categories when the underlying pattern is recurring harassment rather than a single dramatic event. A forecast that Iran would halt all Gulf oil traffic in response to Monday's sanctions graded wrong, consistent with this campaign's established pattern of threats outrunning action. A forecast that a Ukraine strike at or above Kryvyi Rih's casualty scale would land this week graded correct, resolved by the Myla strike covered in Today's News.

Canada's trade fight keeps producing wrong forecasts, now for a fourth straight week. A forecast that the US and Canada would reach a deal or extension within five trading days of the Aug 19 deadline graded wrong — talks instead collapsed outright, with Canada's Sept 8 retaliatory tariffs now the operative fact rather than a deal. This report is treating "close to a deal" language in this specific negotiation as an unreliable signal until it sees a signed text.

Courts moved on schedule when a specific date forced their hand. A forecast that Judge Talwani would reissue a narrower mail-ballot injunction before Aug 28 graded correct, consistent with her pattern throughout this litigation of promptly refiling after being overturned procedurally.

Fed communications discipline broke down selectively. A forecast that hot inflation data (July's core PPI) would draw explicit Fed comment before Jackson Hole graded correct — KC Fed's Jeffrey Schmid called inflation "stubborn" and "sticky" days before the keynote. A parallel forecast that a Fed official would comment on the same week's soft retail-sales and consumer-sentiment data before Jackson Hole graded wrong: no such comment surfaced. Officials, it turns out, spoke up early about inflation running hot but stayed quiet on demand-side softness — a asymmetry worth tracking into future Fed-communications forecasts.

Standalone calls that missed cleanly. A forecast that oil would move more than 3% intraday on the day of the Iran sanctions announcement graded wrong — the actual move was a moderate 2.0–2.3%, most of the expectation already priced in. A forecast on whether the week's hardest-hit space-sector names would recover half their Aug 24 decline within three sessions graded partial: the group showed a real but uneven, catalyst-dependent bounce (a Falcon 9 launch lifted ASTS specifically) rather than a clean recovery or a clean failure to recover.

Overhyped & Undercovered

Overhyped. Last Sunday's scenario matrix treated Nvidia's earnings, Warsh's Jackson Hole debut and the Iran sanctions rollout as three roughly independent catalysts that could combine into a coherent bull, bear or base case; in practice the sanctions story stayed a slow-burn thread all week while Nvidia and Warsh dominated every session's price action and moved in opposite directions from each other, breaking the tidy combined-scenario framing this report itself built. Thursday's Anthropic-Pentagon court win was covered across outlets, including this one, as a clean legal victory without the caveat that a separate, differently framed designation remains in place pending a different case.

Undercovered. That same D.C. Circuit nuance on Anthropic's Pentagon designation is the week's clearest undercovered story — it changes how complete Thursday's win actually is, and this report did not catch it until today's own research. Ukraine's Patriot-interceptor shortage, disclosed the prior week, is the quiet backdrop to why the Myla strike's intercept rate was as low as it was; no outlet this week connected the two directly. The gap between Nepal Police's and the Nepal PM's Office's own flood tolls — two domestic government bodies with substantially different numbers — received far less scrutiny than the more visible Nepal-China cross-border discrepancy.
