Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 13/71 · Forces

This morning introduced forces as pushes or pulls — vector quantities with both size and direction, arising from identifiable physical sources (gravity, contact, tension, friction, air resistance), whose combined effect (the "net force") determines an object's actual motion. Tonight, deepen the idea of net force specifically: when several forces act on an object at once, they don't just coexist independently — they add together as vectors, meaning forces in the same direction reinforce each other while forces in opposite directions partially or fully cancel. An object sitting still on a table has gravity pulling it down and the table pushing up with an equal, opposite force; the net force is zero, which is exactly why it doesn't move, even though two real forces are actively acting on it the whole time.

A common misconception is that "no motion" means "no forces are acting" — in fact it usually means the forces are balanced, not absent, as the table example shows. The real-world tie-in from today's report: SpaceX's stock move and the CPI-driven market reaction are a loose but genuine analogy for net-force thinking — today's tape had several forces acting on it simultaneously (a cooler CPI print pushing broadly higher, an idiosyncratic Home Depot decline pulling one sector down, an AI-infrastructure rally pushing tech up while software lagged) and the modest, mixed headline index moves are the "net force" of several real, larger, partially offsetting individual pushes, not evidence that nothing was happening. This sets up Newton's Second Law (F = ma), the next topic in this sequence, which turns "net force" from a qualitative idea into a precise, quantitative one.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 13/75 · Atmospheric Pressure

This morning introduced atmospheric pressure as the weight of the air column above a given point — substantial at sea level (about 14.7 pounds per square inch) and thinning rapidly with altitude as less air remains stacked overhead. Tonight, deepen the concept: because pressure falls off roughly exponentially with altitude rather than in a straight line, the first few miles of ascent matter disproportionately — a rocket loses roughly half of sea-level atmospheric pressure by around 18,000 feet, but takes much more altitude to lose the next half again. This is why the earliest seconds of a launch, when a vehicle is still deep in dense air and moving relatively slowly, produce very different aerodynamic conditions than the thin-air, high-speed regime it reaches a few minutes later — the vehicle experiences its most demanding combined pressure-and-speed loads (max-Q, mentioned this morning) somewhere in between, not at either extreme.

A common misconception is that a rocket has "escaped the atmosphere" once it clears the dense lower layers near the ground; in reality there is no hard edge, and meaningful (if thin) atmospheric drag persists for many miles further up, which is why orbital vehicles are shaped and oriented to minimize drag long after they look, to an observer on the ground, like they have already left the atmosphere behind. Today's news tie-in: Spire Global (Top Story 9) and other small-satellite operators on this report's watchlist build spacecraft that must survive brief but intense atmospheric heating and pressure change on the way up and, eventually, manage a very different, gentler kind of atmospheric drag once in low orbit, where thin residual atmosphere still slowly decays an orbit over months to years.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 13/118 · Moving Average Model MA(1)

This morning introduced the MA(1) model: Xt = μ + εt + θ1εt−1 — today's value depends on today's random shock plus a fraction (θ1) of yesterday's shock, and nothing further back than that. Tonight, deepen the intuition using today's actual data: SpaceX's +9.30% move is a textbook example of a fresh shock (εt) hitting a series that, over the prior three sessions, had been drifting down under an entirely different, slower-moving influence (pre-unlock positioning). An MA(1) lens would predict that today's shock leaves only a single, fading echo (θ1εt) in tomorrow's expected return before vanishing entirely — a falsifiable, testable claim, unlike vaguer language about the market "still digesting" news. By contrast, the CPI-driven VIX decline looks less like a clean MA(1) shock and more like a shift in the general level of uncertainty, which an MA(1) model (with its hard, one-period memory cutoff) is a poor fit for describing on its own.

A common misconception, repeated from yesterday but worth restating: MA(1) is not a "1-day moving average of prices" in the everyday sense — it describes how a shock's effect on the level decays, not a smoothing window applied to observed values. The real-world tie-in: distinguishing "a shock with a one-period echo" (MA-like) from "a shift in the underlying mean or variance" (not MA-like) is exactly the kind of judgment call a quant analyst has to make before choosing a model, and today's tape offered a clean example of each.
