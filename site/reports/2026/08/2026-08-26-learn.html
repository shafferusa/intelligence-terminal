The Four Percent Shortfall That Put America in Line for Gas

Wednesday, August 26, 2026 · 27 min read

In the spring of 1979, a drop of about four percent in the world’s oil supply, met by a price the government would not let move, turned into hours-long lines at pumps across the country — a case study, run at national scale, in what economics actually studies: how people choose when there is not enough to go around, and where that explanation quietly stops working.

Economics

Day 8 of 150 · Economics 1 of 20 · Foundations

On the morning of May 9, 1979, roughly ten million drivers in California woke up to a new rule: if the last digit of your license plate was odd, you could buy gasoline only on odd-numbered days of the month; even digits, even days. Cars began lining up at four in the morning outside stations that would not open until half past six. By the time the pumps started, lines already stretched for blocks, and it was common for a station to run dry by noon — hours before the next driver in line ever reached the nozzle. Pennsylvania adopted its own version of the same rule that summer. So did New York, New Jersey, and a dozen other states. People negotiated new rituals into their lives that had never existed before: waking before dawn to sit in a car for an hour and a half, keeping a paperback in the glovebox for the wait, calling a neighbor to check whether the station on the corner still had fuel before making the drive worth it.

Here is the detail that ought to stop you: the country was not running out of oil. Iran’s 1979 revolution had knocked its oil exports down hard — from about 5.8 million barrels a day in mid-1978 to roughly 445,000 barrels a day by January 1979 — and that flowed through into a global shortfall economists now put at somewhere around four percent of world oil supply. Four percent. If every filling station in the country had simply raised its price enough to make total demand match that slightly smaller total supply, the shelves — or in this case, the underground tanks — would not have run empty in the way they did. Higher prices would have been unwelcome, and they would have hurt, but a four-percent hole in supply is not remotely large enough on its own to explain a driver waiting ninety minutes at 4 AM to buy ten dollars of gas. Something else had to be going on, and that something else is the actual subject of this lesson.

The missing piece was price controls. Since August 1971, first under Richard Nixon’s wage-and-price freeze and then, after 1973, under the Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act, the federal government had capped what refiners and stations were legally allowed to charge for gasoline, and had assigned each state a fixed allocation of fuel based on how much it had used the year before — a formula that could not adjust in real time to a shock like Iran’s. The lines of 1979 were not, mainly, a story about geology or geopolitics. They were a story about what happens when the ordinary mechanism a market economy uses to reconcile “people want more than there is” is switched off, and something else — in this case, literally standing in line for hours — has to do that job instead. That is where today’s subject begins, and it will not leave that image behind for the rest of the lesson.

The idea before the notation

Strip away the equations, the graphs, and the jargon, and economics is trying to answer one question: given that there is not enough of everything for everyone to have as much as they would like, who gets what, and how is that decided? That is the whole of it. Not the stock market. Not just money. The British economist Lionel Robbins put the discipline’s scope into a single, famous sentence in 1932, and it has held up for nearly a century: economics is “the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses.” Read that slowly, because every word is doing work. “Ends” means goals, wants, desires — anything a person is trying to get more of, and there is no restriction to material goods; love, sleep, reputation, a clean house, and a promotion are all “ends” in this sense. “Scarce means” is the constraint: time, money, energy, land, oil, attention — the things it takes to pursue those ends, and of which there is never, for any person or any society, an unlimited supply. “Alternative uses” is the sentence’s quiet engine: those scarce means could be put toward more than one end, which is exactly what forces a choice in the first place. If gasoline could only ever be used for one thing, there would be no economic problem to speak of — you would simply have as much or as little of that one thing as the world happened to produce. Because a gallon of gasoline can fuel a commute, a delivery truck, a vacation, or a farm tractor, someone — a market, a government, a household — has to decide which of those uses it goes to, and that decision is what this entire subject studies.

Scarcity, in this technical sense, is not the same thing as poverty, and it does not mean “in short supply” the way a store might run low on a popular toy at Christmas. It means, simply, that the total amount of a thing people would want if it were free exceeds the total amount that exists. Water is scarce in this sense even in a rainy place, because if water cost nothing at all, people would use it for things they do not currently bother with — watering every inch of a lawn twice a day, running a fountain around the clock — until the free supply ran out. Time is scarce for a billionaire exactly as it is for anyone else: twenty-four hours a day, no more, regardless of income. Scarcity is not a temporary glitch to be fixed. It is the permanent condition that makes economics necessary in the first place, and it is why the subject reaches into things that have nothing to do with a cash register: which patients get an organ transplant when donors are few, which students get into a school with more applicants than seats, which projects a government funds when tax revenue cannot cover every worthy idea.

Scarcity forces choice, and choice has a specific, precise cost that is easy to underrate because it never shows up on a receipt. Economists call it opportunity cost: the value of the next-best alternative you gave up by choosing what you chose. Spend an evening studying this curriculum and the opportunity cost is not zero just because no money left your pocket — it is whatever you would otherwise have done with that time: an hour of a show you wanted to watch, sleep, a conversation with someone you care about. Every real choice, without exception, carries this shadow cost, whether or not a dollar figure is attached to it, and the central discipline of economic thinking is learning to see it even when it is invisible to an ordinary accounting of what came in and what went out. That last distinction — between what shows up in a ledger and what a decision actually cost — is not a throwaway line. It is where this lesson is going to end up.

And this is also where the ordinary market mechanism you have been living inside your whole life comes in. In an economy where prices are free to move, the price of a good is the signal that tells everyone, simultaneously and without anyone having to be told directly, how scarce that good currently is relative to how much people want it. When gasoline gets scarcer, an unconstrained price rises; the higher price does two things at once, both essential and both often missed. It tells consumers to use a little less — combine errands, carpool, put off the road trip — and it tells producers there is more money to be made supplying more of it, pulling in extra output from wherever it can be found. Price is doing the rationing. It decides, without a bureaucrat, a queue, or a coupon book, who gets how much, based on who is willing to pay the new, higher price for it. Suppress that price artificially, hold it below the level at which the amount people want to buy equals the amount available to sell, and the rationing job does not disappear. It just moves — onto something else, something usually far more wasteful than money, like standing in a car for ninety minutes at four in the morning.

The paradox that took a century to resolve

The idea that price reflects scarcity runs into an old and genuinely puzzling objection almost immediately, and working through it is the fastest way to see what “value” means to an economist as opposed to in ordinary speech. In 1776, in The Wealth of Nations, Adam Smith — the same figure whose “invisible hand” metaphor for the price system you have likely already heard secondhand — wrote down what became known as the diamond-water paradox. Nothing, Smith observed, is more useful than water: a person deprived of it dies within days. Almost nothing is less useful than a diamond: it cannot be eaten, cannot keep anyone warm, does no useful work. And yet water, throughout most of human history, has sold for next to nothing, while a diamond of modest size commands a price that could buy a house. If price is supposed to track how much something matters, this looks backwards, and Smith himself could not fully resolve it. He is generally credited with quietly setting the puzzle aside, in favor of a separate theory that value came from the labor required to produce a good — itself a reasonable-looking idea that turned out to have its own serious problems, and not one this curriculum needs to unpack today.

The actual resolution did not arrive until nearly a century later, in the 1870s, and it arrived independently, within a few years of one another, from three economists working in three different countries who had never read each other’s work: Carl Menger in Vienna, William Stanley Jevons in Manchester, and Léon Walras in Lausanne. Historians of economic thought call this near-simultaneous discovery the marginal revolution, and the single idea at its center is this: nobody ever actually chooses between “all the water in the world” and “all the diamonds in the world.” Every real choice is made at the margin — one more glass of water, one more diamond — and the value of that next, additional unit is not the same thing as the total value of the whole category to human life.

MU(n) = TU(n) − TU(n−1)

TU(n)
total utility — the total satisfaction a person gets from consuming n units of a good altogether
MU(n)
marginal utility — the additional satisfaction from the n-th unit specifically, on top of what the first n−1 units already provided
“Utils”
an artificial unit economists use to make satisfaction comparable across goods for teaching purposes; nothing in the real world is measured in utils, and no claim is being made that satisfaction is literally countable this precisely

Water’s total utility to a person, or to humanity, is enormous — life itself depends on it. But because water is, for most people in most places, abundant relative to how much they need, the marginal utility of one more glass, once thirst is already satisfied and the day’s cooking and washing are already accounted for, is small: the tenth gallon a household uses today adds very little on top of the first nine. A table makes the shape of this concrete. Suppose a household’s daily water use produces the following satisfaction, in illustrative “utils,” for each successive hundred gallons:

Total utility keeps climbing — 100, then 180, then 235 — but each successive increment adds less than the one before it: 100, then 80, then 55, then 30, then 10. This pattern, in which each additional unit of a good delivers less added satisfaction than the last, is common enough across ordinary goods that economists treat it as close to a rule of thumb and call it diminishing marginal utility. A diamond sits at the opposite end of the same logic. Because diamonds of any real size are naturally rare, almost nobody ever owns enough of them to reach the flat part of that curve; the marginal utility of one more diamond stays high precisely because so few people ever get to the second or third one. Price, in a working market, tracks that marginal figure, not the total: it answers “what is the next unit worth to the marginal buyer,” not “what would civilization pay, in total, to have this entire category of good rather than none of it at all.” Water can be worth everything in total and next to nothing at the margin at the same time, with no contradiction whatsoever — and once you can hold total value and marginal value apart as two different questions, Smith’s two-thousand-year-old paradox stops being a paradox at all.

Scarcity, choice, and the price system, made precise

Put the plain-English idea into working form and three tools carry almost all of the weight: opportunity cost as a number you can actually compute, a picture of an economy’s full menu of possible choices, and the supply-and-demand machinery that explains why 1979’s gas lines were not really about oil at all.

Opportunity Cost(chosen option) = Value(best forgone alternative)

Chosen option
whatever you actually decided to do with a scarce resource — money, time, land, fuel
Best forgone alternative
the single most valuable thing you could have done instead with that same resource, not the sum of every possible alternative — only the best one you gave up
Value(·)
what that forgone alternative was actually worth to you, which may or may not have a dollar price attached

Notice what this formula deliberately leaves out: it says nothing about money spent. A choice can have a real, calculable opportunity cost even when zero dollars change hands, because the “cost” here is what you did not get, not what you paid. This is also where economics and accounting, lessons three and seven of this curriculum, quietly diverge, and the divergence has a name.

Economic Profit = Total Revenue − Explicit Costs − Implicit (Opportunity) Costs

Total Revenue
money actually received
Explicit Costs
money actually paid out — rent, wages, materials, interest; this is the “costs” an accountant records, and Total Revenue minus Explicit Costs alone is accounting profit, the figure that appears on the income statement built in lesson seven
Implicit (Opportunity) Costs
the value of resources you already owned and used in the venture — your own time, your own capital — measured by what they would have earned in their next-best use, and never recorded in any ledger because no invoice was ever issued for them

A business, or a person, can show a healthy accounting profit — the books balance, revenue exceeds every dollar that went out the door — and still be making the economically wrong choice, once the value of what was given up is counted. That single distinction is the reason a trained economist can look at a set of books that any accountant would sign off on without objection and still say, correctly, “this is a bad decision.” The first worked example below makes this concrete.

The second tool is the production possibilities frontier, or PPF: a picture of every combination of two goods an economy (or a person, or a business) could produce if it used all of its scarce resources as efficiently as possible. Points inside the frontier are achievable but wasteful — resources sitting idle. Points outside it are not achievable at all, given the resources on hand right now. Points on the frontier itself are the efficient trade-offs, and moving along that frontier from one point to another is opportunity cost made visual: more of one good, by definition, means less of the other, because every resource that goes to producing more of the first good is a resource that is no longer available for the second.

Qd(P) = Qs(P) → defines the equilibrium price P* and quantity Q*

Qd(P)
quantity demanded at price P — how much buyers want to purchase at that price; normally falls as P rises
Qs(P)
quantity supplied at price P — how much sellers want to offer at that price; normally rises as P rises
P*, Q*
the equilibrium price and quantity: the one price at which the amount buyers want to buy exactly equals the amount sellers want to sell, so nothing is left over and nobody is turned away

Shortage(Pc) = Qd(Pc) − Qs(Pc), for a price ceiling Pc < P*

Pc
a price ceiling — a legal maximum price set below the market equilibrium P*
Qd(Pc)
quantity demanded at the artificially low ceiling price — higher than Q*, because the good is now cheaper than the market would otherwise make it
Qs(Pc)
quantity supplied at the ceiling price — lower than Q*, because producers are less willing to sell at the lower price
Shortage(Pc)
the gap between what buyers want at the ceiling price and what sellers are willing to provide; this gap has to be resolved by something other than price — a queue, a lottery, a coupon, or, in 1979, an odd-even license plate rule

Working the numbers

Worked example 1 · a rush job, and the profit an income statement cannot see

A freelance graphic designer is offered a rush logo project: $400 in fees for an estimated eight hours of work, due tomorrow. Accepting it means declining two client check-in meetings already on her calendar for that same eight-hour window, worth $250 and $350 respectively in billable time — $600 combined — that she would have to cancel and, realistically, could not reschedule before both clients moved to another designer.

  1. Accounting profit if she takes the rush job: Total Revenue $400 minus Explicit Costs (say, $20 in software and file-delivery fees) = $380. By the income statement alone, this looks like an unambiguously good day’s work.
  2. Opportunity cost of taking the rush job: the best forgone alternative is the two client meetings she would give up, worth $600 — not the theoretical sum of every possible use of those eight hours, just the single most valuable one she actually had lined up.
  3. Economic profit: $400 (revenue) − $20 (explicit costs) − $600 (implicit/opportunity cost) = −$220.

The rush job pays real money, and an accountant would record it as a $380 gain with no error anywhere in the arithmetic. But the designer is $220 worse off than she would have been keeping her original schedule, because the ledger she filed with the IRS has no line for “client relationships and future work I gave up.” That gap between the two numbers — $380 recorded, $220 actually lost — is not a mistake in either calculation. It is the entire reason economics needs a concept accounting does not: a ledger records what happened; opportunity cost is trying to capture what would have happened instead, and the two questions have different answers on purpose.

Worked example 2 · a farm, two crops, and a frontier that bends

Consider a hypothetical 500-acre farm that can grow only soybeans and corn, with land that is not uniformly suited to either — some acres are naturally better corn ground, some drain better for soybeans. As the farmer devotes progressively more land to soybeans, the acres given up to it are, at first, land that was only mediocre for corn anyway; later, the best corn land has to go too. Read the right-hand column and the pattern is the entire point: the opportunity cost of each additional 10,000 bushels of soybeans keeps rising — 0.2 units of corn per unit of soybean for the first step, then 0.6, then 1.2, then 2.0, then 6.0 for the last, most extreme step. This is called the law of increasing opportunity cost, and it is why a real PPF bows outward rather than running as a straight line: resources are not perfectly interchangeable between uses. The first acres shifted to soybeans are the ones the farm loses the least by giving up; the last acres shifted are the best corn ground on the property, and giving those up costs far more corn per soybean gained. A straight-line frontier would imply every acre is equally good at both crops, which is essentially never true of a real piece of land, a real worker’s time, or a real factory’s machinery.

Worked example 3 · a price ceiling, a shortage, and the arithmetic 1979 lived through

Build a small, simplified gasoline market to see the mechanism 1979 exposed, using illustrative numbers chosen only to make the arithmetic clean — not a fit to any historical dataset. Let quantity be in thousands of gallons per day and price in dollars per gallon:

  1. Demand: Qd = 100 − 10P. Supply: Qs = −20 + 20P.
  2. Equilibrium: set Qd = Qs. 100 − 10P = −20 + 20P → 120 = 30P → P* = $4.00. Substitute back: Qd = 100 − 40 = Q* = 60 thousand gallons/day, and Qs = −20 + 80 = 60, confirming the two sides match.
  3. Now impose a price ceiling at Pc = $2.00 — a legal maximum, below the $4.00 equilibrium, of exactly the kind the Emergency Petroleum Allocation Act put in place. Quantity demanded at $2.00: Qd = 100 − 20 = 80. Quantity supplied at $2.00: Qs = −20 + 40 = 20.
  4. Shortage = Qd(Pc) − Qs(Pc) = 80 − 20 = 60 thousand gallons per day — a gap equal to the entire equilibrium quantity, produced by a price held only half the equilibrium level.

That 60,000-gallon gap in this illustrative market does not vanish because the law says the price cannot rise. It has to be absorbed somehow, by someone, and price is no longer the mechanism doing the absorbing. What replaces it is whatever shows up first: a line that starts forming before dawn, a station that runs dry by noon, an odd-even rule that cuts the pool of eligible buyers on any given day roughly in half. None of that is irrational behavior by drivers — standing in line is exactly the rational response to a good that is worth more than its legal price and simply is not available for sale at any price above that ceiling. The real 1979 shortage had one more ingredient this simplified model leaves out, and it made the shortage worse than the static arithmetic above predicts: reporting on empty stations and long lines led drivers to start “topping off” — filling a half-full tank instead of waiting until it was low — which pushed the effective demand curve itself further to the right than ordinary driving needs alone would have. A Brookings Institution analysis published that same year also pointed to the allocation rules themselves: because the regulations made it more profitable for some distributors to hold gasoline back than to sell it immediately, part of the shortage was inventory being withheld from the market rather than simply unavailable. A model that treats Qd and Qs as fixed curves cannot see either effect on its own; both are the model’s assumptions breaking under the very policy being analyzed, which is exactly the limitation this lesson is building toward.

The episode has an ending worth knowing, because it is the cleanest confirmation available that the mechanism, not the oil, was the real story. On January 28, 1981, eight days into his presidency, Ronald Reagan signed Executive Order 12287, immediately eliminating the remaining federal price and allocation controls on crude oil and refined petroleum products that had been in place, in one form or another, since 1971. Reagan’s own stated reasoning tracked the analysis in this lesson almost exactly: nine years of price controls, his administration argued, had held domestic production below its potential, encouraged wasteful consumption by keeping the price artificially low, and deepened American dependence on OPEC oil — the price ceiling had not made the underlying scarcity go away, only hidden its true cost and let the shortage keep resurfacing as lines instead. Economists at the time expected the immediate, uncontrolled price to rise somewhat — contemporary estimates ran to roughly eight to thirteen cents a gallon over the following year, a real and unwelcome cost to drivers, and exactly what the equilibrium-price arithmetic of worked example three would predict for a market moving up from a ceiling toward P*. What did not reappear, once price was allowed to do the job it had been legally blocked from doing since the Nixon administration, were the lines.

Where it already shows up

You do not need a 1979 gas line to see this machinery at work; it is running under two subjects you already read about regularly. The first is the Federal Reserve. Since a 1977 amendment to the Federal Reserve Act, the central bank has operated under what is usually called a dual mandate: pursue maximum employment and stable prices at the same time. Those two goals are not always pulling in the same direction — policy that cools inflation by raising rates typically also cools hiring, and policy that supports employment by holding rates low can let inflation run hotter — and every time this newspaper reports a Fed decision, it is describing policymakers choosing a point on a trade-off, not solving a problem that has one clean answer. That is scarcity again, just measured in percentage points of unemployment and inflation instead of gallons of gasoline: there is no policy setting that maximizes both goals at once, so a choice, with a real opportunity cost, has to be made.

The second is closer to 1979’s own mechanism than it might first appear: rent control. Dozens of American cities cap how much a landlord may raise rent in a given year, and the economic logic is the identical price-ceiling arithmetic from worked example three, applied to housing instead of gasoline — a legal maximum price held below what the market would otherwise set produces a gap between how much housing people want at that price and how much landlords are willing to keep offering at it. A 2012 poll of forty-one prominent academic economists by the University of Chicago’s Initiative on Global Markets found 81 percent agreed that rent control has reduced the quantity and quality of affordable rental housing available in cities that have imposed it, against just 2 percent who disagreed — one of the more lopsided results that panel has ever recorded on a policy question. That is a positive economic finding — a claim about what rent control actually does to housing supply, testable in principle against evidence — and it is worth holding apart from the separate, normative question of whether protecting existing tenants from sudden rent increases is worth that cost, which is a judgment about values and priorities that economic analysis alone cannot settle. Keeping those two questions distinct, rather than letting an answer to one quietly stand in for an answer to the other, is one of the most useful habits this subject can teach.

The mistake almost everyone makes

The most common misunderstanding about economics is that it is fundamentally about money — stock prices, GDP, a household budget. Money is one of the units economics measures things in, because it is convenient and widely comparable, not what the subject is actually about. The actual subject, per Robbins’s definition, is choice under scarcity in general, and money is often absent from the examples that matter most: a parent deciding how to split attention between two children, a hospital deciding which patient gets the one available ICU bed, a student deciding between two hours of sleep and two hours of studying. All of these are economic problems in the technical sense, with real opportunity costs, and none of them require a price tag to be analyzed the same way worked example one analyzed a freelancer’s rush job.

A second, more specific misconception sits inside worked example three and is worth naming directly, because it is the one this lesson’s focus — the limits of the models — is really pointing at: a supply-and-demand diagram is a deliberately simplified picture, built on a phrase economists lean on constantly, ceteris paribus — Latin for “other things held equal.” The basic model in worked example three assumes the demand curve itself stays fixed while price changes around it. That assumption is a genuine simplification, not a law of nature, and 1979 is a clean demonstration of exactly how it can fail: the shortage caused by the price ceiling triggered topping-off behavior, which shifted the demand curve itself outward, which made the shortage worse than the original, static Qd and Qs curves alone predicted. The model was not wrong about the direction of the effect — a price held below equilibrium really does produce a shortage, every time, and that basic prediction held up completely in 1979. What the simplest version of the model could not see on its own was the feedback loop the policy itself created. The lesson is not that supply and demand is a bad tool. It correctly called the headline result decades before it happened, wherever a government has tried a price ceiling. The lesson is that every model is built on assumptions, that naming those assumptions out loud is not optional homework but part of using the model honestly, and that the interesting, dangerous cases are usually the ones where a real-world policy quietly breaks the assumption the simple version relies on.

A third boundary is worth stating plainly rather than discovering the hard way later in this curriculum: most of the models built here assume people act to get the most value out of their scarce resources, reasonably consistently, once they know the relevant costs and benefits. That assumption — sometimes shortened to “rational actors” — is not a claim that people are calculating machines, or that they never panic, procrastinate, or act on incomplete information; the topping-off behavior in worked example three is itself a case of people responding sensibly to genuine uncertainty about whether gas would still be there tomorrow, not a breakdown of rationality at all. It is a simplifying assumption, adopted because it makes a model tractable and because it gets the direction of most everyday economic behavior right, not because economists believe it describes every decision perfectly. Later lessons in this track will spend real time on where that assumption bends and where a more careful, behavioral model is needed instead. For now, the honest version of the rule is this: treat “this model assumes rational, self-interested behavior” the same way you would treat any other stated assumption — as a known simplification to keep in view, not a hidden trapdoor.

Where this leaves you
  • You can state, precisely, what economics studies: choice under scarcity, in any currency — money, time, attention — wherever a scarce resource has more than one possible use.
  • You can compute an opportunity cost and an economic profit, and you know why a decision can look profitable on an income statement and still be the wrong choice once what was given up is counted.
  • You can read a production possibilities frontier, explain why it bows outward, and build a simple supply-and-demand model to show exactly why a price ceiling produces a shortage — and how large.
  • You know the limits of that model as well as its power: it assumes other things stay equal, and the sharpest failures of simple economic reasoning tend to show up exactly where a real policy makes that assumption false, as the 1979 gas lines did.

One more connection is worth carrying forward from a different subject entirely. Day seven of this curriculum built double-entry bookkeeping around a single, load-bearing fact: every real transaction has two sides, and a ledger that only records one of them is a record of half an event. Opportunity cost is the same insight, transplanted out of the accounting department. Every choice also has two sides — what you got, and what you gave up to get it — and an income statement, however carefully kept, only ever records the first one. Worked example one’s freelancer did not lie on her books; her accounting was flawless. Her books simply were not built to see the $600 she gave up, because no invoice existed for it, the same way a trial balance cannot see a transaction that was never entered in the first place. Economics exists, in part, precisely because the ledger’s blind spot is real, and because most of the choices that matter most in a life — how to spend an evening, which job to take, which of two goods a scarce acre of land should grow — are decided in that blind spot, where nothing was ever going to show up on a receipt.
