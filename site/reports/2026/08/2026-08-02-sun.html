Section 6 · high-risk claims, two-source rule appliedGeopolitical Outlook

US-Iran: strikes canceled, but the announcement itself is disputed

Confirmed — multiple sources Late Saturday, President Trump posted on Truth Social that he is canceling a planned US-Israeli strike campaign against Iranian energy infrastructure, writing that the US was “locked and loaded and ready to go against the Islamic Republic of Iran, at levels of Military Terror, Strength, and Power not seen since World War II,” but that “we have just been asked by Iran, and other Middle Eastern Countries, to hold off any attack in that the parameters of a deal has been agreed to,” and that he had “agreed... to cancel the attack, subject to being able to rapidly make a DEAL.” He described the deal’s outline as the “Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT, and an end to Iran’s nuclear threat.” Disputed Iranian state media (the semi-official Mehr agency, seen as close to the Supreme Leader) explicitly denied Iran asked for any pause, with one line of commentary describing Trump as having “once again backed away from his hollow threats and presented his retreat to the world as a favor.” Iranian military officials separately called Trump’s account a pressure tactic aimed at Gulf state rulers. What is confirmed: a strike order was not given as of Friday evening and the operation is now paused. What remains uncertain: whether any deal framework genuinely exists, what if anything Iran actually agreed to, and how long the pause holds. Regional context reported alongside the cancellation: Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman is reported to have called Trump directly Saturday urging cancellation and a return to negotiations, with Qatar, the UAE, Turkiye and Pakistan also pressing both sides to de-escalate.

Iran’s warnings and the State Department’s response

Confirmed — multiple sources Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi held separate calls Saturday with his Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani counterparts, warning in the Saudi call that any US or Israeli attack — or regional-state facilitation of one — would draw a “decisive and proportionate response” from Iran’s armed forces, per a statement on his Telegram account. Separately, the US State Department issued a security alert Saturday morning advising Americans in the Middle East to “consider departing, or be prepared to depart should there be escalation,” warning of possible flight cancellations and airspace closures — issued before Trump’s cancellation announcement and, as of this report’s cutoff, not rescinded.

Shipping under threat independent of the ceasefire news

Confirmed — multiple sources Two LNG carriers managed by the same shipping company were struck within days of each other. The Gaslog Salem was hit by a drone while docked at Egypt’s Damietta port earlier in the week. The Bermuda-flagged Gaslog Shanghai, carrying Qatari LNG, was struck by an unidentified projectile roughly 11 nautical miles northeast of Oman at 23:30 UTC Friday while transiting outbound through the Strait of Hormuz — the impact hit the portside engine room, causing a fire (extinguished by the crew, no injuries reported) and a temporary loss of propulsion; UKMTO reported no environmental impact. No party has claimed responsibility for either strike as of this report’s cutoff. The Strait carries roughly a fifth of global LNG shipments; continued attacks on tankers there are a distinct risk channel from any land-based Iran strike campaign and are not addressed by Trump’s weekend announcement.

Poland/NATO: no Article 4 invocation, forecast tracking toward correct

Confirmed — multiple sources No change since last week: NATO has confirmed the missile that crashed roughly 90 km inside Polish territory July 30 was Russian (fragment analysis points to a Kh-101 cruise missile manufactured in Q2 2026), but the alliance has taken no Article 4 consultation request as of this report’s cutoff — a measured posture consistent with forecast 2026-07-30-pm-3 (horizon today, Aug 2; grading is a Saturday-routine task and will be recorded next weekend).

Domestic spillover: suspected Iran-linked water-utility cyberattacks

Confirmed — multiple sources (underlying intrusions) Unverified — attribution Michigan reported cyberattacks on nine water systems this week, joining Minnesota in disclosing intrusions; federal officials say at least seven states’ water systems have been affected. Most confirmed attacks involved the remote-monitoring/control technology (SCADA-type systems) water utilities use, forcing some utilities onto manual processes while defenses are strengthened; no damage, injuries, or water-quality impact has been reported, and all systems are described as operating safely. The FBI is investigating both the Michigan and Minnesota incidents. US officials describe Iran as the leading suspect but stress the investigation is preliminary and that definitive forensic attribution has not been established — carrying an explicit confidence qualifier per this report’s attribution standard, not a confirmed state-actor finding.

Section 22 · learning · no advance on weekendsQuant/ML Week Preview

Quant/ML 6/118 · next up: Covariance and Correlation

The first five topics built the statistical vocabulary for a single return series — simple vs. log returns, probability from a density, central moments, hypothesis testing, and the t-statistic. This week moves from one asset to two (or more): Covariance and correlation — Cov(X,Y) measures how two series move together in absolute terms, while correlation ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σ_Xσ_Y) rescales that co-movement to a unit-free number between -1 and 1, making it comparable across any pair of assets. This is the single equation underneath portfolio diversification: the whole reason holding multiple assets can reduce risk without proportionally reducing expected return. The week continues into the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) — tools for measuring how a series correlates with its own past, used to diagnose whether returns show any exploitable persistence or mean-reversion — then weak stationarity (the assumption, often violated by real financial data, that a series’ statistical properties don’t drift over time), and the autoregressive model AR(p), the simplest model built directly on the ACF/PACF diagnostics.

Real-world tie-in: this week’s Market Intelligence Appendix (Section 23) reports the HYG/LQD and HYG/IEF ratios and rate-differential-vs-equity relationships throughout — every one of those cross-asset comparisons is, mechanically, a correlation calculation. Common misconception: a high correlation between two assets does not mean one causes the other to move (see this report’s own market-causality language rules in the methodology section) — correlation is a purely statistical co-movement measure, and it can and does break down exactly when it would be most useful, such as during liquidity crises when normally uncorrelated assets suddenly move together.
