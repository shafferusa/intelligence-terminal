The Ledger That Was Built to Catch Every Mistake — And Didn’t Catch This One

Tuesday, August 25, 2026 · 25 min read

Double-entry bookkeeping forces every transaction to check itself against its own record — a rule strong enough that Barings Bank ran on it for 233 years, until a single trader in Singapore found the one thing it was never built to catch.

Accounting

Day 7 of 150 · Accounting 2 of 18 · Foundations

On the morning of February 27, 1995, Barings Bank stopped existing. It had been Britain’s oldest merchant bank, founded in London in 1762 by Francis Baring, old enough and trusted enough that in 1802 the United States government came to Barings and a Dutch partner, Hope & Co., to help finance the Louisiana Purchase — a deal that doubled the size of the country before Barings had even reached its fiftieth birthday. It had survived the Napoleonic Wars, the collapse of half of Europe’s ruling houses, two World Wars, and 233 years of ordinary banking crises. Queen Elizabeth II banked there. And it was destroyed in a matter of days, not by a war or a panic or a crash it never saw coming, but by the trading losses of one 28-year-old employee in a Singapore back office, hidden inside a single account with the number 88888.

By the time regulators and Barings’ own executives in London finally understood what had happened, that trader, Nick Leeson, had lost roughly £827 million — about US$1.4 billion at the time, and, by later reporting, close to twice the bank’s entire trading capital. Barings was sold to the Dutch bank ING for £1, its debts included, the following week. Leeson went to prison in Singapore. And the part of this story that ought to stop you cold, if you have spent even one lesson learning what accounting actually is, is this: Barings did not keep sloppy books. It ran on double-entry bookkeeping, the same five-hundred-year-old system taught in this lesson, a system whose entire reason for existing is that it is supposed to be structurally difficult to get away with exactly this. Every transaction it records has to check itself against its own record, automatically, the moment it is written down. So how, inside a system built explicitly to make a wrong number announce itself, does one person hide over a billion dollars for more than two years?

The honest answer is not a flaw in the arithmetic. Double-entry bookkeeping did exactly what it has always done, every single day Leeson used it. The answer is about the difference between what that check actually promises and what almost everyone — reasonably, and wrongly — assumes it promises. Lesson three of this curriculum told you why accounting exists: to close the trust gap between an owner of money and the person actually handling it. Today’s lesson is how — the actual mechanics that make a transaction “land twice,” the trial balance those mechanics feed, and, by the end, the precise and much narrower thing that check is actually guaranteed to catch.

The idea before the notation

Start from a fact that has nothing to do with bookkeeping at all: a real transaction is never one-sided. Money and value do not appear or vanish; they move, and something moves in exchange. Borrow $800 from a bank and two true things happen at once — you now hold $800 more in cash, and you now owe $800 more to the bank. Buy a delivery van with $30,000 cash and, again, two things happen — your cash goes down by $30,000, and you now own a van worth $30,000. Sell a customer $500 of consulting work for cash and two things happen — your cash goes up $500, and your business is $500 richer, a fact that has to land somewhere in the books too. Double-entry bookkeeping did not invent this two-sidedness. It simply refuses to look away from it. Where an ordinary diary might write “bought a van, $30,000” and consider the sentence finished, a ledger insists on writing down both of the real things that happened, because both of them are true, and a record that only captures one of them is a record of half an event.

This is also where the words themselves come from, and the etymology is not decoration — it tells you what the two sides actually mean. Debit descends from the Latin debere, “to owe.” Credit descends from credere, “to entrust” or “to believe” — the same root behind “credence” and “credibility.” That is a strange pair of words to build an entire numerical system on, until you remember the commenda contract from lesson three: one party entrusts capital, the other owes an account of what became of it. Debit and credit are not, at bottom, a mathematician’s plus and minus. They are a ledger’s way of asking, of every single transaction, who owes, and who is owed — which is exactly the trust problem this whole subject was invented to solve, now built directly into the grammar of every entry.

It helps to see, briefly, what the alternative looks like, because it is not a hypothetical — it is what most small operations still use today without calling it anything in particular. A single-entry record is just a running diary of cash in and cash out: money received, money spent, a running total. It is easy to keep and it answers one real question well — how much cash is on hand right now — but it cannot answer almost anything else. It cannot tell you what the business owns beyond cash (that van, that inventory, never appear in a pure cash diary at all), what it owes, or whether last month was actually profitable as opposed to merely cash-flush from, say, a loan that came in and will have to be repaid later. And because every line stands alone, nothing in a single-entry diary can ever be checked against anything else in the same diary; an error just sits there, unfindable by the record itself, until someone happens to notice the world does not match what the diary says. Double entry does not just add a second column for the sake of symmetry. It adds the one thing a cash diary structurally cannot have: an internal cross-check, built from the plain fact that every transaction already has two real sides, whether or not the bookkeeper bothers to write both of them down.

None of that yet explains why a ledger “checks itself.” That comes from a second observation, just as plain: every dollar recorded in a business’s books is simultaneously two things — a resource the business holds, and a claim on that resource held by someone (an owner, a lender, a supplier waiting to be paid). Those are not two separate facts that happen to be equal by coincidence; they are the same fact, described from two directions, the way “north of Pittsburgh” and “south of Erie” can describe the identical stretch of highway. That is the whole content of the accounting equation from lesson three, Assets = Liabilities + Equity, and it is also the whole content of double entry: if every transaction is recorded as its two true, simultaneous effects, and if resources and claims-on-resources are always, by definition, the same size, then the two sides of the ledger cannot help but stay equal to each other. The balance is not a rule bolted on from outside to catch mistakes. It is what “recording both sides honestly” already guarantees, whether or not anyone is watching for errors — which is exactly why, when it stops being true, something specific and findable has gone wrong.

Two columns, one rule

Turn that plain-English idea into a working system and you need three things: a place to record each account’s two-sided history, a rule for which side of that record grows an account and which side shrinks it, and a way to prove, after any number of transactions, that the whole system still balances. The traditional recording device is the T-account — named for its shape, a vertical line under a horizontal one, forming a T, with the account’s name written across the top and its two sides, debit on the left and credit on the right, running down beneath it. A cash account with $2,000 deposited and $60 later paid out looks, in T-account form, like a single line on the left and a single line on the right:

The rule for which side means “increase” is not arbitrary, though it is memorized so often that it starts to feel that way; it falls directly out of the accounting equation. Assets sit on the left of that equation, so an asset account’s natural, or normal, balance is a debit, and a debit entry increases it. Liabilities and equity sit on the right of the equation, so their normal balance is a credit, and a credit entry increases them. Revenue increases equity (a business that earns money is worth more), so revenue behaves like equity and carries a normal credit balance; expenses decrease equity, so they behave like the mirror image of equity and carry a normal debit balance, the one asset-like exception on the equation’s liability-and-equity side.

ΣDebits (this entry) = ΣCredits (this entry), for every transaction, without exception

ΣCredits

Because this holds true of every individual transaction, it holds true of the sum of all transactions ever posted — which is exactly what a trial balance checks. A trial balance is nothing more elaborate than a list of every account in the ledger with its current balance written in the normal-balance column (debit balances in one column, credit balances in the other), and the two columns totalled. If the two totals do not match, a debit was posted somewhere without its matching credit, or vice versa, or the amounts on the two sides of some transaction did not agree — and the ledger says so immediately, before anyone has to read a single transaction to find out.

The convention that debits sit on the left and credits on the right is not something the mathematics of the accounting equation requires — the equation would balance exactly as well the other way around. It is an inherited layout choice, and it traces directly back to Pacioli’s 1494 Summa, where the twenty-seven-page bookkeeping section used the Italian dare (“to give,” the root that later became “debit” in English usage) for the left column and avere (“to have,” the root behind “credit”) for the right, describing goods and cash given away on one side and value received or owed on the other. The specific choice of which side is which has stayed fixed for over five centuries since, purely by inheritance — every accountant since has learned it exactly as Pacioli laid it out, the way English speakers still drive on the right in most of the world for reasons that trace back to which side a right-handed rider kept a sword arm free, long after anyone alive remembers why.

In practice, a transaction is not usually kept in T-account form on paper — that shape is a teaching and reasoning device. The actual record is a journal entry: a dated line naming the accounts touched, debits listed first and flush left, credits listed beneath them and visually indented, with the two dollar amounts required to match. The espresso cart’s equipment purchase from worked example one below, written as a journal entry rather than three separate T-accounts, looks like this:

A journal entry — debits first and flush left, credits indented beneath them, amounts equal in total.
AccountDebitCredit
Equipment$1,200—
    Cash—$400
    Notes Payable—$800

Every transaction this lesson computes is written this same way before it is ever posted into a T-account: one journal entry, one date, debits and credits that must sum to the same total before the entry is even allowed into the ledger. The T-account is where those entries get sorted by account so a balance can be read off; the journal is where they are first written down, in the order they actually happened. Together the two views are the whole of what “keeping the books” means.

Running the ledger

Worked example 1 · six transactions and a trial balance that has to close

Imagine a one-person espresso cart, capitalized and run over its first week. Each transaction below is recorded as a full double entry — a debit somewhere, a matching credit somewhere else, always equal in total.

  1. The owner deposits $2,000 of personal savings into the business. Debit Cash $2,000; credit Owner’s Equity $2,000. (Cash, an asset, goes up; equity, the owner’s claim on the business, goes up by the same amount — the same fact, seen from two sides.)
  2. The cart buys a $1,200 espresso machine, paying $400 in cash and financing the rest with a note from the equipment dealer. Debit Equipment $1,200; credit Cash $400; credit Notes Payable $800. Three accounts, still one transaction: the two credits, $400 plus $800, total exactly the one debit, $1,200.
  3. The cart buys $150 of coffee beans and cups on account — the supplier bills later. Debit Supplies $150; credit Accounts Payable $150.
  4. The first week’s cash sales total $500. Debit Cash $500; credit Revenue $500.
  5. The cart pays $60 in cash to rent its curbside spot for the week. Debit Rent Expense $60; credit Cash $60.
  6. The supplier is paid in full. Debit Accounts Payable $150; credit Cash $150.

Post each line to its account and tally the ending balances: Cash = 2,000 − 400 + 500 − 60 − 150 = $1,890. Equipment = $1,200. Supplies = $150. Accounts Payable = 150 − 150 = $0. Notes Payable = $800. Owner’s Equity = $2,000. Revenue = $500. Rent Expense = $60.

Now build the trial balance. Debit-side balances: Cash $1,890 + Equipment $1,200 + Supplies $150 + Rent Expense $60 = $3,300. Credit-side balances: Notes Payable $800 + Owner’s Equity $2,000 + Revenue $500 = $3,300. The two sides match, exactly, to the dollar — not because the numbers were chosen to make them match, but because every one of the six entries above kept its own debits and credits equal, and six balanced transactions can only ever sum to a balanced total. Check it a second way, against the accounting equation itself: Assets (Cash $1,890 + Equipment $1,200 + Supplies $150 = $3,240) should equal Liabilities (Notes Payable $800) plus Equity (the owner’s original $2,000, plus this week’s net income of Revenue $500 minus Rent Expense $60, or $440, for a total of $2,440). $800 + $2,440 = $3,240. It matches Assets exactly. This is not a coincidence to marvel at once and move past — it is the mechanical guarantee this entire lesson is about, and it will hold after transaction six thousand exactly as reliably as it held after transaction six.

Worked example 2 · a $180 gap, and a number theory fact bookkeepers have leaned on for a century

Suppose the cart’s bookkeeper needs to post a $1,860 cash sale but, copying the figure by hand, transposes two digits and writes $1,680 into the Cash debit column instead — the credit to Revenue is still correctly recorded at $1,860. The trial balance no longer closes.

  1. Total debits are now short by 1,860 − 1,680 = $180 relative to total credits.
  2. A bookkeeper checking this by hand has a shortcut, and it is worth actually deriving rather than just quoting: every power of ten is one more than a multiple of nine — 10 = 9+1, 100 = 99+1, 1,000 = 999+1, and so on, forever. That means any single digit’s contribution to a number’s value, however many places it sits from the right, always leaves the same remainder modulo nine as that digit alone.
  3. Swap two digits in a number — say, the ‘8’ and the ‘6’ in 1,860, giving 1,680 — and the change in value is exactly (8−6) × (100 − 10) = 2 × 90 = $180, matching the discrepancy found in step 1 precisely, and 180 divides evenly by 9 (180 ÷ 9 = 20) with nothing left over.
  4. This is not specific to this example. Any transposition of two digits changes a number’s value by a multiple of nine, always, because the two swapped digits’ place values — whatever they are — both reduce to the same remainder mod nine, and their difference cancels that remainder out completely.

So a trial balance that fails to close by an amount evenly divisible by nine is not proof of a transposition error — other mistakes can also happen to land on a multiple of nine — but it is exactly specific enough to be worth checking first, and bookkeepers have used it as a first move for well over a century, long before anyone could run software to search every line. It is a small, genuine piece of number theory hiding inside a completely unglamorous filing-cabinet skill, and it is also, not incidentally, the last worked example in this lesson that involves an honest mistake rather than a dishonest one.

Worked example 3 · what the trial balance inside account 88888 actually looked like

Return to Barings, and to the specific mechanical question this lesson opened with: how does a trial balance — the exact check performed in worked examples one and two — fail to catch £827 million in losses?

  1. By the end of September 1992, within months of Leeson opening it, account 88888 already carried S$8.8 million in losses. By the end of 1992 that had grown, by later reporting, to roughly £2 million; by the end of 1993, £23 million — a roughly 11.5-times jump in a single year. By the end of 1994, £208 million — another roughly 9-times jump. By the account’s final tally in early 1995, £827 million — a further near-4-times jump in under two months, as the January 1995 Kobe earthquake sent the Nikkei crashing straight through the very options positions Leeson had bet would stay range-bound.
  2. Every one of the trades producing those losses was still a real, double-entry transaction. A losing futures position closed out still debits a loss and credits cash or a margin account for the identical amount; the trial balance for account 88888, taken on its own, would have “balanced” on any given day in exactly the mechanical sense worked example one balanced — because every individual entry inside it still obeyed the one rule this lesson has built up from the start, debits equal to credits, transaction by transaction.
  3. What broke was not the arithmetic; it was what got included in the number London ever saw. Leeson ran both Barings Futures Singapore’s front office, which executed the trades, and its back office, which settled and reported them — two functions that a properly run trading firm keeps under separate people specifically so that one cannot quietly control what the other discloses. He then simply left account 88888 out of the reports that were sent up the chain to London, presenting only the profitable side of his trading. A trial balance is a check on a ledger’s internal arithmetic; it has no way of noticing that an entire account, correctly balanced within itself, has been excluded wholesale from what a reader elsewhere in the organization is shown.
  4. To keep the operation funded while the true losses grew, Leeson also requested and received large cash transfers from London, justified at various points as margin needed to support client and hedging positions that supporting documentation later showed did not exist in the size claimed. Those transfers were themselves recorded as ordinary, balanced double entries — debit cash, credit whatever liability or client account the request was booked against. A correctly balanced entry, built on a false justification, still balances.
  5. This was not an unforeseeable failure. Barings’ own internal audit function raised the exact structural problem in writing, twice, in August and again in October 1994 — warning explicitly that Leeson holding responsibility for both the front office and the back office exposed the bank to a heightened risk of error and fraud, and recommending the two be separated. Management in Singapore acknowledged the recommendation and represented, in writing, that steps would be taken; by the time of the collapse four months later, no meaningful separation had actually been put in place. A Bank of England Board of Banking Supervision inquiry, reporting in July 1995, later laid out the full chain of missed and unenforced warnings in detail. The warning existed. It named the precise mechanism. It was not acted on. No trial balance was ever going to substitute for that.

Put the whole mechanism in one sentence: double-entry bookkeeping guarantees that the two sides of a recorded transaction agree with each other. It was never designed to, and cannot, guarantee that the transaction itself, or the set of accounts a reader is shown, describes the truth. The failure was a control failure that a warning had already named and management had already promised to fix, not a bookkeeping failure — and the distinction is the entire point of this lesson.

Worked example 4 · two mistakes that cancel, and why the trial balance never blinks

One category from the misconception section below is worth seeing in numbers before it is only described in words: a compensating error, where two unrelated mistakes happen to offset each other in total even though every individual account is wrong.

  1. Return to the espresso cart. Suppose the bookkeeper, posting the $500 cash sale from worked example one, mistakenly debits Cash for $550 instead of $500 — a $50 overstatement.
  2. Unrelated to that, the same bookkeeper later posts the $60 rent payment as $110 — crediting Cash for $110 instead of $60, a $50 overstatement in the opposite direction on the same account.
  3. Cash, after both errors: 2,000 − 400 + 550 − 110 − 150 = $1,890 — the identical figure computed correctly in worked example one, because the $50 overstatement on the debit side and the $50 overstatement on the credit side of the same account exactly cancel.
  4. Revenue still shows $550 instead of the true $500, and Rent Expense still shows $110 instead of the true $60 — both individually wrong by $50 — yet the trial balance closes perfectly, because it only ever checks that the grand total of every debit balance equals the grand total of every credit balance, never that each individual account is correct.

Nothing about this required deception; a tired bookkeeper transposing two invoices late on a Friday produces exactly this pattern by accident. That is precisely why it belongs on the standard list of errors a trial balance cannot see: it needs no bad intent at all, only two ordinary mistakes that happen to land on the same account from opposite sides.

Where it already shows up

You do not have to take any of this on faith the next time this newspaper cites a company’s balance sheet from an SEC filing. Every 10-K and 10-Q on data.sec.gov — the same EDGAR system this paper’s own entity-registry checks pull from — presents a balance sheet in which total assets is required to equal total liabilities plus equity, because the double-entry ledger that produced it makes that equality structurally unavoidable, the identical guarantee worked example one demonstrated on a six-line espresso cart. You can open any public company’s balance sheet and personally verify that it foots — adds up correctly, top to bottom — without trusting a single word management wrote about why the numbers are what they are. That is real, load-bearing information. It is just a much narrower guarantee than “these numbers are true,” which is precisely the gap Barings fell through.

The institutional response to that gap, since 1995 and especially since the accounting scandals at Enron and WorldCom in the early 2000s, has been to stop relying on the trial balance to do work it was never built for and instead build separate, human controls around it: segregation of duties (the front office that trades and the back office that settles and reports are never, on paper, allowed to be the same person, which is the specific control Barings’ own internal auditors had already named and management had already failed to enforce), and mandatory external audits, in which an outside firm with no stake in the result examines a sample of transactions against outside evidence — bank statements, counterparty confirmations, physical inventory counts — that the company itself cannot alter. In the United States, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, passed directly in response to Enron and WorldCom, went a step further and made this a legal requirement rather than a best practice: Section 404 requires public companies to document and test their internal controls over financial reporting — segregation of duties chief among them — and requires the outside auditor to separately attest that those controls actually work, not merely that the resulting numbers happen to foot. Both the audit and the Section 404 control review exist because a trial balance answers “does the arithmetic agree with itself,” and both were built to answer the harder, different question this lesson keeps circling back to: does the arithmetic agree with the world.

The mistake almost everyone makes

The single most common misunderstanding about double-entry bookkeeping is believing that a balanced trial balance means the books are correct. It means something much narrower: that every posted transaction moved equal value onto the debit side and the credit side. Accountants have a standard list, refined over generations of finding the same handful of mistakes, of error types a trial balance is structurally incapable of catching, because in every one of them, both sides of the double entry are still equal to each other — just wrong, or missing, together.

An error of omission is a transaction left out of the books entirely — neither its debit nor its credit was ever recorded, so nothing is unequal, because nothing is there to compare. This is, precisely, what happened to account 88888 at the reporting level in worked example three: the account itself was internally consistent, and simply absent from what London was shown. An error of commission posts the right amount to the wrong account of the same type — paying the electric bill and debiting “Repairs Expense” instead of “Utilities Expense” still balances, because both are expense accounts and the credit side, Cash, is untouched by the mistake. An error of principle is the same slip one level more serious — posting to the wrong class of account entirely, say recording the espresso machine purchase from worked example one as an expense instead of an asset; the trial balance still closes, but the business now looks less profitable and less capitalized than it actually is, in every report drawn from that ledger. An error of original entry is worked example two’s transposed $1,860 sale in its general form — the wrong figure copied onto both the debit and the credit side alike, so the two sides still agree with each other while both are simultaneously wrong by the same amount; this is the one category on this list a trial balance can sometimes still flag indirectly, because a transposition specifically usually appears on only one side of the pair, which is exactly why worked example two’s version showed up as a genuine, findable $180 gap rather than slipping through silently like the other five. A complete reversal swaps debit and credit outright — crediting Cash and debiting Revenue for the same sale — and still balances, because equal-and-opposite is, mechanically, still equal. And a compensating error is two independent mistakes that happen to cancel — one account overstated by $50, an unrelated account understated by the same $50 — leaving the grand total untouched while every account beneath it is individually wrong.

None of these are exotic. They are the ordinary, well-catalogued ways a set of books can be perfectly balanced and still not tell the truth, and Leeson’s fraud is best understood as a deliberate, sustained version of the first one on the list, carried out by someone with the unusual power — through his control of both front and back office — to decide what got included in the first place. The lesson is not that double-entry bookkeeping is weak. It is that it was only ever built to answer one specific question, and Barings needed a different check — segregation of duties, an outside auditor, a supervisor who read the flagged concerns and acted — to answer the question the ledger itself could never have been asked.

What today points toward

Everything computed in worked example one — the ending balances sitting in Cash, Equipment, Supplies, the two liability accounts, Owner’s Equity, Revenue, and Rent Expense — is, right now, just a trial balance: a flat, unordered list, proven internally consistent but not yet organized into anything a reader outside the business could actually use to answer “what does this business own, and how is it doing.” Day 12 of this curriculum takes those same debit and credit balances and formally arranges the asset, liability and equity accounts into a balance sheet — a snapshot, at a single moment, of what the equation Assets = Liabilities + Equity actually looks like for a real business. Day 17 takes the revenue and expense accounts — Revenue $500 and Rent Expense $60 in the cart’s case — and arranges those into an income statement, the record of performance over a stretch of time rather than a snapshot of a single instant. Day 25 returns directly to the distinction raised a few sections back, between cash on hand and genuine profitability, and gives it a name — accrual versus cash accounting — and a full formal treatment of why a business can be profitable on paper while short of cash, or flush with cash while losing money. Every one of those later lessons will assume you already know, without re-deriving it, that every number arriving on any financial statement came from a ledger that was mechanically forced to balance the moment each transaction was written down — and that “balanced” and “true” are two different words for a reason.

That distinction connects to something taught four days into this curriculum, in a different subject entirely. Day four’s lesson on units and dimensional analysis showed that checking whether an equation’s units match on both sides catches a large and specific category of physics mistakes — a dropped factor, a term that cannot possibly belong — while proving nothing at all about whether the surviving, dimensionally consistent equation is actually correct; a formula can have exactly the right units and still be wrong by a missing factor of two, or a wrong constant, or an assumption that does not hold. That is precisely the shape of what a trial balance does for a ledger: both checks are necessary, not sufficient — real, mechanical, valuable filters that a mistake must pass through, and that a large share of real mistakes never do pass through, while a smaller, more dangerous category slips through both of them precisely because it never disturbs the one property being checked. Learning to recognize that shape — a check that is genuinely strong and still not the same thing as proof — turns out to be one piece of machinery that keeps reappearing under different names across entirely different fields, and it is worth having in hand now, twice, from two directions, well before this curriculum runs out of new fields to find it in again.

Where this leaves you
  • You can post a real transaction as a double entry — debit here, matching credit there — build a T-account, and produce a trial balance that has to close if every entry was recorded correctly.
  • You know exactly what “the books balance” actually proves: that debits equal credits across every transaction. Nothing more.
  • You can name the six classic ways a trial balance can close perfectly while the books are still wrong — omission, commission, principle, original entry, reversal, and compensating errors — and you know which one, at the reporting level, describes what Nick Leeson did to Barings Bank.
  • You know where this is going: day 12 arranges today’s balances into a balance sheet, day 17 arranges them into an income statement, and both will lean on the mechanics built here without re-explaining them.
