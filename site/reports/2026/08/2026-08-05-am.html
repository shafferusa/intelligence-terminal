Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 8/71 · Position

Position is the first genuinely kinematic idea in this sequence — not just a number, but a number tied to a reference point and a direction. Where the last several topics built the toolkit (measurement, units, dimensional analysis, significant figures, scalars, vectors, coordinate systems), position is the first quantity that actually describes where something is: a vector, usually written x (in one dimension) or as a full vector r in two or three, that specifies an object's location relative to a chosen origin. The choice of origin and axis directions is arbitrary — physics doesn't care where you put x = 0 — but once chosen, it has to stay fixed for the rest of a problem, or every subsequent velocity and acceleration calculation built on top of it breaks.

This is where coordinate systems (yesterday's topic) stop being an abstract bookkeeping exercise and start doing real work: position is a coordinate system's first payload. A position of "+5 meters" is meaningless without knowing what "+5" is measured from and which direction counts as positive; that dependency on a reference frame is the seed of everything that follows in kinematics, from displacement (a change in position) through velocity and acceleration, which are just position's first and second derivatives with respect to time.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 8/75 · Mass fraction

Mass fraction is the number that turns the rocket equation from an abstract logarithm into a hard engineering constraint. It's usually written as the ratio of propellant mass to a rocket's total (wet) mass at launch — propellant mass fraction, often called PMF — and it answers a blunt question: of everything sitting on the pad, how much of it is fuel you're going to burn and throw away, versus structure, engines, and payload you have to build, fly, and (ideally) either land or pay for outright? A high mass fraction (propellant dominating the total mass) is exactly what the rocket equation rewards, since it directly sets the mass ratio (m₀/m_f) that gets fed into the natural log this report covered a few sessions ago.

This builds directly on yesterday's introduction to the rocket equation: mass fraction is the design lever engineers actually pull to hit a target Δv, because exhaust velocity is capped by propellant chemistry (or engine technology) while mass fraction is a structural design choice — thinner tank walls, lighter engines, less unused margin. It's also why reusability is such a hard trade: hardware you keep (landing legs, grid fins, extra structural margin to survive re-entry and landing) is mass that doesn't burn, which directly lowers the propellant mass fraction and therefore the achievable Δv for a given vehicle size, unless the vehicle is scaled up to compensate — exactly the design tension Starship's oversized structure is built to resolve.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 8/118 · Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)

The partial autocorrelation function answers a sharper version of the question yesterday's autocorrelation function (ACF) asked. ACF measures how strongly a series correlates with its own value k periods ago, full stop. PACF asks the same thing but strips out the "middleman" effect of all the shorter lags in between: it's the correlation between X_t and X_{t-k} that remains after already accounting for X_{t-1} through X_{t-k+1}. Formally, it's defined via a regression of X_t on its own k most recent lagged values, X_t = φk1X_{t-1} + ... + φkkX_{t-k} + εt, where PACF(k) is just the last coefficient in that regression, φkk — the piece of the lag-k relationship that isn't already explained by shorter lags.

Why bother separating "direct" from "indirect" correlation at all? Because it's the standard diagnostic for choosing the order of an autoregressive (AR) model: an AR(p) process's PACF should, in theory, cut off sharply to near-zero after lag p, while its ACF just decays gradually. A common misconception is treating ACF and PACF as redundant or interchangeable diagnostics; in practice they answer different questions and are read together (an ACF that decays slowly alongside a PACF that cuts off sharply at lag 1 is close to a textbook signature of an AR(1) process) — using only one of the two risks misidentifying a series' actual lag structure.
