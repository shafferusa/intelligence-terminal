Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 10/71 · Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes over time — not the rate of motion itself, but the rate at which that motion is speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction. In plain terms: velocity tells you how fast and which way you're going right now; acceleration tells you how quickly that "how fast and which way" is itself changing. It's measured in units of velocity per time — meters per second, per second (m/s²) — which is why the units look doubled: it's a change-of-a-change quantity. A car going a steady 60 mph has zero acceleration even though its velocity is large; a car going from 0 to 60 mph in five seconds has a large acceleration even though, for an instant near the start, its velocity is small.

This builds directly on yesterday's topic, velocity: velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time (how position changes), and acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time (how velocity changes) — the same operation applied one level deeper. Just as velocity can be zero while an object still has position, an object can have significant velocity while its acceleration is zero (constant speed, straight line) — the two quantities are independent at any given instant, which is precisely why physics needs both to fully describe motion.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 10/75 · Payload fraction

Payload fraction is simply the share of a rocket's total liftoff mass that is actually useful cargo — the satellite, crew capsule, or cargo being delivered — expressed as payload mass divided by total (gross) liftoff mass. For most orbital rockets this number is small, often just a few percent, because the overwhelming majority of a rocket's mass at launch is propellant, with structure making up most of the remainder. A higher payload fraction means a more capable, more economical vehicle for a given size; it's one of the single most-cited efficiency metrics when comparing rockets, alongside cost per kilogram to orbit.

This builds directly on yesterday's topic, propellant fraction: propellant fraction (propellant mass over total mass) and payload fraction are two slices of the same pie, competing for the same limited liftoff-mass budget alongside structural mass. Because the rocket equation shows that reaching orbital velocity requires an enormous propellant fraction, the leftover fraction available for structure and payload combined is small — and every kilogram saved in structural mass (lighter tanks, engines, avionics) becomes, one-for-one, a kilogram that can instead go to payload. This is the central engineering tension of rocket design: propellant fraction and payload fraction are always fighting over the same remaining mass budget.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 10/118 · Autoregressive Model AR(p)

An autoregressive model says something intuitive: today's value is a weighted sum of its own recent past, plus some unpredictable noise. Formally, Xt = Σj=1p φjXt-j + εt — the value at time t depends on the last p values of the same series (each with its own weight φj), plus a white-noise error term εt that captures everything the model doesn't explain. The "p" is the model's order: how many lags back it looks. An AR(1) model says today depends only on yesterday; an AR(5) says today depends on a weighted blend of the last five periods.

This builds on yesterday's topic, the AR(1) model, which is just this same equation with p=1 — AR(p) is the direct generalization, allowing persistence to be modeled over a longer memory window rather than just one lag back. AR(p) models are a workhorse for modeling persistence in financial returns, interest rates, and volatility: a security whose returns are positively autocorrelated (momentum-like) will show positive φ coefficients, while mean-reverting series show negative ones.

A real-world tie-in from today's own data: Thursday's session-wide, still-unexplained financials weakness (GS, C, IBKR, COIN, KKR, BX all down 2%+ together) is exactly the kind of short-horizon co-movement an AR(p) model on the sector's own returns could help characterize — if the group's returns show significant positive autocorrelation at lag 1, that's consistent with the move having some persistence (worth watching today's open for confirmation or reversal); if not, it's more consistent with an isolated shock. A common misconception: a high R² or "good fit" AR(p) model on past returns does not mean the model can reliably predict tomorrow's return — financial returns are notoriously close to white noise at short horizons, and AR(p) models are far more useful for characterizing volatility and risk persistence than for direct price forecasting.
