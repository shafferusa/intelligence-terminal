Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 12/71 · Newton's Laws

This morning introduced Newton's three laws: an object at rest or in uniform motion stays that way unless acted on by a net force (first law, inertia); force equals mass times acceleration, F = ma (second law); every action has an equal and opposite reaction (third law). Tonight, deepen the second law specifically, since it is the one that does the most quantitative work. F = ma is really a statement about acceleration as the response variable: for a fixed force, a more massive object accelerates less — mass is a measure of an object's resistance to having its motion changed, not just "how much stuff" it contains. Rearranged as a = F/m, the law says acceleration is directly proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass; double the force and acceleration doubles, double the mass and acceleration halves, holding the other fixed.

A common misconception is that force is required to keep something moving at constant velocity — Newton's first law says exactly the opposite: constant velocity requires zero net force, and the reason everyday objects seem to need a continuous push is that friction (a real opposing force) is quietly present and must be countered, not that motion itself "runs down." The real-world tie-in for tonight: this report's own Section 11 space coverage discussed thrust-to-weight ratio yesterday and today (tomorrow's spaceflight lesson deepens it) — that ratio is a direct application of F = ma, comparing the rocket engine's thrust force against the vehicle's own weight (gravity's force on its mass) to determine whether net upward acceleration is even possible at liftoff.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 12/75 · Thrust-to-Weight Ratio

This morning introduced thrust-to-weight ratio (T/W): a rocket's engine thrust divided by its total weight (mass times local gravitational acceleration) at a given moment. A T/W ratio greater than 1 at liftoff is a hard requirement for a vehicle to leave the pad under its own power — below 1, the engines simply cannot out-push gravity, no matter how efficient they are. Tonight, deepen the concept: T/W is not a fixed number for a given rocket, it changes continuously through flight, because propellant mass burns away while thrust (for a fixed engine at a given altitude) stays roughly constant or even rises slightly as the vehicle climbs into thinner air. This means a rocket's T/W ratio is lowest and most critical at the moment of liftoff, when the vehicle is at its heaviest, and climbs steadily as tanks empty — by the time a rocket like Falcon 9's first stage is near burnout, its T/W ratio can exceed 3 or 4, producing rapidly increasing acceleration that vehicle designers must actively manage (often by throttling down mid-flight) to avoid excessive aerodynamic and structural stress, a phase called "max-Q throttling."

A common misconception is that a higher T/W ratio is always better — in practice, engineers target the minimum T/W that reliably clears the pad and climbs through the atmosphere efficiently, because every unit of extra thrust capability generally costs extra engine mass, complexity, and propellant flow rate, all of which eat into payload capacity. Today's news tie-in: yesterday's Long March 7A failure occurred roughly 83–85 seconds after liftoff during the joint first-stage/booster burn — precisely the phase where T/W ratio is climbing fastest and structural loads from the interaction between thrust, aerodynamic drag, and vehicle mass are most demanding, which is consistent with (though it does not confirm) the shared-engine, propulsion-related failure theory reporters are tracking.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 12/118 · Moving Average Model MA(q)

This morning introduced the MA(q) model: Xt = μ + εt + θ1εt-1 + … + θqεt-q, where today's value depends on the current shock plus a weighted sum of the past q shocks — not on past observed values directly, which is what distinguishes it from an AR model (row 11, covered two days ago). Tonight, deepen the intuition: an MA(q) model is a natural fit whenever a single piece of news has an effect that lingers for a few periods and then cleanly disappears, rather than persisting indefinitely. The order q is literally "how many periods of memory the shock has" — an MA(1) model's today is affected by yesterday's shock but not the day before; an MA(q) extends that memory to q periods and then hard-cuts it to zero.

Today's actual market data offers a clean illustration: the alt-asset-manager rally (KKR, APO, ARES, BX) is a textbook shock-with-lingering-effect story — Nvidia's $500B financing announcement (the shock, ε) hit Monday, and its effect visibly carried into today's session (a "lag-1" effect, θ1εt-1) rather than fully resolving in a single day. An MA model would treat tomorrow's expected return contribution from this specific news item as θ2 times today's already-decayed remainder, presumably smaller than today's own effect, and expect it to vanish entirely after q periods — a genuinely testable, falsifiable prediction, unlike vaguer "the news is still being digested" narration. A common misconception: MA(q) models are not moving averages in the everyday sense of smoothing past prices; the "moving average" here averages past forecast errors (shocks), not past price levels, which is a frequent source of confusion for newcomers to time-series notation.
