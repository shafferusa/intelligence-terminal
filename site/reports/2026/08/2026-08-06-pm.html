Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 9/71 · Velocity (deepened)

This morning introduced velocity as the rate of change of position — the first quantity in this sequence built entirely out of yesterday's topic. The key equation to hold onto is the instantaneous form, v(t) = dx/dt: velocity at any instant is the slope of the position-versus-time curve at that instant, not an average over some longer interval. The distinction between average velocity (Δx/Δt over a finite interval) and instantaneous velocity (the limit as that interval shrinks to zero) is the conceptual bridge from algebra-based motion problems into calculus-based ones, and it is exactly why an x-vs-t graph's slope is more than a convenient visualization — it is the definition. Velocity is also a vector, inheriting position's directionality: an object's speed (the vector's magnitude) can be constant while its velocity changes continuously, which is exactly what happens in uniform circular motion, a case this sequence returns to explicitly once acceleration is introduced.

A real-world tie-in: a car's speedometer displays speed, not velocity — it has no notion of direction, which is why two cars can have identical speedometer readings while approaching a head-on collision, a distinction that matters enormously to collision-avoidance systems, which must track full velocity vectors (including direction) rather than speed alone. The common misconception this sets up for tomorrow's topic: students often assume zero velocity means zero acceleration, but the two are independent instant-by-instant quantities — a ball thrown straight up has zero velocity at the peak of its flight while its acceleration (gravity) remains a constant −9.8 m/s² throughout, never zero. That gap, between "not moving right now" and "not accelerating right now," is exactly where this sequence heads next.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 9/75 · Propellant fraction (deepened)

This morning framed propellant fraction as the fraction of a rocket's total wet mass that is propellant: PF = mpropellant / m0, distinct from yesterday's propellant mass fraction discussion in that it is expressed relative to the vehicle's starting mass rather than its structural mass alone — a subtlety worth being precise about, since spaceflight literature uses several closely related "mass fraction" definitions interchangeably. The key relationship to hold onto: combined with the rocket equation, propellant fraction directly caps a stage's achievable Δv for a given exhaust velocity, since m0/mf = 1/(1 − PF) — the same nonlinear blow-up near PF = 1 that makes every kilogram of structural mass expensive in Δv terms.

Today's real-world tie-in connects to this report's own coverage: SpaceX's staggered share-lockup structure, unlocking roughly 20% of insider shares today rather than the entire float at once, is a deliberate engineering-style tradeoff of a different kind — trading a single large, risky "burn" (the whole float at once) for a metered release, much as a multi-stage rocket trades one enormous single-stage propellant fraction for several smaller stages, each discarding dead mass once its propellant is spent, to achieve a total Δv no single stage could reach alone. A common misconception worth correcting: a higher propellant fraction is not automatically the "better" design choice in isolation, because it says nothing about exhaust velocity, the rocket equation's other lever; real vehicle design trades the two rather than maximizing either independently — the same principle Wednesday's discussion of Starship's stainless-steel structural choices illustrated in practice.

Quant/ML 9/118 · Weak (Covariance) Stationarity Conditions (deepened)

This morning defined weak stationarity: a series is weakly stationary if its mean and variance stay constant over time and its autocovariance at any lag k depends only on k, never on the specific time t. The equation below states this precisely — three conditions that must all hold simultaneously, not just one of them, for a series to qualify.

Today's real-world tie-in uses this report's own data: SPCX's daily closing-price series has shown three sharply different single-day moves in three sessions this week (a fresh post-IPO low Tuesday, a further fresh low Wednesday, then +6.14% today) — a pattern of large, changing-magnitude swings that is a textbook symptom of a non-stationary variance, one of the three conditions above failing even if the mean condition happens to hold. Raw price levels for almost any equity are non-stationary for an obvious reason (the mean trends over time as the company grows or shrinks), which is exactly why this sequence's first lesson (Simple Return and Log Return, topic 1) converts prices into returns before any of the stationarity machinery becomes useful — returns are much closer to stationary than prices, though as SPCX's own week illustrates, even returns can display non-constant (time-varying) variance, a phenomenon called volatility clustering that later topics in this sequence address directly. A common misconception worth naming: "stationary" does not mean "the series doesn't move" — a weakly stationary series can be highly volatile, it just needs that volatility (variance) to be constant on average across time rather than itself trending or clustering, a subtlety SPCX's own week-of-earnings-driven swings makes concrete rather than abstract.
