The Ledger Was Invented to Settle a Problem of Trust, Not Arithmetic

Wednesday, August 19, 2026 · 26 min read

A Venetian investor who financed a spice voyage never saw the storm, the bazaar, or the sale — only the returning captain’s account of what happened, and the entire discipline of accounting exists to make that account provable rather than merely believed.

Accounting

Day 3 of 150 · Accounting 1 of 18 · Foundations

Sometime in the twelfth or thirteenth century, a Venetian merchant with money but no interest in a long sea voyage would sit down with a ship’s captain who had exactly the opposite problem — nerve and seamanship, but no capital — and sign a contract called a commenda. The investor, called the stans (“the one who stays”), put up the money. The traveling merchant, the tractor, took the goods, the ship, and the risk of the open sea, sailed to Alexandria or Constantinople or the Black Sea coast, traded Venetian wool and glass for pepper, silk, and dye, and came home — if he came home — a year or more later.

Picture a specific one. The stans puts up 300 ducats. The tractor loads a ship with cloth, sails for Alexandria, and returns the following season with a hold full of pepper and cinnamon, which he sells in the Rialto market for a total of 620 ducats. Under the most common version of this contract, the stans gets his 300 ducats back first, and the remaining 320 ducats of profit is split three-quarters to the stans and one-quarter to the tractor, because the stans bore the entire risk of losing his capital — to storms, pirates, or a bad market — while the tractor risked only his own time and labor. That split sounds simple. Now ask the question that actually matters: how does the stans know the cargo really sold for 620 ducats?

He wasn’t there. He never smelled the cinnamon, never haggled in the Alexandria bazaar, never watched the ship ride out a squall off Crete. Everything he knows about the voyage — what was bought, what was lost, what it sold for, what it cost to get it home — comes from one source: the tractor’s own account of it, delivered after the fact, from a man who stands to keep a larger cut of the profit if he can make it seem the loss along the way was larger than it was, or the true selling price lower. This gap — between what genuinely happened to money and goods, and what the person who wasn’t there can actually know about it — is not a medieval curiosity. It is, almost word for word, the situation of a shareholder who has never set foot in the company whose stock they hold, a bank that will never personally supervise the business it lent money to, a government taxing income it did not watch anyone earn. Accounting was not invented to do arithmetic; arithmetic already existed, and had for millennia. It was invented to close this particular gap, and everything that follows in this lesson — the strange, symmetrical rule at the center of the whole system, the different numbers it produces for the same business, the entire question of who a financial statement is actually written for — falls out of that one problem.

The idea, before any notation

Start with the word itself. “Account” descends from the Old French aconter, “to reckon,” which comes from the Latin computare, “to sum up.” But Old French aconter carried a second meaning that modern French later split into two separate words — compter, to count, and conter, to tell a story. In the century the commenda contract was common, those were not yet two ideas. To render an account was to count and to tell, in the same breath, because the whole point of the exercise was to turn a private set of facts — what actually happened on a voyage nobody else witnessed — into a story someone else could check. “Audit” makes the same point from the listener’s side: it comes from the Latin audire, “to hear,” because for centuries an audit was literally that — the steward stood before an official and read the account aloud, and the official heard it, listening for numbers that didn’t add up or a story that didn’t hang together. Both words assume, at the root, that whoever is telling the account is not the only person who gets to decide whether it is believed.

That assumption is the whole discipline in miniature. Accounting does two jobs, and it is worth naming them separately because they are easy to blur together. The first is stewardship: proving what happened to resources you were given control over but did not personally own — the tractor’s job, and, seven hundred years later, the job of a company’s management reporting to shareholders who funded the business but do not run it day to day. The second is measurement: taking a messy, continuous stream of real activity — goods bought here, sold there, paid for late, partly spoiled, promised but not yet delivered — and compressing it into one clean number, “profit,” precise enough to divide fairly or judge honestly. These two jobs are related but not identical, and a fair amount of what makes accounting hard, then and now, is that a measurement choice which is perfectly defensible on its own terms can still make the stewardship story easier to shade. You will see exactly that tension in the worked examples below.

Here is the detail almost every first encounter with this subject skips past, and it is the actual point of today’s lesson: an account is never written for the person who already knows what happened. The tractor does not need his own ledger to know whether the voyage went well — he was there. The account exists entirely for the stans, the person who wasn’t. Generalize that one observation and you have the answer to a question that sounds bureaucratic but isn’t: who is a financial statement actually for? Not, in the first instance, the company’s own managers — they already know what happened inside their own walls, the same way the tractor knew what happened at sea. It is for the people who have a claim on the business but were not there to watch it get earned: shareholders and potential buyers of the stock deciding whether to hand over their savings; lenders deciding whether to hand over a loan, and at what interest rate, given how risky the business looks on paper; tax authorities who need a number to apply a rate to, whether or not they trust the taxpayer’s honesty; regulators checking whether a bank or insurer holds enough capital to survive a bad year; and, more quietly, the company’s own employees, trying to judge from the outside whether the employer that pays them is actually stable. The modern accounting rulemaker in the United States, the Financial Accounting Standards Board, states this almost exactly: the purpose of general-purpose financial reporting is to provide information useful to existing and potential investors, lenders, and other creditors in deciding whether to give the business their money. That is the stans, formalized into a standing objective seven centuries later. The audience was never really in doubt; what changed is that today there are millions of stans-equivalents, most of whom will never meet a single executive at the companies whose stock sits in their retirement account, and the entire architecture of modern financial reporting exists to give every one of them the same trustworthy account a Venetian investor once had to take almost entirely on faith.

The commenda itself is older than any of this formal machinery, and the system that eventually made its numbers trustworthy took centuries to arrive, not decades. The earliest known ledger kept in full double-entry form belongs to a branch of the Farolfi merchant company in Salon-de-Provence, recorded by a bookkeeper named Amatino Manucci around 1299 and 1300 — a working merchant’s ledger, not a textbook. Roughly four decades later, the government of Genoa itself was keeping its public accounts — the treasury records known as the massari accounts — in what historians studying the surviving 1340 ledgers describe as already perfect double-entry form, applied not to one merchant’s private voyage but to an entire city-state’s revenue and debt; a Genoese law passed in 1327 had already required public accounts to be kept “by the method of the bankers,” meaning the practice was established enough by then to be written into statute. The system, in other words, was already old and already trusted enough to run a government’s books more than a century and a half before anyone published a description of how it worked. That publication finally came in 1494, when a Franciscan friar and mathematician named Luca Pacioli included a twenty-seven-page section, Particularis de Computis et Scripturis, inside a much larger mathematics textbook, the Summa de Arithmetica, Geometria, Proportioni et Proportionalita, printed in Venice. Pacioli did not invent double-entry bookkeeping — he said so himself, calling it simply “the method of Venice,” a practice merchants there had used for generations. What he did was write it down clearly enough, in a book that the newly invented printing press could copy by the hundreds, that a scattered set of local merchant customs became one teachable, exportable system almost overnight by the standards of the age. Within decades it had been translated and copied across Europe, and by the time double-entry crossed the Atlantic it looked, mechanically, almost exactly like the version taught in the formalism section below.

Building the formal system

The first formal object accounting needs is a way to say, at any moment, what a business actually owns and who has a claim on it. Everything a business controls — cash, inventory, a building, money customers owe it — is an asset. Every claim an outsider has against those assets — a loan not yet repaid, a supplier bill not yet paid — is a liability. Whatever is left over once every outside claim is subtracted belongs to the owners; that remainder is equity. Because equity is defined as whatever is left after liabilities are subtracted from assets, one relationship is true of every business, in every period, without exception:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Assets
everything of value the business controls: cash, inventory, equipment, amounts owed to it by others
Liabilities
everything the business owes to outsiders: loans, unpaid bills, wages earned but not yet paid
Equity
the owners’ residual claim — what is left once every outside claim on the assets is subtracted

This is called the accounting equation, and it is less a formula to plug numbers into than a constraint the whole system is built to protect. Every single transaction a business makes has to leave this equation balanced — not because reality has some hidden two-sided structure, but because the people who developed double-entry bookkeeping deliberately built the equation’s stubbornness into the recording process itself, as a built-in error check. If a clerk records a transaction and the two sides of the equation no longer match, something was written down wrong, full stop — there is no third possibility. That single property is why double-entry bookkeeping caught mistakes and outright fraud for seven hundred years before anyone had a calculator, let alone software: an error rarely announces itself, but an imbalance always does.

The mechanism that enforces this is the part most people find needlessly confusing on first contact, and it doesn’t have to be: every transaction is recorded as at least one debit and at least one credit, of equal size, and “debit” and “credit” are nothing more than the names for the left side and right side of a ledger entry — not, despite how the words are used in everyday speech, “bad” and “good,” or “decrease” and “increase.” What a debit or credit does to an account’s balance depends entirely on what kind of account it is:

Assets & Expenses: a debit increases the balance, a credit decreases it.
Liabilities, Equity & Revenue: a credit increases the balance, a debit decreases it.

debit
an entry on the left side of a ledger account
credit
an entry on the right side of a ledger account

Buy 10,000 ducats of inventory with cash, and you debit Inventory (an asset going up) and credit Cash (a different asset going down) — two debits or two credits never appear alone, because the whole point is that every transaction is described from two angles at once, and the accounting equation stays balanced by construction rather than by luck. This is also exactly why a bank statement calling your deposit a “credit” and your withdrawal a “debit” is not the bank being inconsistent with what you just learned: from the bank’s own books, the money you deposited is not an asset to them, it is a liability — money they now owe back to you — and a liability account increases with a credit, exactly as the rule above says. The word never changed meaning. Whose books you are standing on did.

The second piece of machinery accounting needs is a decision about when an event counts, and this is where the stewardship problem and the measurement problem tangle together most visibly. The simplest option is cash-basis accounting: count a sale when the cash actually arrives, count a cost when the cash actually leaves. It is intuitive, and it is exactly how most individuals track their own personal finances, and it is allowed for very small businesses. But it has a serious flaw once a business is large enough, or has enough outside owners, for the stewardship problem to really bite: it lets the timing of cash movements — which a manager can often delay or accelerate somewhat at will — distort the picture of how the business is actually performing. Accrual accounting fixes that by counting a sale the moment the business has done what it promised — delivered the goods, performed the service — and earned the right to be paid, whether or not cash has actually changed hands yet. Costs are matched to the same period as the revenue they helped produce, a rule with its own name:

Net income = Revenue − Expenses, both recognized in the period they belong to, not the period cash happens to move

Revenue
value earned by delivering goods or services, recognized when earned
Expenses
costs matched to the same period as the revenue they helped generate
Net income
the accrual-basis measure of profit for a period

This is the matching principle, and set against it is a second, entirely different, entirely correct number: cash flow, which simply tracks money physically moving in and out, on whatever schedule it actually moves. Net income and cash flow are not rival estimates of the same underlying truth, one of which must be more accurate than the other. They are answers to two different questions — how much value did the business create this period, versus how much cash changed hands this period — and a business can give a wildly different answer to each one in the very same month, which is precisely what the second worked example below is built to show. Once a company is large enough to file audited statements, or to sell shares to the public, accrual accounting stops being a choice: U.S. accounting standards require it, for exactly the reason the matching principle exists — without it, a manager under pressure to look good this quarter could simply delay paying bills or rush cash collections, changing nothing about how the business is actually performing while changing everything about how it appears to.

Doing it with real numbers

Three worked examples: settling the commenda’s profit split, watching accrual accounting and cash flow disagree inside the same set of transactions, and using a ratio to compare two businesses of wildly different size on equal footing.

Worked example 1 · splitting the commenda’s profit

The stans invested 300 ducats. The tractor returns and reports total sale proceeds of 620 ducats for the cargo. Under the unilateral commenda — investor supplies all the capital, traveling merchant supplies none — profit is split three-quarters to the stans, one-quarter to the tractor, and the stans absorbs the entire loss if the voyage fails.

  1. Return the stans’s original capital first, since that is not profit, only the recovery of what he put in: 620 − 300 = 320 ducats remaining.
  2. That 320 ducats is the profit to be split. Stans’s three-quarter share: 320 × 0.75 = 240 ducats. Tractor’s one-quarter share: 320 × 0.25 = 80 ducats. Check: 240 + 80 = 320, the full profit accounted for.
  3. Total return to the stans: his 300 ducats of capital back, plus his 240-ducat profit share, for 540 ducats on a 300-ducat stake — an 80% return, for a voyage that likely took the better part of a year and could just as easily have ended at the bottom of the Aegean.
  4. Now the question that matters more than the arithmetic: every number above depends on one unverified claim — that the cargo actually sold for 620 ducats. If the tractor sold it for 700 and quietly kept 80 for himself before rendering his account, the stans would see an entirely plausible, profitable-looking voyage — 540 ducats back on 300 invested — and have no way whatsoever to know he had actually been shorted 60 ducats of his rightful three-quarter share. Nothing in the arithmetic itself flags the fraud, because the arithmetic is only ever as honest as the number fed into it.

That fourth step is the entire reason double-entry ledgers, witnessed accounts, and eventually independent auditors developed at all: not because merchants suddenly got worse at addition, but because a single unverifiable number at the top of a calculation can make every subsequent step look correct while still being wrong. Worth noting, too, that Venetian merchants had a second version of this same contract, the bilateral commenda, in which the traveling partner put up a third of the capital himself and the investor two-thirds, with profits then split evenly rather than three-to-one; the split changed with how much of the risk the tractor was actually carrying, which is itself an early, informal version of a very modern idea — that the return someone is entitled to should track the risk they are actually bearing, not just the effort they put in.

Worked example 2 · why profit and cash can disagree inside the same month

A shop buys 10,000 dollars of inventory in December on 30-day credit from its supplier — no cash changes hands yet. It sells the entire lot in December for 15,000 dollars, also on 30-day credit — the customer will pay in January. Work through the actual ledger entries, then compute December’s profit under accrual accounting and December’s cash flow, and compare.

  1. The December purchase, recorded as a debit and a credit of equal size, exactly as the formalism section requires: debit Inventory 10,000 (an asset increasing), credit Accounts Payable 10,000 (a liability increasing, since the shop now owes its supplier). The accounting equation stays balanced — assets and liabilities both rose by 10,000, so the gap between them, equity, is untouched.
  2. The December sale is recorded in two linked entries. First, the revenue: debit Accounts Receivable 15,000 (an asset increasing — the customer now owes the shop), credit Sales Revenue 15,000 (a revenue account, which increases equity). Second, the matching cost, following the matching principle from the formalism above: debit Cost of Goods Sold 10,000 (an expense, which decreases equity), credit Inventory 10,000 (the asset leaving the shelves).
  3. Accrual revenue for December: 15,000 dollars, recognized because the shop delivered the goods and earned the right to be paid. Accrual cost matched to that same revenue: 10,000 dollars. December net income: 15,000 − 10,000 = 5,000 dollars of profit.
  4. December cash flow: zero. None of the four ledger entries above touched the Cash account at all — not a single dollar physically moved in or out of the shop’s bank account all month, because the purchase was on credit and so was the sale.
  5. January: the customer pays the 15,000 dollars owed (debit Cash 15,000, credit Accounts Receivable 15,000), and the shop pays its supplier the 10,000 dollars owed (debit Accounts Payable 10,000, credit Cash 10,000). January’s cash flow: +15,000 − 10,000 = +5,000 dollars. The same 5,000 dollars of value shows up eventually — just an entire month later than the profit that predicted it.

Both numbers are correct. December’s income statement is right to say the shop earned 5,000 dollars of profit that month. December’s bank balance is right to say the shop had zero additional cash to spend that month. If the shop had a 6,000-dollar payroll due on December 31st, a genuinely profitable month would not have covered a single paycheck — the accrual figure and the number the shop could actually spend were two entirely different quantities, and the gap between them, not any error in either one, is what this worked example exists to demonstrate.

Worked example 3 · a ratio that ignores size entirely

A small independent bakery reports current assets of 20,000 dollars and current liabilities of 10,000 dollars. A national retail chain, thousands of times larger, reports current assets of 200,000,000 dollars and current liabilities of 100,000,000 dollars. Compute the current ratio — a standard measure of short-term financial health — for each, and compare.

  1. Current ratio is defined as current assets divided by current liabilities — a business’s short-term resources measured against its short-term obligations.
  2. Bakery: 20,000 / 10,000 = 2.0.
  3. Retail chain: 200,000,000 / 100,000,000 = 2.0.
  4. The two businesses differ in scale by a factor of 10,000, and yet the ratio — the actual measure of short-term financial health this number exists to convey — is identical for both. A lender or investor reading these two statements learns, from this one number alone, that both businesses cover their near-term obligations twice over, with no need to know or care that one of them is a single storefront and the other operates in every state.

Financial ratios exist precisely to do this: strip away scale so that businesses of any size can be judged on the same footing, by expressing a relationship between two numbers rather than either number on its own. It is worth being honest about the limit of that trick, too — a ratio of 2.0 says nothing about what those current assets actually are. A bakery holding 20,000 dollars mostly as cash is in a very different position from one holding 20,000 dollars mostly as flour that is a week from spoiling, and the current ratio alone cannot tell the two apart. Stripping away scale is genuinely useful, but it is not the same as telling the whole story, and no single ratio in this subject ever is.

Where this already shows up

The clearest modern descendant of the tractor’s account is a public company’s own filings with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission — the 10-K filed once a year, the 10-Q filed each quarter. This intelligence terminal itself leans on that exact machinery every time it checks whether a company is genuinely publicly traded: the entity registry in this system is verified, when in doubt, against `data.sec.gov`, the same federal repository these filings live in. A 10-K is, structurally, nothing but the tractor’s account scaled up by seven centuries and several orders of magnitude — a report rendered by people who ran a business day to day, addressed to shareholders and creditors who did not, required by law to be checked by an outside auditor with no stake in the outcome before it can be published at all. The stans once had to trust one tractor he presumably knew personally. A shareholder today trusts a filing prepared by executives they have never met, on the strength of an audit process built, brick by brick, out of the same stewardship logic this lesson opened with.

The double-entry mechanism itself never really left, either. Every major piece of accounting software in use today — whether it is running a five-person bakery or a multinational retailer — still enforces debits and credits under the hood, the same seven-hundred-year-old error check from the formalism section, just executed by a database instead of a clerk with a quill. And the ratio from the third worked example is not a classroom curiosity: bank loan agreements routinely write a minimum current ratio, or a similar debt-to-equity limit, directly into the contract as a condition of the loan, meaning a business can technically default not by missing a payment but simply by letting a ratio computed straight off its own balance sheet fall below a number a lender wrote into the paperwork — a modern echo of the commenda contract specifying exactly how a shortfall gets divided before the voyage ever sails.

Not every number attached to a company’s earnings has gone through this same discipline, and the difference matters for exactly the audience this lesson has been naming throughout. Public companies routinely publish, alongside their audited GAAP figures, a second set of “adjusted” or “non-GAAP” numbers — income excluding a one-time charge, or “adjusted EBITDA” excluding items management judges non-representative of the underlying business. Those adjusted figures are not run through the accounting equation the way GAAP net income is; they do not have to balance against a corresponding balance-sheet entry, and no outside auditor is required to sign off on the specific adjustments the way one must sign off on the audited statements themselves. That does not automatically make an adjusted number dishonest — sometimes it genuinely gives a clearer read on the underlying business than a GAAP figure temporarily distorted by a one-time write-off. But it does mean the two kinds of number are trustworthy for different reasons and to different degrees, and a reader who cannot tell which one a headline is actually quoting has quietly lost the protection this lesson’s formal system was built, over seven centuries, to provide.

The same equation works at a much smaller scale than a company balance sheet, closer to home than a 10-K. A personal net-worth statement — the kind a bank asks for on a mortgage application — is nothing but the accounting equation solved for equity: assets (a house, a car, a retirement account, cash in the bank) minus liabilities (a mortgage balance, a car loan, credit card debt) equals net worth, the personal version of shareholders’ equity. A credit score exists for exactly the reason a loan covenant does two paragraphs above: a bank extending a mortgage is, structurally, standing in the stans’s position, handing over capital to someone it cannot personally supervise and relying on a reported account — pay stubs, tax returns, a credit history — to judge whether the loan will actually be repaid. None of this requires a company, a ledger clerk, or a Venetian ship at all. It requires only a resource controlled by one party and a claim held by another who wasn’t there to watch it managed, which is the entire condition this lesson opened with, on a Venetian dock, seven hundred years ago, in miniature.

Two accounting rulebooks now govern most of the world’s public companies — U.S. GAAP, set by the same Financial Accounting Standards Board quoted earlier, and IFRS, used across most of Europe, much of Asia, and elsewhere — and while they disagree on a long list of technical details, both are built from the identical foundation this lesson has been teaching: the accounting equation, double-entry recording, accrual measurement, and an audience of outside investors and creditors who were not there to watch the business being run. That agreement on fundamentals is itself a kind of proof of the argument this lesson opened with. Two standard-setters working on opposite sides of an ocean, for different economies with different legal traditions, independently converged on the same core machinery to solve the same underlying trust problem, because the problem — someone who wasn’t there needing to trust someone who was — does not change no matter which country writes the rulebook.

The mistake almost everyone makes

The central misconception is the one Worked Example 2 was built to demolish: the assumption that a profitable business cannot run out of money. It is an entirely reasonable-sounding assumption, and it is wrong often enough, and expensively enough, that “profitable but out of cash” is one of the most common ways real businesses actually fail. The shop in that example was genuinely, correctly profitable in December by every accounting rule that governs the word “profit” — and genuinely, completely unable to make payroll that same month, because profit measures value earned, not cash on hand, and the two are only guaranteed to line up eventually, never immediately. A business that grows quickly by selling more and more on credit can show rising profit every single month while its cash position quietly deteriorates, because faster sales growth on credit terms means an ever-larger gap between revenue recognized and cash collected — a pattern real enough that accountants have a specific, unflattering name for a company that keeps reporting profit while running low on cash: growing broke. It is exactly why a serious reading of any company’s financial statements never stops at the income statement; the cash flow statement, tracking the same period from the opposite angle, is what catches the gap the profit figure alone cannot show.

Even the word “eventually” in that account is doing more work than it first appears to. Worked Example 2 assumed the December customer pays the full 15,000 dollars owed in January, but a promise to pay is not the same thing as a payment, and some fraction of every business’s customers never pay at all. Accountants handle that risk with an estimate called an allowance for doubtful accounts — a deliberate reduction of the receivable, based on historical experience, that quietly admits the accrual figure is a claim on future cash, not a guarantee of it. If the shop’s December customer eventually defaults on half the bill, the 15,000 dollars of revenue recognized that month was correctly recognized under the rule that recognizes it, and the shop still only ever collects 7,500 dollars in cash — a second, smaller version of exactly the profit-versus-cash gap this section has been building toward, now driven not by timing but by a promise that was never fully kept.

A smaller, related error belongs here too, because Worked Example 2’s neighbor in the formalism section invites it directly: treating “debit” as a synonym for “decrease” or “bad,” full stop, rather than checking which kind of account is actually involved. A debit decreases a liability account and increases an asset account — both true statements, both “debit,” opposite effects, because the rule was never about good news or bad news. It was always about which side of the ledger, for which type of account, and the everyday meanings of the words “debit” and “credit” — a debit card takes money away, a credit to your account feels like a gift — are exactly backwards from how neutral, technical, and purely positional the two words actually are inside the system that gave them their name. The fix is the same one from Worked Example 2’s ledger entries: before deciding whether a debit is welcome news, ask what kind of account it landed in.

What today points toward

Yesterday’s lesson spent real effort on one particular fact about angles: that they measure a fraction of a turn, completely independent of how large the circle is — sweep the same angle across a dinner plate and a wedding ring and you have turned through an identical amount, even though the two arcs traced are wildly different lengths. The current ratio in this lesson’s third worked example is doing precisely that trick with money instead of geometry. A bakery with 20,000 dollars of current assets and a retail chain with 200,000,000 dollars are, in absolute terms, incomparable — but divide each business’s short-term resources by its short-term obligations, exactly the way yesterday’s lesson divided an arc length by a radius, and the size difference vanishes entirely, leaving behind a single number that measures the same underlying thing regardless of how big the business generating it happens to be. Different subject, same machinery: whenever a raw quantity is too tangled up with scale to compare honestly, the fix is very often the same one — stop looking at the quantity, and look at its ratio to something else instead.

Where this leaves you
  • You can name accounting’s two separate jobs — stewardship and measurement — and say who a financial statement is actually written for: not the people who already know what happened, but the people who have a claim on a business and weren’t there to watch it get earned.
  • You know the accounting equation, Assets = Liabilities + Equity, and that debit and credit are positional terms whose effect on a balance depends entirely on the type of account involved, not a built-in judgment of good or bad.
  • You can explain why accrual profit and cash flow are both correct and routinely disagree within the same period — and why “profitable” is not a guarantee a business can pay its bills this month.
  • You can compute a financial ratio and explain why it strips scale out of a comparison — the same move trigonometry made with angles, applied to a balance sheet instead of a circle.
