Trump signs executive order directing a 90-day review of the childhood vaccine schedule, including a recommendation to split the MMR shot into three doses

Event ~mid-morning ET · Published 2026-08-10 (multiple outlets datelined today) · Last checked 15:50 ET

What happened
President Trump signed an executive order directing HHS to reassess the childhood immunization schedule within 90 days. The order includes a recommendation that the combined MMR (measles-mumps-rubella) shot be administered as three separate doses at separate visits rather than one combined shot, and calls for expanded federal research into vaccines and autism.
Why it matters
It is the most concrete step yet by the administration to act on HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s long-stated position that the current schedule warrants reexamination, and arrives as measles cases nationally are at a 35-year high.
What is confirmed
The order was signed and its stated provisions (90-day HHS review, MMR-split recommendation, autism-research directive) were reported consistently by Washington Post, CNN, CNBC and Axios, all datelined today.
What remains uncertain
Whether HHS’s eventual review changes the ACIP-recommended schedule in practice, what the order means for state school-entry requirements, and the order’s exact legal text (this report has not independently read the primary White House text).
Context
The existing scientific consensus, built on large epidemiological studies over two decades, has found no causal link between vaccines and autism. Outlets note the order arrives against the backdrop of rising measles case counts, citing historical examples (e.g., Samoa’s 2018 outbreak following reduced vaccination coverage) as a cautionary comparison.
Impact
Policy: HHS/ACIP procedural review. Public health: potential effect on vaccination compliance and outbreak risk if uptake falls. Political: sharpens an already-polarized fight over RFK Jr.’s HHS agenda.
What happens next
HHS has 90 days to report back; watch for ACIP schedule changes, state-level responses, and public-health-establishment reaction.

Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 11/71 · Graphs of motion

This morning introduced the two core readings: on a position-vs-time graph, slope is speed; on a velocity-vs-time graph, slope is acceleration and the area under the curve is distance traveled. The equation that ties these together for constant acceleration is the one behind that area calculation: d = v₀t + ½at², where d is displacement, v₀ is the starting velocity, a is the (constant) acceleration, and t is elapsed time. Graphically, that ½at² term is exactly the triangular area you'd shade in above a straight, sloped velocity-vs-time line — the equation and the picture are two descriptions of the same fact.

A common misconception is reading a steep position-time graph as meaning "high acceleration." It doesn't — a steep but straight line means high, constant speed with zero acceleration; acceleration only shows up as curvature in the position graph, or equivalently as nonzero slope in the velocity graph. As a real-world tie-in: a car cruising at a steady 70 mph on the highway traces a straight, steep line on a position-time graph (fast, but not accelerating), while the same car merging onto the highway traces a curving line as its velocity itself changes moment to moment.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 11/75 · Staging

This morning covered why rockets stage at all: dropping spent, empty tank mass the instant it stops being useful, so the vehicle keeps accelerating only the smallest remaining mass. The key design choice inside that idea is how many stages to use and how to split the total velocity budget (Δv) between them. More stages mean less dead weight carried at any given moment, which sounds strictly better — but each added stage also adds its own structure, engines, separation hardware and failure points, so real vehicles converge on two or three stages as the practical sweet spot rather than pushing the theoretical staging-efficiency logic to an extreme.

Today's real-world tie-in is directly overhead: China's Long March 7A failure this morning occurred during the "joint first-stage/booster burn" — the period when the core first stage and its strap-on boosters are firing together, before the boosters separate. That handoff moment, where multiple stage elements are burning and separating in sequence, is a well-known higher-risk phase in staged flight; a common misconception is that failures cluster at the dramatic-looking main-engine-cutoff/stage-separation event itself, when in practice anomalies during the sustained joint-burn phase (structural loads, propellant-feed issues) are at least as common a failure mode.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 11/118 · Autoregressive Model AR(1)

This morning introduced the AR(1) model, Xt = φ1·Xt−1 + εt: today's value is yesterday's value, scaled by φ1, plus fresh noise. Today's tie-in uses this report's own VIX data: the Cboe complex closed Friday at 14.9 and closed at 15.43 today, a modest +3.6% move on a session where the underlying S&P 500 was essentially flat — exactly the kind of small, noisy, partially-persistent daily change an AR(1) fitted to VIX levels (which are far more autocorrelated than equity returns; φ1 for VIX typically sits well above 0.9) would describe reasonably well. That high persistence is also why volatility is judged more forecastable than direction: a high-φ1 series like VIX drifts slowly and mean-reverts, while daily equity returns behave close to φ1 ≈ 0 — near-independent from one day to the next.

The misconception flagged this morning is worth restating with today's example: a high φ1 on VIX does not by itself mean VIX (or products tracking it) is profitably tradeable — VIX futures famously carry a persistent contango roll cost that has eaten most naive long-volatility strategies' theoretical edge, autocorrelation notwithstanding.
