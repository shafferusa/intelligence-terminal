Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 7/71 · Coordinate systems

This morning introduced coordinate systems as the bookkeeping layer underneath every physics problem — a choice of origin and axes that lets a position, velocity, or force be written as numbers instead of a vague description. The most common choice, Cartesian coordinates (x, y, z), works well for straight-line motion and rectangular symmetry, but it is not the only option and often not the best one. Polar coordinates (r, θ) describe a point by its distance from an origin and an angle from a reference direction — the natural choice whenever a problem has rotational or circular symmetry, since a circle centered at the origin is simply "r = constant" instead of the awkward x² + y² = r².

The choice of coordinate system never changes the physics — a ball falls the same way regardless of what grid you draw on the page — but it changes how much algebra you do to describe it. A common misconception is treating this as a cosmetic choice with no real consequence; in practice, picking coordinates aligned with a problem's natural symmetry (radial coordinates for orbits, coordinates along an incline for a ramp problem) can turn a page of trigonometry into a few lines. This is exactly why the rocket equation below is written in terms of velocity change along the rocket's direction of travel, not broken into separate x/y components: the natural coordinate for a rocket accelerating in a straight line is the one axis pointed along its own thrust.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 7/75 · Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

This morning's introduction to the rocket equation, Δv = ve·ln(m0/mf), established the core trade-off: a rocket's total velocity change (Δv) depends on its exhaust velocity (ve, essentially engine efficiency) and the natural logarithm of its mass ratio — starting mass over final, propellant-depleted mass. The logarithm is the equation's least intuitive and most consequential feature: because ln grows so slowly, each additional unit of Δv costs proportionally more propellant than the last. Doubling your mass ratio does not double your Δv; it only adds a fixed increment (ln 2 ≈ 0.69, times ve).

Tonight's real-world tie-in: SpaceX's Falcon 9, which flew twice today (the Starlink Group 17-53 launch, with booster B1063 landing for the 34th time), is a working demonstration of managing this equation's tyranny through reuse rather than raw propellant mass — the booster doesn't need a better mass ratio on every flight if the hardware itself survives to fly again, spreading its effective cost across dozens of missions instead of one. A common misconception is treating Δv as a simple physical speed, like a car's speedometer reading; it's better understood as a rocket's fuel-cost currency, a budget spent in stages (launch, orbit-raising, landing burns) that must each be paid for before the rocket can do anything further.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 7/118 · Autocorrelation Function (ACF)

This morning introduced the autocorrelation function as a way to ask a time series a simple question: does knowing today's value tell you anything about tomorrow's? Formally, ρk = γk / γ0 — the covariance between a series and its own past self at lag k, normalized by the series' own variance so the result always falls between −1 and +1, just like an ordinary correlation coefficient.

Today's actual market data offers a clean illustration of what a high, positive short-lag autocorrelation looks like: today's broad rally (S&P +1.8%) followed Monday's own rally (S&P +1.48%) and Friday's gain before that — a short run of same-direction daily returns, which is exactly the pattern a positive ρ1 would flag if this were a genuine, statistically significant effect rather than three data points. That caveat is the lesson's common misconception: eyeballing a short streak of same-direction days and calling it "momentum" is not the same as measuring a statistically significant ACF value over a long sample. Daily equity returns are famously close to uncorrelated once you control for volatility clustering — a few days moving the same direction is exactly what you'd expect from a fair coin now and then, not proof the market has memory.
