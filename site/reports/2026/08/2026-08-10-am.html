Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 11/71 · Graphs of motion

Motion can be read directly off a graph without ever writing an equation. On a position-vs-time graph, the object's speed at any instant is the steepness (slope) of the line at that point: a flat line means the object is standing still, a steep line means it's moving fast, and a line that curves means the speed itself is changing. On a velocity-vs-time graph, the same idea applies one level up — the slope now shows acceleration, and the area trapped between the line and the time-axis gives the total distance traveled over that interval.

This builds directly on last time's topic, Newton's laws of motion: those laws explain why an object speeds up, slows down, or holds steady, while today's graphs are simply a way of seeing that behavior at a glance. A constant-slope position graph is Newton's first law in picture form (no net force, no change in motion); a curving position graph, or a sloped velocity graph, is the visible signature of a net force at work.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 11/75 · Staging

A rocket rarely flies to orbit as one single vehicle burning one tank of fuel start to finish. Instead it's built in stages — separate sections, each with its own engines and propellant — that fire in sequence and are discarded once spent. The lowest, biggest stage does the hard initial work of lifting the whole stack off the pad and through the thick lower atmosphere; once its propellant is gone, it detaches and falls away, and a smaller upper stage ignites to carry the payload the rest of the way to orbital velocity.

Staging exists because of a brutal fact from the rocket equation covered a few lessons back: every kilogram of empty, spent tank a rocket still has to carry is a kilogram it must also accelerate, wasting propellant on hauling its own dead weight. By dropping each stage's empty structure the moment it's no longer useful, the rocket keeps accelerating the smallest possible remaining mass, which is what makes reaching orbital speeds achievable at all with chemical propellants.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 11/118 · Autoregressive Model AR(1)

An AR(1) model says the simplest useful thing you can say about a time series: today's value is mostly yesterday's value, scaled by a coefficient, plus some fresh randomness. Written out, Xt = φ1·Xt−1 + εt — where φ1 (phi) controls how strongly the past persists into the present, and εt is the unpredictable shock that arrives fresh each period. If φ1 is close to 1, the series has a long memory and drifts slowly; if it's close to 0, each period is nearly independent of the last.

This builds on last time's topic, log returns: analysts rarely fit an AR(1) to raw prices, since prices trend and aren't stationary, but they do fit it to returns or to rate series like Treasury yields, where mean-reverting, persistence-driven behavior is a genuinely useful description. A common misconception is treating a high φ1 as proof that a series is "predictable" in a tradeable sense — a series can be highly autocorrelated (weather, interest rates) without that autocorrelation being large enough, after transaction costs, to trade profitably.
