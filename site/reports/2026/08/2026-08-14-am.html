Event Aug 11 (ruling) · Published Aug 11–12 · Last checked 05:50 ET

What happened
Judge Indira Talwani (D. Mass.) issued a 27-page ruling Aug 11 in League of Women Voters of Massachusetts v. Trump expanding her injunction against the March 31 mail-ballot executive order from the original 23-state-plus-D.C. coalition to nationwide scope, blocking USPS from implementing Section 3 ahead of the Nov. 3 midterms. Context

Impact

What happens next

Sources: SCOTUSblog (primary-adjacent, docket tracking) · Votebeat · Democracy Docket · The Hill · Washington Times (secondary, multiple).

Expanded analysis (collapsed by default)

The split procedural posture — one circuit narrowing a nationwide injunction while a district judge just widened a different one on the same order — is unusual and suggests the underlying legal question (can a single district court bind the government nationwide on election administration months before a vote) is genuinely contested among judges, not just among litigants. That makes a substantive Supreme Court ruling, rather than a narrow procedural stay, more likely to be the meaningful resolution here.

Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 15/71 · Friction

Friction is the resistive force that opposes relative motion (or attempted motion) between two surfaces in contact. It comes in two flavors: static friction, which resists the start of motion between surfaces that are momentarily at rest relative to each other, and kinetic friction, which acts once those surfaces are actually sliding. Counterintuitively, static friction is the stronger of the two — it’s why a heavy box resting on the floor takes a noticeable shove to get moving, but then slides comparatively easily once it’s already in motion. Both are modeled as proportional to the normal force pressing the surfaces together, via a coefficient (μ) that depends on the two materials involved, not on the contact area.

This builds directly on yesterday’s topic, free-body diagrams: friction is simply another force vector to add to the diagram, drawn opposing the direction of motion (kinetic) or opposing the direction an object would move if it weren’t “stuck” (static). Once you can draw the normal force and the applied force correctly, friction is the missing piece that explains why objects don’t slide at the slightest push — and, going back two topics further to Newton’s third law, friction is itself a reaction-pair force: the ground pushes back on your foot exactly because your foot pushes back on the ground, which is the entire reason walking is possible at all.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 15/75 · Max Q

Max Q — short for maximum dynamic pressure — is the point during ascent when a rocket experiences the greatest mechanical stress from the air pushing against its structure. It isn’t the moment of highest speed (the rocket is still relatively slow and low) or highest altitude; it’s the sweet spot, typically around 60–90 seconds into flight, where speed has built up quickly but atmospheric density hasn’t yet thinned out enough to compensate. Dynamic pressure is proportional to air density times velocity squared, so it peaks where the product of a still-fairly-dense atmosphere and rapidly increasing speed is largest, then falls off as the rocket climbs into thinner air even while still accelerating.

This connects directly to yesterday’s topic, drag: Max Q is precisely the moment drag forces (which also scale with dynamic pressure) are at their most punishing on the vehicle’s structure, which is why engines are often throttled back briefly through this phase — not to save fuel, but to reduce speed just enough to keep dynamic pressure, and the structural loads that come with it, within the vehicle’s design limits.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 15/118 · CAPM Alpha Formula

Alpha measures how much better (or worse) an investment actually performed compared to what its risk level alone would predict. The Capital Asset Pricing Model says a stock’s expected return should equal the risk-free rate plus its beta (market sensitivity) times the market’s excess return over the risk-free rate — that expected figure is your baseline. Alpha is simply the investment’s actual return minus that baseline: a positive alpha means the investment did better than its risk profile alone would justify; a negative alpha means it did worse, even if the raw return looked fine.

This builds on yesterday’s topic, the ARMA(p,q) model: where ARMA tries to forecast where a price or return series is headed next based on its own history, alpha is a backward-looking scorecard for whether a strategy’s actual results beat what pure market exposure would have delivered. A common misconception is treating any outperformance as “alpha” — but a portfolio that simply took on more market risk (a beta above 1) and rode a rising market isn’t generating alpha at all, it’s just being appropriately rewarded for extra risk already priced into the CAPM baseline. Today’s own data offers a live illustration: Thursday’s broadly positive breadth (62% of screened stocks advancing) means most of the day’s gains reflect market-wide beta exposure, not stock-picking alpha — a reminder that a rising tide really does lift most boats.
