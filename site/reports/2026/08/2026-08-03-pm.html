Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 6/71 · Vectors

This morning introduced vectors as arrows carrying both magnitude and direction, added tip-to-tail. The key equation for that addition, in components, is simple: if vector A = (Ax, Ay) and vector B = (Bx, By), then A + B = (Ax+Bx, Ay+By) — you add the matching components separately, then the magnitude of the result is √((Ax+Bx)² + (Ay+By)²) by the Pythagorean theorem. That's the formal version of this morning's 3-miles-east-then-4-miles-north example: (3,0) + (0,4) = (3,4), magnitude √(9+16) = 5.

A real-world tie-in from today's own report: a ship's velocity through the Strait of Hormuz (covered in Top Stories) is itself a vector sum — the vessel's engine-driven velocity relative to the water, plus the water current's own velocity relative to the seabed, combine to give its actual track over ground. A missile or drone's flight path (also referenced in today's Russia-Ukraine coverage) works the same way: its velocity is the vector sum of thrust-driven motion and any wind or guidance corrections, which is why intercept geometry is fundamentally a vector-addition problem. A common misconception worth flagging now: subtracting vectors is not the same as subtracting magnitudes. A − B means A + (−B), where −B is B reversed in direction (not just made negative in some abstract sense) — two vectors of equal magnitude pointing in different directions do not cancel to zero unless they point exactly opposite each other.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 6/75 · Exhaust velocity

This morning introduced exhaust velocity (ve) as the speed propellant leaves the nozzle relative to the rocket, and its relationship to specific impulse: ve = Isp × g0, where g0 is standard gravitational acceleration (9.80665 m/s²), used purely as a unit-conversion constant here, not as a statement about gravity's role in the physics. A chemical rocket engine (like the ones powering tomorrow's calendared Falcon 9 and Long March 8A launches) typically produces an exhaust velocity in the range of roughly 3,000–4,500 m/s; an ion or Hall-effect electric thruster can exceed 20,000–30,000 m/s, trading much lower thrust for dramatically better propellant efficiency.

Real-world tie-in: SpaceX's first-ever public earnings print tomorrow (see Top Stories) will include commentary on Starship's Raptor engines, whose full-flow staged-combustion cycle is specifically engineered to push exhaust velocity higher than earlier-generation kerosene-fueled engines by burning methane and liquid oxygen at very high chamber pressure. A common misconception worth flagging now: a higher exhaust velocity does not automatically mean a “more powerful” or higher-thrust engine — thrust depends on both exhaust velocity and propellant mass flow rate (F = ṁ × ve), so a highly efficient electric thruster with tiny mass flow can have excellent exhaust velocity but only a whisper of thrust, useless for lifting off a planet but well-suited to gradual orbit-raising.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 6/118 · Covariance and Correlation

This morning introduced covariance and correlation and the equation Cov(X,Y) = E[(X−μX)(Y−μY)], ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σXσY). Today's own session is an unusually clean illustration of why correlation matters for portfolio construction: this system's own data shows SPY (cap-weighted S&P 500) and RSP (equal-weight S&P 500) diverging sharply on an otherwise highly-correlated pair — a gap this large between two funds that normally track each other tightly is itself informative, because it tells you today's rally is concentrated in a handful of mega-cap names rather than broadly correlated across the index's full membership. When correlation between assets you thought were highly correlated suddenly weakens like this, it is a live example of this morning's misconception warning: correlations estimated from historical data are not fixed constants, and a portfolio built assuming SPY and RSP move in lockstep would have been mispriced for exactly today's kind of session.

The covariance matrix generalizes this pairwise idea to an entire portfolio: for N assets it is an N×N table where the diagonal holds each asset's own variance and the off-diagonal cells hold each pairwise covariance. Portfolio variance itself is computed as wᵀΣw, where w is the vector of portfolio weights and Σ is that covariance matrix — the mathematical foundation the next several topics in this sequence (moving toward portfolio optimization) will build on directly.
