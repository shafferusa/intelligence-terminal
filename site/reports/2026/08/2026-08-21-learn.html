Usain Bolt Peaked at 65 Meters. Everyone Watching Thought He Was Still Speeding Up.

Friday, August 21, 2026 · 26 min read

Usain Bolt’s own split times from the fastest 100 meters ever run show he had already stopped speeding up with thirty meters still to go — a small, well-documented gap between what a race looks like and what a graph of it says, and the starting point for reading any curve of motion correctly, from a sprinter’s blocks to a spacecraft’s descent.

Physics

Day 5 of 150 · Physics 1 of 26 · Foundations

On the evening of August 16, 2009, in Berlin’s Olympiastadion, Usain Bolt ran the fastest 100 meters any human has ever run, in a world championship final, in front of a full stadium and a television audience of hundreds of millions. The number that made every headline the next morning was 9.58 seconds — 0.11 seconds faster than his own world record from the Beijing Olympics exactly a year earlier, the single largest one-time improvement in the 100-meter world record since electronic timing began. It is still the record today, seventeen years later.

What made the coverage in the days afterward interesting, if you go back and read it, was not just the time. It was a specific claim, repeated by commentators, coaches, and more than a few journalists writing it up: that Bolt was still accelerating in the closing meters of the race, that he had crossed the line still gaining speed, and that a longer track would have shown an even more absurd number. It is an intuitive thing to believe. He looked utterly relaxed and utterly dominant all the way to the line, gliding past a world-class field as if they were standing still, and “still speeding up” is the natural way to describe a man who looks like that.

It is also, according to Bolt’s own timing data from that race, not quite what happened. Somewhere in the second half of the run, his speed stopped increasing and very gently began to fall — not enough to be visible to a stadium full of people watching a man run faster than anyone in history, but enough to show up cleanly in the numbers, if you know what to do with them. Finding that moment, and understanding why nobody in the stadium could have seen it happen, is this lesson’s subject: kinematics, the physics of describing motion — position, velocity, and acceleration — and the discipline of reading those three quantities correctly off a graph, rather than off an impression.

The idea, before any notation

Motion has exactly three layers to it, and almost every mistake people make in thinking about motion comes from collapsing two of those layers into one. The first layer is simply where something is — its position, at a given moment, relative to some fixed starting point you get to choose. The second layer is how fast, and in which direction, that position is changing — its velocity. The third layer is how fast the velocity itself is changing — its acceleration. Each layer is built directly out of the one below it: velocity is the rate at which position changes, and acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. Nothing else is added at any step; the same one relationship — “the rate at which the thing below is changing” — is simply applied twice.

The reason this is worth stating so plainly is that everyday language is careless about the difference between the second layer and the third, in a way that causes real, specific errors. “Speeding up” and “going fast” sound like the same idea, spoken with different emphasis. They are not. Going fast is a statement about velocity, layer two — it is a description of where you are right now, in the sense of how quickly your position is currently changing. Speeding up is a statement about acceleration, layer three — it is a description of what your velocity is doing, not what it currently is. A car cruising at a steady 70 miles an hour on a highway is going very fast and accelerating not at all. A car pulling away from a stoplight at 5 miles an hour is going quite slowly and accelerating hard. Bolt, in the last third of his race, was doing almost exactly the first of those two things: still going extraordinarily fast, and no longer speeding up. The eye, watching a man run faster than anyone else on the planet, reads that as continued acceleration, because it has no other frame of reference for what “fast and no longer increasing” is supposed to look like. The numbers do not have that problem.

One more plain-language distinction is worth having before any notation arrives, because it is the single most reliably misremembered fact in this entire subject: a negative acceleration does not mean “slowing down,” on its own, any more than a positive one automatically means “speeding up.” What acceleration actually does is push velocity in a particular direction — and whether that push speeds an object up or slows it down depends on which way the object is already moving, not on the sign of the acceleration by itself. A car moving forward that brakes is slowing down under a negative, backward-pointing acceleration — the everyday case, and the one that gives “negative acceleration” its common misreading as a synonym for braking. But that same backward-pointing acceleration applied to a car already moving backward would speed the car up, not slow it down, because acceleration and velocity would then point the same way instead of opposite ways. The honest rule has nothing to do with the sign of acceleration in isolation: an object speeds up when its acceleration points the same direction as its velocity, and slows down when the two point in opposite directions, whichever direction anyone has labeled “positive.” Keep that rule in reserve; the misconception section below is built around a case where it is easy to get backward.

There is a companion idea worth having in plain words before any notation arrives, because it will matter directly in the first worked example: the difference between an average rate and an instantaneous rate. If you drive 60 miles in one hour, your average speed for the trip was 60 miles per hour — but that number, on its own, tells you nothing about whether you were doing exactly 60 the entire time, or crawling through traffic for forty minutes and then doing 100 on an open stretch to make up the difference. The average smooths all of that away. An instantaneous rate, by contrast, is the rate at one single moment — what the speedometer needle says at 3:14:22 PM specifically. The two agree only in the special case where the rate never changes at all. Almost everything interesting about reading motion correctly, including the entire Bolt story, lives in the gap between those two numbers.

Building the formal system

Position needs a coordinate first: a fixed reference point, an axis, and a direction called positive. For a sprinter, the natural choice is the starting blocks as position zero and the direction down the track as positive; position, conventionally written x, is then just how far along that axis the runner is at a given time t. Average velocity over some stretch of the race is the change in position divided by the change in time it took:

v‒avg = Δx / Δt = (x₂ − x₁) / (t₂ − t₁)

v‒avg
average velocity over the interval, in units of distance per time (meters per second throughout this lesson)
Δx
the change in position, x₂ − x₁ — the Greek letter Δ (delta) conventionally means “change in” throughout physics
Δt
the change in time, t₂ − t₁, i.e. how long the interval lasted
x₁, x₂
position at the start and end of the interval being measured
t₁, t₂
the two times bounding that interval

Instantaneous velocity — the reading at one exact moment, the physics equivalent of a speedometer needle — is what you get by shrinking that interval down until it is not really an interval anymore: making Δt smaller and smaller, arbitrarily close to zero, and watching what the ratio Δx/Δt settles down to. Graphically, this has an exact and useful picture: on a graph of position against time, the average velocity between two points is the slope of the straight line connecting them (a secant line). As the second point is dragged closer and closer to the first, that secant line rotates and settles into the line that just grazes the curve at a single point — the tangent line — and the instantaneous velocity at that point is exactly the tangent line’s slope. This is the single most important fact for reading a position-time graph correctly: velocity is not a height on the graph, it is a steepness. A position-time graph that is climbing steeply describes something moving fast; one that is flat, however high up on the page, describes something standing still.

v(t) = slope of the tangent line to x(t) at time t   |   a(t) = slope of the tangent line to v(t) at time t

x(t)
position as a function of time — a graph with time on the horizontal axis and position on the vertical axis
v(t)
velocity as a function of time; reading its value at any instant means reading the steepness of the x(t) graph at that instant, never its height
a(t)
acceleration as a function of time; reading it means reading the steepness of the v(t) graph, by the identical logic one layer up

The relationship runs the other direction too, and it is just as useful: if slope on a position graph gives velocity, then area under a velocity graph gives a change in position. A velocity-time graph that shows a constant 5 meters per second for 4 seconds describes something that has covered 20 meters — the area of a 5-by-4 rectangle — regardless of what the velocity was doing before or after that stretch. When velocity is not constant, the same idea still holds; the area under an irregular curve is simply harder to compute by hand than the area of a rectangle, though it is exactly the quantity a speedometer-and-clock combination is quietly measuring every time a trip odometer ticks over.

For the specific, common case where acceleration is constant — not zero, just unchanging, which describes gravity near Earth’s surface, a car braking at a steady pedal pressure, or (to a good approximation) a sprinter’s opening drive out of the blocks — the three layers combine into four standard equations, sometimes called the kinematic equations or, informally, SUVAT (for s, u, v, a, t, the symbols British physics texts traditionally use for displacement, initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, and time). This lesson keeps the x/v/a notation already introduced above rather than switching to SUVAT’s letters, but the equations are the same four either way, and worth having assembled in one place:

(1)   v = v₀ + at      (2)   x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²      (3)   v² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀)      (4)   x = x₀ + ½(v₀ + v)t

x₀, v₀
position and velocity at the moment timing starts (t = 0)
x, v
position and velocity at time t
a
acceleration, assumed constant across the whole interval being described
t
elapsed time since the starting moment

None of the four equations is independent new physics; each is derivable from the definitions already given (velocity as the rate of change of position, acceleration as the rate of change of velocity), and each is useful because it lets you solve for an unknown quantity without needing to know all five (x, x₀, v, v₀, a, t — six symbols, but each equation involves only four of them, chosen so that whichever quantity you have not been given, one of the four equations skips over it). Which of the four to reach for in a given problem is simply a matter of noticing which quantity you were not told and are not asked to find, and picking the equation that omits it.

Doing it with real numbers

Three worked examples: Bolt’s own splits, turned into an actual velocity-time curve; a free-fall drop performed 380,000 kilometers from the nearest strip of tarmac; and the ordinary, everyday physics of stopping a car.

Worked example 1 · building Bolt’s velocity curve from his own splits

Berlin’s electronic timing system recorded Bolt’s position every 10 meters. Officially, his reaction time off the gun was 0.146 seconds, and his total time to 100 meters, including that reaction time, was the record 9.58 seconds. The interval it took him to cover each individual 10-meter segment, measured from the moment he actually left the blocks (excluding the 0.146-second reaction), was published as: 1.74s (0–10m), 0.99s (10–20m), 0.90s (20–30m), 0.86s (30–40m), 0.83s (40–50m), 0.82s (50–60m), 0.81s (60–70m), 0.82s (70–80m), 0.83s (80–90m), 0.83s (90–100m). Adding the 0.146-second reaction time back onto the running total of these ten intervals reproduces the officially timed splits at each 10-meter mark almost exactly, which is the first useful check: the numbers are internally consistent before a single calculation is done with them.

  1. Average velocity in each 10-meter segment is simply that segment’s 10 meters divided by the time it took, v‒avg = Δx/Δt. Out of the blocks, 0–10m: 10 ÷ 1.74 = 5.75 m/s (about 12.9 mph) — a slow, straining opening, exactly as expected of a start.
  2. The next several segments show the acceleration phase clearly: 10–20m gives 10 ÷ 0.99 = 10.10 m/s, 20–30m gives 10 ÷ 0.90 = 11.11 m/s, 30–40m gives 10 ÷ 0.86 = 11.63 m/s, 40–50m gives 10 ÷ 0.83 = 12.05 m/s. Each segment is faster than the one before it, by a shrinking amount each time — velocity still rising, but the rate of rise itself easing off.
  3. The 50–60m segment gives 10 ÷ 0.82 = 12.20 m/s, and the 60–70m segment — the fastest 10 meters of the entire race — gives 10 ÷ 0.81 = 12.35 m/s (44.4 km/h, 27.6 mph). By the logic of an average velocity approximating the instantaneous velocity at the segment’s midpoint, this places Bolt’s peak speed at roughly the 65-meter mark, not near the finish.
  4. What happens after that is the part the broadcast missed: 70–80m gives 10 ÷ 0.82 = 12.20 m/s, and both 80–90m and 90–100m give 10 ÷ 0.83 = 12.05 m/s. Three consecutive segments, each slightly slower than the 60–70m peak, not faster. The velocity curve rises steeply for the first 60 meters, crests at 65 meters, and then very gently comes back down for the final 35.
  5. The size of that final decline can be turned into an actual acceleration. Taking the midpoint of the 60–70m segment (roughly t ≈ 6.55s from the blocks, v ≈ 12.35 m/s) and the midpoint of the 90–100m segment (roughly t ≈ 9.02s, v ≈ 12.05 m/s): a = Δv/Δt = (12.05 − 12.35) ÷ (9.02 − 6.55) = −0.30 ÷ 2.47 ≈ −0.12 m/s². Compare that to his acceleration out of the blocks over the same style of calculation, using the 0–10m and 10–20m segment midpoints (t ≈ 0.87s, v ≈ 5.75 m/s and t ≈ 2.24s, v ≈ 10.10 m/s): a = (10.10 − 5.75) ÷ (2.24 − 0.87) ≈ 4.35 ÷ 1.37 ≈ 3.18 m/s².

Twenty-seven times more acceleration in the opening segment than the (negative) figure in the closing one — 3.18 m/s² against −0.12 m/s². That second number is real, and it is a genuine deceleration, not a rounding artifact: three consecutive segments each slightly slower than the one before is not noise in a data set this internally consistent. It is also completely invisible from a stadium seat or a television camera, because the runner it belongs to was still travelling at roughly 44 kilometers an hour while it happened, decelerating by less than the width of a percentage point every second. “Still going very fast” and “still speeding up” looked, to every eye in the building, like the same fact. The velocity curve says they were not.

Worked example 2 · a hammer, a feather, and one-sixth of Earth’s gravity

On August 2, 1971, at the end of Apollo 15’s final moonwalk, Commander David Scott held up a 1.32-kilogram aluminum geological hammer in one hand and a 0.03-kilogram falcon feather in the other, roughly 1.6 meters above the lunar surface, and let go of both at the same instant, live on camera, as a tribute to Galileo’s centuries-old claim that all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass — a claim that is true only when there is no air resistance to push back harder on the lighter, more draggy object. The Moon, with essentially no atmosphere, is one of the few places that claim can be demonstrated cleanly rather than argued about.

  1. Both objects start at rest (v₀ = 0) and fall the same height, h = 1.6 m, under the Moon’s surface gravity, gₖ ≈ 1.62 m/s² — about one-sixth of Earth’s 9.8 m/s². Equation (2) from the formalism above, with downward as positive and the drop height standing in for x, becomes h = ½gₖt², since the v₀t term vanishes for an object released from rest.
  2. Solve for t: t = √(2h ÷ gₖ) = √(2 × 1.6 ÷ 1.62) = √(3.2 ÷ 1.62) = √1.975 ≈ 1.41 seconds. That is the predicted fall time for both objects, mass appearing nowhere in the equation — which is precisely Galileo’s and Scott’s point. A heavier object does feel a proportionally larger gravitational force, but Newton’s second law divides that same force by the object’s own (proportionally larger) mass to get acceleration, and the two proportions cancel exactly, leaving gₖ as the only thing that matters. This is why the h and t in the equation above never picked up a mass term to begin with.
  3. The same drop performed on Earth, height and starting velocity unchanged, uses g = 9.8 m/s² instead: t = √(2 × 1.6 ÷ 9.8) = √0.3265 ≈ 0.57 seconds — well under half of the lunar fall time, and fast enough that human reflexes barely register it as more than a flicker.
  4. The ratio of the two fall times, 1.41 ÷ 0.57 ≈ 2.46, is not a coincidence of these particular numbers; because t ∝ 1/√g in this equation, the ratio of two fall times for the same height always equals the square root of the inverse ratio of the two gravities: √(9.8 ÷ 1.62) = √6.05 ≈ 2.46, matching exactly. A lower surface gravity does not just make things fall a little slower — it stretches out fall time by the square root of how much weaker gravity is, which is part of why the Apollo 15 footage reads, even to a modern eye that has never done the arithmetic, as visibly, unmistakably slow-motion, without a single frame of the film actually being slowed down.

The 1.41-second prediction is a calculation, not a stopwatch reading off the 1971 broadcast — the footage’s frame rate and the astronaut’s imprecise, by-hand release make a precise independent video measurement genuinely hard to pin down, and this lesson does not claim one. What the equation delivers instead is the right answer to a cleaner, checkable question: given the stated drop height and the Moon’s known surface gravity, how long should that fall take, and does it match what a viewer of the clip can plainly see, a fall lasting a bit over a second and clearly longer than the same drop would take in a living room on Earth? It does.

Worked example 3 · how far a car actually travels before it stops

A common textbook estimate for hard, controlled braking on dry pavement is a deceleration of roughly 8 m/s² (about 0.8 times the acceleration of gravity) once the brakes are actually engaged, and a commonly cited average driver perception-and-reaction time — from noticing a hazard to a foot actually reaching the brake pedal — of about 1.5 seconds. Take a car traveling at 60 mph, which is 26.8 m/s, and ask how far it travels from the instant the hazard appears to the instant it stops.

  1. Reaction distance. For that 1.5-second reaction window, the car is not braking at all — it is still traveling at a constant 26.8 m/s, so this stretch uses plain distance = velocity × time: 26.8 × 1.5 ≈ 40.2 meters (about 132 feet) covered before the brakes do anything.
  2. Braking distance. Once braking begins, this is exactly the free-fall equation from worked example 2, with deceleration instead of gravity and a horizontal car instead of a vertical drop: equation (3), v² = v₀² + 2aΔx, solved for the stopping case where the final velocity v = 0: Δx = −v₀² ÷ 2a = −(26.8)² ÷ (2 × −8) = −718.2 ÷ −16 ≈ 44.9 meters (about 147 feet).
  3. Total stopping distance is the sum of the two, since they happen one after the other, not at the same time: 40.2 + 44.9 ≈ 85 meters, or about 279 feet — nearly the length of a full American football field, from the moment a hazard becomes visible to the moment the car is actually stopped, at a speed most drivers consider entirely ordinary.
  4. The split is the point worth sitting with: only about 53% of that distance (44.9 of 85 meters) is the physical braking itself, the part a better set of brakes or tires could shorten. The other 47% (40.2 meters) is spent covering ground at full speed before the driver’s foot has even moved, and no amount of braking hardware touches that portion at all — it is pure v₀ × t, reaction time multiplied by however fast the car happens to be going.

Double the initial speed in this same calculation and the reaction distance only doubles (it is linear in v₀), but the braking distance quadruples (it depends on v₀²) — which is exactly why speed limits on highways are not simply twice the speed limits on residential streets despite the roads looking, to the eye, only moderately different in character. The v² term in equation (3) is doing real, unglamorous work every time a driver’s-ed instructor says that speed kills disproportionately, not proportionately.

Where it already shows up

Every economic release this newspaper covers in its news editions is, quietly, a kinematics problem wearing a suit. When the Closing Brief reports that initial jobless claims came in at some level for the week, and separately reports the four-week moving average of that same series, it is reporting a position and something closer to a smoothed velocity — the four-week average exists specifically to strip out the noisy, individual-week jitter (the equivalent of measuring velocity over an interval too short and too jumpy to trust) and recover something closer to the true underlying trend, the way this lesson’s ten-meter segments smooth Bolt’s actual, continuously-varying speed into ten clean average numbers. A single week’s claims figure jumping around is not evidence the labor market lurched; it is closer to evidence that a one-week interval is too short an interval to read reliably, exactly the caution built into treating any average velocity as a decent stand-in for an instantaneous one only once the interval is short enough to trust and long enough to be stable.

Inflation reporting leans on the identical third layer, under a different name. When a Morning Brief describes inflation as “cooling” even while prices are still rising, it is making an acceleration-layer claim about a velocity-layer quantity: the inflation rate itself (already a rate of change of price levels, the economic equivalent of velocity) can be positive — prices still climbing — while its own rate of change is negative — the climb itself slowing down. “Cooling inflation” and “falling prices” describe two different, frequently confused situations, in exactly the same way “decelerating” and “going backward” describe two different things for Bolt’s velocity curve. A monthly CPI print can disappoint the very same reading in which annual inflation continues to ease, if that single month’s pace ticked up even as the underlying trend keeps bending down — the one-month reading standing in for a single, possibly noisy interval, the same caution this lesson gave the four-week claims average two paragraphs above.

The same layered structure sits directly behind every live mission broadcast this system’s Science & Space coverage draws from. A launch webcast’s telemetry display — altitude, velocity, and a downrange-distance readout, updating continuously through ascent — is nothing more exotic than x(t) and v(t) rendered as a live number board instead of a plotted curve, with acceleration recoverable, exactly as in worked example 1 above, from how quickly the velocity figure itself is climbing. The stage where a rocket’s velocity readout stops climbing steeply and flattens toward a near-constant number is the same kind of moment, structurally, as the 65-meter mark in Bolt’s race: a point where the position-time curve is still climbing fast, but the velocity curve — the slope of that climb — has stopped increasing. Reading a mission clock correctly means watching the second number, not just the first.

The mistake almost everyone makes

The Bolt story above is a specific instance of the single most common and most thoroughly documented error in how people read motion: mistaking a graph, or an event, for a picture of the thing it describes rather than an abstract record of a number changing over time. Physics education researchers Lillian McDermott, Mark Rosenquist, and Emily van Zee, in a widely cited 1987 study of how students actually interpret kinematics graphs, gave this specific error a name — the “graph as picture” misconception — after finding it, and closely related confusions between a graph’s height and its slope, among the most persistent errors even in students who could correctly recite the formal definitions of velocity and acceleration on a written exam.

The clearest version of the error looks like this: shown a position-time graph of a ball rolling along level, horizontal ground — a graph that happens to rise, then curve over, then fall, because the ball rolls forward, decelerates, and rolls backward — a large fraction of students describe the ball as having gone up into the air and come back down, reading the shape of the curve as a literal picture of the ball’s path through space, rather than as an abstract plot in which the vertical axis represents a number (position along the ground) that has nothing to do with height at all. The graph looks like a hill. The ball never left the floor. The Bolt case is a milder version of the identical error: a race that felt like continuous acceleration, because acceleration is what a dominant, still-fast performance instinctively suggests, when the actual velocity curve behind that feeling had already crested and begun, very gently, downhill.

The plain-language warning from earlier in this lesson — that a negative acceleration does not automatically mean slowing down — is the second half of the same family of error, and it shows up most reliably in exactly the kind of problem this lesson has not yet used: a ball thrown straight up. Ask someone what the ball’s acceleration is at the very top of its flight, the instant its velocity is momentarily zero, and a large fraction will say zero, reasoning that no velocity means no acceleration — another instance of collapsing two different layers into one, this time in the opposite direction from the Bolt case. The correct answer is that the ball’s acceleration is exactly −g the entire time it is in the air, rising or falling, including at that single instant of zero velocity: gravity never switches off, and never changes strength, just because the object it is acting on has momentarily stopped moving. What is actually zero at the peak is velocity, layer two; acceleration, layer three, is constant throughout the entire flight, a textbook case of the same free-fall equation used for the Apollo 15 drop, simply run with an initial upward velocity instead of starting from rest. Trowbridge and McDermott’s 1981 study of introductory-level students, working through problems on exactly this kind of one-dimensional motion, found that a majority of students showed at best a shaky, inconsistent qualitative grip on acceleration as Δv/Δt — well behind their (still imperfect) grip on velocity — and the ball-at-the-top case was one of the specific setups where that gap was most visible: velocity vanishing gets misread as acceleration vanishing, because the sign-and-direction rule from earlier in this lesson has quietly been dropped in favor of the much simpler, much less accurate rule that acceleration is just whatever velocity happens to be doing right now.

The corrective habit is the one this lesson has tried to build from the first paragraph of the formalism section onward: before describing what a graph, or a race, or a launch is doing, ask specifically which of the three layers — position, velocity, or acceleration — the description is actually about, and check that against what the data, not the impression, supports. “Still speeding up” is a claim about acceleration. Bolt’s own splits, added up and divided correctly, say it was false for the final third of the fastest 100 meters ever run — not because anyone lied about it, but because a stadium full of people watching a man run faster than anyone in history had no way to feel the difference between a very high velocity and a still-rising one. The graph could.

What today points toward

Yesterday’s lesson built one specific habit around a single check: dimensional homogeneity, the requirement that both sides of any physical equation reduce to the same combination of base dimensions — length, mass, and time — before the equation is even allowed to be a candidate for true. It is worth running that exact check against the four kinematic equations assembled in this lesson’s formalism section, because they were never labeled as dimensionally safe; they simply are, and it is worth seeing why. Take equation (2), x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at². Every term on the right must carry the same dimension as x on the left — length, L. x₀ is a position, so [x₀] = L, trivially. v₀t is a velocity times a time: [v₀t] = (L T⁻¹)(T) = L, the T⁻¹ and T canceling exactly. And ½at² is an acceleration times a time squared: [at²] = (L T⁻²)(T²) = L, again cancelling cleanly. All three terms reduce to L. The equation was never at risk of being dimensionally wrong — but yesterday’s lesson is exactly what makes it possible to notice that, and to notice instantly if a remembered or misquoted version of the equation (an extra t where a t² belongs, say) had broken that agreement.

That is not a coincidence of these particular four equations; it is what building a formal system out of an already-checked, already-consistent set of definitions buys you for free. Yesterday’s lesson showed dimensional homogeneity catching two real, expensive failures — a jet that left the gate with less than half its needed fuel, a spacecraft that burned up 57 kilometers lower than planned — precisely because a bare number crossed a boundary between two people or two systems with no label attached to say what it actually was. Every equation taught today crossed no such boundary un-labeled: x, v, a, and t were each defined once, with a stated dimension, before being combined. The check from yesterday is not a separate tool bolted onto today’s subject. It is the same discipline, run one layer earlier, that made today’s equations trustworthy enough to hand you real numbers — a sprinter’s deceleration, a hammer’s fall time, a car’s stopping distance — and expect them to mean something.

Where this leaves you
  • You can state the difference between position, velocity, and acceleration as three layered rates of change, and explain precisely why “going fast” and “speeding up” are different claims, about different layers.
  • You can read a position-time or velocity-time graph correctly — slope gives the next layer up, area under a velocity curve gives a change in position — and you know the specific, documented “graph as picture” error that causes most people to misread one.
  • You can apply the four constant-acceleration kinematic equations to a real problem, choosing the one that omits whichever quantity you were not given, as in the free-fall and braking-distance examples above.
  • You can compute a real velocity or acceleration from raw split, timing, or position data, the way Bolt’s own Berlin splits were turned into the specific finding that his top speed came at roughly 65 meters, not the finish line — the finding this lesson opened with.
