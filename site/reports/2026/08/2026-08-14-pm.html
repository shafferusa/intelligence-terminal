July retail sales and consumer sentiment both miss estimates, denying the market a clean read on the consumer heading into the Fed's next decision

Retail sales released 08:30 ET · Consumer sentiment released 10:00 ET · Last checked market close

What happened
The Census Bureau's advance estimate (this report's own direct fetch of census.gov/retail/marts/www/marts_current.pdf, cross-verified against Bloomberg, Axios and Floor Daily's reporting of the same release) put July retail and food-services sales at $763.6B, down 0.6% from June — the sharpest monthly decline since May 2025 — though still up 5.0% year-over-year. Excluding autos and gasoline, sales fell 0.2%. The "control group" that feeds directly into GDP's consumption estimate (ex autos, gasoline, building materials, and food services) fell 0.4%, against a consensus expecting a 0.3% gain, and May/June control-group figures were revised down. By category: motor vehicles −1.8% (the sharpest decliner), nonstore/online retailers −2.2%, while clothing (+1.9%), health and personal care (+0.7%) and food services (+0.5%) posted gains. Separately, the University of Michigan's preliminary August consumer sentiment index came in at 51.0, well below the 54.5 consensus and down from July's final 55.2 — a 7.6% monthly decline. The expectations sub-index fell to 50.6 from 55.4 and current conditions to 51.8 from 54.8; year-ahead inflation expectations ticked up to 4.3% from 4.2%, while the 5–10-year outlook held at 3.3% for a third straight month. Sentiment fell across demographic and political lines, most sharply among Republicans, older consumers, lower-income households, and those without college degrees, per the University of Michigan's own release.
Why it matters
This is the first hard evidence this week that the consumer, not just producer-side pricing, may be cooling: retail sales resolves this morning's open forecast (2026-08-14-am-1, that sales would print meaningfully above consensus) as wrong — the actual result was a miss, not a beat, and the control-group's downward revision compounds it. Combined with a sentiment index at its lowest levels of the year and rising inflation expectations, today's data complicates a clean read in either direction for the Fed's Warsh-hawkish-vs-jobs-dovish tension this report has tracked since Aug 10: soft demand data argues against a hike, but sentiment's inflation-expectations component cuts the other way.
What is confirmed
Both releases' headline figures, via this report's own direct fetch of the Census Bureau's PDF release (retrieved successfully, though this run's tooling could not machine-extract its text; the $763.6B/−0.6%/+5.0% figures are cross-verified against multiple independent wire and financial-media reports of the same release) and the University of Michigan's own preliminary index values as reported by multiple outlets (Axios, Yahoo Finance, Investing Live).
What remains uncertain
Whether today's twin misses reflect a genuine inflection in consumer demand or a single soft month within a still-positive year-over-year trend (retail sales remain up 5.0% y/y and mark a tenth straight month of annual gains); no Fed official had commented on either release as of this report's news cutoff.
Context
Follows this week's mixed CPI (cooler, Wed) and PPI (hotter core, Thu) prints; today's data is the week's last major economic release before the Fed's Sept 16 decision and Chair Warsh's Aug 27 Jackson Hole remarks.
Impact
Rates: front-end most sensitive to a softer growth read. Equities: consumer-discretionary and retail names most directly exposed. Fed policy: adds a dovish data point to a week that otherwise leaned mixed-to-hawkish on core PPI.
What happens next

Stearns dismissed the Justice Department's lawsuit against Harvard University, which had alleged the school's response to campus antisemitism following the October 7, 2023 attack was so inadequate it amounted to "deliberate indifference" under civil-rights law. Section 16 · learningPhysics Lesson

Physics 15/71 · Friction

This morning introduced friction: the resistive force between two surfaces in contact, which comes in two flavors — static friction, which resists the start of relative motion, and kinetic friction, which resists motion already underway. Static friction is the stronger of the two right up until the object actually starts to slide, at which point the resistance drops to the (smaller) kinetic value; that is why a heavy box is hardest to get moving and, once moving, takes noticeably less force to keep sliding.

Tonight's tie-in: today's market had its own version of a static-vs-kinetic distinction. York Space Systems needed a large "force" — a second consecutive earnings miss plus a same-day CFO change — to overcome the market's static resistance to pushing the stock meaningfully lower a second day in a row after Thursday's regular-session gain; once that combined force exceeded the threshold, the decline (like kinetic friction taking over) proceeded at a lower "resistance," extending through the session rather than reversing. A common misconception worth correcting: friction is not proportional to the contact area between two surfaces — a wide, flat block and a narrow, tall block of the same weight experience essentially the same friction force, because friction depends on the normal force (roughly, weight) and the materials involved, not on how much surface touches. This sets up Newton's Second Law next: once forces (including friction) are correctly identified and summed, F = ma turns the diagram into an actual prediction of motion.

Section 17 · learningSpaceflight Lesson

Spaceflight 15/75 · Max Q

This morning introduced Max Q: the point during ascent where dynamic pressure on the vehicle (a combination of air density and velocity squared) peaks. Contrary to what the name might suggest, Max Q does not happen at maximum speed or maximum altitude — it happens in between, typically 60–90 seconds after liftoff, when the vehicle is going fast enough to generate serious aerodynamic pressure but is still low enough that the air is dense enough to matter. Past Max Q, the rocket keeps accelerating, but the air thins out faster than the speed increases, so the structural stress from aerodynamic pressure actually falls even as velocity climbs.

Tonight's tie-in: China's Long March 7A failure on Aug 10, which this report has tracked since it happened, broke apart roughly 83–85 seconds after liftoff — squarely inside the window where Max Q pressure is typically highest for a vehicle of that size and profile. That timing is one reason investigators (per this report's earlier coverage) are reportedly focused on the shared YF-100 engine architecture and the joint first-stage/booster burn phase: a structural or engine anomaly is far more likely to become catastrophic exactly when aerodynamic loads are already near their peak, since the vehicle has the least structural margin to spare at that moment. A common misconception is that rockets throttle down before Max Q to reduce speed and therefore reduce stress; in reality, many vehicles (including Falcon 9) do throttle down briefly around Max Q, but the purpose is to limit dynamic pressure directly, not to slow the vehicle's overall climb — the throttle-up resumes immediately after the pressure peak passes. Tomorrow's lesson connects this to the vehicle's structural margins and why Max Q dictates a rocket's maximum survivable dynamic-pressure rating.

Section 18 · learningQuant/ML Lesson

Quant/ML 15/118 · CAPM Alpha Formula

This morning introduced CAPM alpha: α = Ri − [Rf + β(Rm − Rf)] — a measure of whether an investment earned more or less than what its market-risk exposure alone would predict. Rf is the risk-free rate, Rm is the market's return, β measures how sensitive the investment is to market moves, and the bracketed term is the "expected" return given that sensitivity; alpha is simply the leftover the model can't explain — positive alpha means the investment beat its risk-adjusted expectation, negative means it lagged it.

Tonight, use today's actual watchlist data to see why alpha is a comparison, not a standalone number. Today the broad market (SPY) was little changed to slightly lower on the retail-sales/consumer-sentiment miss, while York Space Systems fell sharply on company-specific news (a second earnings miss and CFO transition). YSS's large decline is not, by itself, "negative alpha" in the CAPM sense — a stock with a high beta would be expected to fall somewhat on a weak macro day even with zero company-specific news, so computing YSS's true alpha requires subtracting out that market-beta-driven component first, isolating only the piece of the move attributable to company-specific information. Today's move is large enough, and the news specific enough, that essentially all of it likely represents true (negative) alpha rather than beta-driven market exposure — but that separation is the actual analytical step, not something you can skip by eyeballing the raw return.

A common misconception, worth stating plainly: a stock that "beat the market today" did not necessarily generate positive alpha, and one that fell more than the market did not necessarily generate negative alpha — both depend on what that stock's beta predicted it should have done, which requires the full CAPM calculation, not just a same-day return comparison. Tomorrow's lesson turns to ARCH(p), the first of the volatility-clustering models, which explains why days like today's YSS move tend to be followed by more (not average) volatility in the same name.
