Argentina’s 100-year bond

19 June 2017. Argentina sold $2.75 billion of US-dollar bonds that would not be repaid until 2117. A century bond.

The borrower’s record was not reassuring. Argentina had defaulted on its debts eight times since independence, most recently in 2014.

Investors queued anyway: about $9.75 billion of orders, more than three times the amount sold.

The coupon was 7.125%: $7.125 a year on every $100 of face value. But buyers did not pay $100. They paid 90 cents on the dollar, $90 for each $100 Argentina promised to repay in 2117.

Bloomberg reported the yield as 7.917%.

So the coupon says 7.125% and the yield says 7.917%. Where do the extra 0.79 points come from, when you pay 90 for a bond that repays 100? And can 7.917% be set beside the yield on a bond that pays once a year?

The idea

Day 15 went from a yield to a price. Today runs the same equation backwards: price to yield.

The yield to maturity (YTM) is the bond’s IRR (Day 10): the one discount rate at which its coupons and par are worth exactly its price. It is your yearly return if you buy today and hold to the end, provided every payment arrives on time and every coupon is reinvested at the YTM itself.

Picture a $100 gift card you buy for $90 that also pays interest. Your return is the interest plus the $10 you gain at the end.

Three narrower measures:

  • Current yield = annual coupon ÷ price: the coupon part only, ignoring the drift to par.
  • Yield-to-call (YTC): the IRR if the issuer repays early, at the call price on a call date.
  • Yield-to-worst: the lowest of the YTM and every YTC. The issuer calls when it suits it, which is when it hurts you.

To compare yields, count periods the same way. US bonds pay twice a year and quote twice the half-year rate: the semiannual-bond basis. Many bonds pay once a year. Convert first.

Quotes assume the street convention: payments on schedule. The true yield uses actual dates (a coupon due on a Saturday arrives Monday), so it is never higher.

Why it works

Build it from Day 15 and Day 10, with a 4-year, 6% annual-coupon bond priced at 96.6128.

  1. List the cash. You pay 96.6128 today. You get 6, 6, 6, then 106 (the last coupon plus par).
  2. Day 15, backwards. Day 15: price = 6/(1 + r) + 6/(1 + r)2 + 6/(1 + r)3 + 106/(1 + r)4. Now the price is known and r is the unknown.
  3. That is an IRR. Move the price across: −96.6128 + 6/(1 + r) + … + 106/(1 + r)4 = 0. The r that solves it is the YTM: the IRR of buying the bond (Day 10).
  4. Search for it. No algebra isolates r, so try rates. Price falls as r rises, so only one r fits. At 6%: 100.0000 (coupon = yield: par), too high a price, so r is bigger. At 8%: 93.3757, too low. At 7%: 96.6128. Found. CPT I/Y does this search for you.
  5. Where the 7% comes from. The coupon on what you paid: 6/96.6128 = 6.21%, the current yield. The other 0.79 points: the climb from 96.61 to 100. Spread that gain evenly and you get the simple yield, which ignores when the gain arrives; the YTM discounts it as cash due at the end.
  6. Half-years. On a semiannual bond, CPT I/Y gives the rate per half-year. The market quotes it doubled: a stated annual rate with m = 2 (Day 4).
  7. One periodicity. A rate stated with m periods a year grows 1 to (1 + APRm/m)m in a year. Two yields match when 1 grows to the same amount: 8% semiannual, 1.042 = 1.0816, is 8.16% annual-pay.
  8. Calls. If the issuer can repay early, the cash stops at the call date, with the call price in place of par. Same IRR, shorter N, new FV: the yield-to-call.

In plain words: the YTM is the one interest rate that makes what a bond promises worth what you pay. Pay under 100 and you earn more than the coupon, because the climb back to 100 is yours too.

Price = PMT/(1 + r) + PMT/(1 + r)2 + … + (PMT + FV)/(1 + r)N, solved for r

Current yield = annual coupon / flat price

Simple yield = [annual coupon + (par − flat price)/years] / flat price

(1 + APRm/m)m = (1 + APRn/n)n

r
the YTM per period: the bond’s IRR. On a semiannual bond the quoted YTM is 2r
PMT
coupon per period
FV
par, repaid at maturity; for a yield-to-call, the call price
N
periods to maturity; for a yield-to-call, periods to the call date
flat price
the quoted price, without accrued interest (more on Day 17); on a coupon date it is simply the price
(par − flat price)/years
the gain (discount bond) or loss (premium bond, negative) spread evenly over the years to maturity
APRm, APRn
the same return as an annual yield stated with m, or with n, periods a year: 2 is the semiannual-bond basis, 1 annual-pay
Memorize
  • Current yield = annual coupon / flat price
  • Yield-to-call inputs: N = periods to the call date, FV = call price, PV = price, PMT = coupon; solve for r
  • Yield-to-worst: the lowest of the YTM and the yields to every call date
  • Converting yields between periodicities: (1 + APRm/m)m = (1 + APRn/n)n; semiannual-bond basis → annual-pay: (1 + rs/2)2 = 1 + rannual
  • YTM’s three assumptions: held to maturity; all coupons and principal paid on time; coupons reinvested at the YTM
  • Simple yield = (annual coupon + straight-line amortized gain, or − amortized loss, per year) / flat price

Worked example

Problem. A 4-year bond with a 6% annual coupon is priced at 96.6128. (a) Show its YTM is 7% by discounting. (b) Current yield. (c) Simple yield. (d) Restate 7% (annual-pay) on a semiannual-bond basis.

By hand
  1. (a) 6/1.07 = 5.6075; 6/1.072 = 5.2406; 6/1.073 = 4.8978; 106/1.074 = 106/1.310796 = 80.8669.
  2. Sum: 5.6075 + 5.2406 + 4.8978 + 80.8669 = 96.6128, the price. So the YTM is 7%.
  3. (b) Current yield = 6/96.6128 = 6.2104%.
  4. (c) Discount: 100 − 96.6128 = 3.3872, spread over 4 years = 0.8468 a year. Simple yield = (6 + 0.8468)/96.6128 = 7.0868%.
  5. (d) (1 + rs/2)2 = 1.07, so rs = 2 × (1.070.5 − 1) = 2 × 0.034408 = 6.8816%. Fewer points, compounded twice, grow to the same 1.07.
  6. The order for a discount bond: coupon 6% < current yield 6.21% < YTM 7%.

Back to the story

Bloomberg’s 7.917% is the IRR of paying 90 for 200 half-yearly coupons of 3.5625 plus 100 at the end: 3.9585% a half-year, doubled.

Nearly all of the extra 0.79 points is the coupon earned on a cheaper price: 7.125/90 is 7.917% too. A century bond is almost a perpetuity, whose yield is exactly coupon ÷ price (Day 8: PV = A/r).

The climb from 90 to 100 adds 0.0004 of a point: it was a century away, and in 2017 that final 100 was worth about 4 cents. On a 10-year bond at 90 the same climb lifts the YTM to 8.64%.

Beside an annual-pay bond, 7.917% becomes 8.07%.

And the YTM assumes every payment arrives on time. In 2020, less than three years after the sale, Argentina defaulted for a ninth time. In that year’s restructuring the century bonds were swapped, at roughly 55 cents on the dollar by one estimate, for bonds due decades sooner.

Exam traps
  • Not doubling. On a semiannual bond, CPT I/Y gives the half-year rate. Argentina’s 3.9585 is a 7.917% YTM; quoting 3.96% is the trap.
  • Comparing unlike yields. Convert first: (1 + rs/2)2 = 1 + rannual. A semiannual-bond-basis yield always looks smaller than the same return quoted annual-pay: 7.917% is 8.07%.
  • Par in a yield-to-call. FV is the call price. A 5-year 8% annual-pay bond at 104, callable in 2 years at 102: N = 2, PV = −104, PMT = 8, FV = 102 gives a YTC of 6.76%. With FV = 100 you get 5.82%.
  • The worst of a premium bond. That bond’s YTM is 7.02%, but its yield-to-worst is the 6.76% YTC. A premium bond’s coupon beats the market, so the issuer would gladly call it: its yield-to-worst is often the yield to first call.

2. Why. For a bond bought below par, the YTM is higher than the current yield, which is higher than the coupon rate. Why?

  • No: the YTM assumes reinvestment at the YTM itself, not the coupon rate. And reinvestment is not what separates the three measures.

  • No: the current yield already divides by the price. Coupon ÷ par is just the coupon rate.

  • Right: each measure adds a source of return. Coupon rate: coupon on par. Current yield: the same coupon on a smaller price. YTM: that plus the pull to par.

Explanation

Question 1’s bond shows the ladder: 5% coupon on 100; 5/88 = 5.68% on the 88 you pay; 6.04% once the climb from 88 to 100 is counted. For a premium bond the ladder flips: the price falls to par, a loss, so YTM < current yield < coupon rate.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what is a bond’s yield to maturity, and what has to be true for you to actually earn it?

Model answer

The YTM is the IRR of buying the bond at today’s price and holding it to maturity: the one discount rate that makes the PV of its coupons and par equal the price. You earn it only if you hold to maturity, every coupon and the principal are paid in full and on time, and each coupon is reinvested at the YTM.

A complete answer says: (1) it is the IRR, the rate that equates the PV of the promised cash flows to the price; (2) every payment is made on time (no default); (3) coupons are reinvested at the YTM, and the bond is held to maturity.
