Fifteen years to climb back

On 10 March 2000, at the top of the dot-com boom, the Nasdaq Composite closed at a record 5,048.62.

Then it fell, and kept falling. Thirty-one months later, on 9 October 2002, it closed at 1,114.11, about 78% below the peak. On paper, $10,000 that tracked the index from that record close was down to about $2,207.

From there the climb back took more than twelve years. On 23 April 2015 it finally closed above the old record, at 5,056.06. That was fifteen years, one month and thirteen days after the peak: 31 months down, then about twelve and a half years back up. “After 15 years, Nasdaq recoups losses of dot-com crash,” ran one news report’s headline.

Here is the puzzle. The index fell 78%, so you might expect a 78% rise to undo it. Why wasn’t a 78% rise enough to get it back? And why did the climb back take so long?

The idea

A holding-period return (HPR) is what an investment earned over the time you held it, however long: a day, six months, twelve years. It counts both ways you get paid, the price change and any income (a dividend or coupon), as a fraction of what you paid.

Returns over back-to-back periods don’t add. Each one is earned on a different amount: whatever the period before left you. So they compound, like Day 1’s interest, and you combine them by multiplying growth factors (1 + R). That is chain-linking.

An everyday picture: a $100 jacket is marked down 50% to $50, then marked up 50%. It now costs $75, not $100. The markup was 50% of a smaller price.

Losses are where this bites. The deeper the fall, the smaller the base the recovery starts from, so the gain needed grows much faster than the loss.

Last, annualizing puts returns of any length on one yardstick, a year, by compounding them, never by multiplying or dividing by the number of periods.

Why it works

One period. You pay P0. At the end you hold something worth P1, and you were paid D1 along the way. Your gain is P1 − P0 + D1. A return is gain per dollar put in, so divide by P0. That is the HPR.

Two parts. D1/P0 is the income yield, (P1 − P0)/P0 the price return. Leave out D1 and you understate the return.

A growth factor. 1 + R = (P1 + D1)/P0 is what each $1 became. Day 1’s (1 + r) is the same thing with one fixed r.

Chain the periods. $1 becomes (1 + R1) after period 1. Period 2’s return works on that amount, so it becomes (1 + R1)(1 + R2). Each period multiplies in one more factor. Subtract 1 at the end to get back to a return.

It always lands on start-to-end. With prices only, the middle price cancels: (P1/P0) × (P2/P1) = P2/P0.

Why adding fails. +50% then −50%: 1.50 × 0.50 = 0.75, a 25% loss, though the returns add to zero. The −50% hit a bigger base.

Climbing back. After a loss L, $1 is (1 − L). Back to $1 needs a factor of 1/(1 − L), a gain of 1/(1 − L) − 1 = L/(1 − L). A 10% loss needs 11.11%, 50% needs 100%, 75% needs 300%.

Annualizing. A quarter’s return R, earned four quarters running, turns $1 into (1 + R)4 in a year. With c periods a year: (1 + R)c − 1. Over T years, c = 1/T: the T-th root, which is Day 2’s r = (FV/PV)1/N − 1 again.

In plain words: each return works on whatever you have at that moment, so you multiply the (1 + R)s, never add the Rs.

HPR = (P1 − P0 + D1) / P0

Rtotal = (1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + Rn) − 1

Rannual = (1 + Rperiod)c − 1

Required gain = L / (1 − L)

P0
price paid at the start of the holding period
P1
price at the end of the period
D1

Rt

1 + R

Memorize
  • HPR = (P1 − P0 + D1) / P0
  • Rtotal = (1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + Rn) − 1
  • Rannual = (1 + Rperiod)c − 1 (for a return over several years, c = 1/years)
  • Required gain = L / (1 − L) (a 50% loss needs +100%)

Worked example

Problem. (a) You buy a share at $40.00, receive a $1.20 dividend and sell it a year later at $43.00. Find the HPR and its two parts. (b) Three yearly returns: +10.5%, −20% and +15%. What is the 3-year return? (c) Annualize a 3-month return of 2.5%.

By hand
  1. (a) Gain = 43.00 − 40.00 + 1.20 = 4.20. HPR = 4.20 ÷ 40.00 = 10.50%.
  2. Income yield = 1.20 ÷ 40.00 = 3.00%. Price return = 3.00 ÷ 40.00 = 7.50%. Together: 10.50%.
  3. (b) Growth factors: 1.105 × 0.80 × 1.15 = 1.0166.
  4. 3-year return = 1.0166 − 1 = 1.66%. Adding the returns (10.5 − 20 + 15) would say 5.5%, more than three times too much.
  5. (c) A year holds four quarters, so c = 4: 1.0254 − 1 = 1.103813 − 1 = 10.38%, not 4 × 2.5% = 10.00%.

On your calculator

Problem. The Nasdaq Composite closed at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000 and at 1,114.11 on 9 October 2002. Find the HPR of the fall, where a 78% rise from the low would get it, the gain needed to get back, and the annualized return from the 2002 low to the record close of 5,056.06 on 23 April 2015 (4,579 days: use 12.54 years). The BA II Plus has a worksheet for this, 2ND Δ% (above the 5 key). It solves NEW = OLD × (1 + %CH/100)#PD: with #PD = 1, %CH is the HPR; with #PD = years, it is the yearly rate. The TI-84 has no such worksheet, so it works on the home screen.

  1. Open the percent-change worksheet and clear it (clearing also sets #PD back to 1)

    BA II Plus 2ND Δ% 2ND CLR WORK
    TI-84 Plus 2nd MODE (QUIT) to the home screen, then CLEAR

    Display: OLD = 0.0000 (BA II Plus). The four lines are OLD, NEW, %CH, #PD, in that order; ↓ and ↑ move between them.

  2. The fall: OLD = the peak, NEW = the low, solve for %CH

    BA II Plus 5048.62 ENTER ↓ 1114.11 ENTER ↓ CPT

    Display: %CH = −77.9324 (BA II Plus) / .2207, then −.7793, then −77.9324 (TI-84). The low was 0.2207 of the peak: an HPR of −77.93%.

  3. Where a 78% rise from the low gets you: OLD = the low, %CH = 78, solve for NEW

    BA II Plus ↑ ↑ (up to OLD) 1114.11 ENTER ↓ ↓ (down to %CH) 78 ENTER ↑ (back to NEW) CPT

    Display: NEW = 1,983.1158 (BA II Plus) / 1983.1158, then .3928 (TI-84). A 78% rise reaches only 39% of the old peak.

  4. The gain needed to get back: NEW = the old peak, solve for %CH

    BA II Plus (the cursor is on NEW) 5048.62 ENTER ↓ CPT

    Display: %CH = 353.1527 (BA II Plus) / 4.5315, then 3.5315, then 353.1527 (TI-84). The low had to multiply by 4.5315: a gain of 353.15%.

    BA II Plus ↑ (to NEW) 5056.06 ENTER ↓ ↓ (past %CH to #PD) 12.54 ENTER ↑ (back to %CH) CPT
    TI-84 Plus 5056.06 ÷ 1114.11 ENTER, then ^ ( 1 ÷ 12.54 ) ENTER (the 12.54th root), then − 1 ENTER, then × 100 ENTER

Back to the story

Returns multiply, so think in growth factors. The fall left 1,114.11 ÷ 5,048.62 = 0.2207 of the peak: an HPR of −77.93%.

A 78% rise multiplies that by 1.78 and reaches 1,983.12, only 39% of the old peak. The rise is measured on the small base. Getting back needed a factor of 4.5315, a gain of 353.15%. That is L/(1 − L) = 0.779324 ÷ 0.220676.

And a 353% climb takes time. From the October 2002 low to the April 2015 record, about 12.54 years, the index compounded at 12.82% a year, a strong return. At that pace a 78% rise takes under five years; 353% takes twelve and a half.

Chain the whole trip and the middle price cancels: (1,114.11/5,048.62) × (5,056.06/1,114.11) = 1.0015. Fifteen years, +0.15%. Add the two returns instead (−77.93% + 353.82%) and you would claim +275.89%.

(The index counts prices only, so these are price returns; dividends would add to them.)

Exam traps
  • Adding period returns. +10% then −10% is 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99, a 1% loss, not zero. Multiply the (1 + R)s, then subtract 1.
  • Forgetting the dividend or coupon in the HPR. The income is part of the return: (P1 − P0 + D1)/P0. The price change alone understates it by the income yield.
  • Annualizing by multiplying. A 2.5% quarter is 1.0254 − 1 = 10.38% a year, not 4 × 2.5% = 10.00%. Likewise a 3-year return is annualized with the cube root, not by dividing by 3.
  • #PD left over on the BA II Plus. The Δ% worksheet keeps #PD until you change or clear it. Left at 12.54, %CH comes out per year instead of for the whole holding period. Start each problem with 2ND CLR WORK.

2. Why. Why does a 50% fall need a 100% rise to get back to where it started?

  • Right. 100 falls to 50. Getting back is +50 on a base of 50, which is +100%. In growth factors: 0.50 × 2.00 = 1.

  • Time plays no part. One step shows it: a 50% fall followed the next day by a 100% rise gets back exactly. The size of the gain comes from the base, not the calendar.

  • This confuses index mechanics with return arithmetic. A $100 jacket marked down 50% and then up 50% ends at $75 with no index anywhere in sight.

Explanation

After a loss L, each $1 is (1 − L). To get back to $1 you need the factor 1/(1 − L), a gain of L/(1 − L). With L = 0.50: 0.50/0.50 = 1, or +100%. The gain is measured on the shrunken base, so the deeper the loss, the faster the required gain grows.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why are returns over successive periods combined by multiplying (1 + R), not by adding the returns?

Model answer

Each period’s return is earned on whatever the previous periods left you, a new and different base, so each one multiplies the running total by its growth factor (1 + R); the product of the factors is what $1 became, and subtracting 1 at the end turns it back into a return.

A complete answer says: (1) each return applies to the new, changed base, not the original amount; (2) so the growth factors (1 + R) multiply; (3) subtract 1 at the end to get the total return.
