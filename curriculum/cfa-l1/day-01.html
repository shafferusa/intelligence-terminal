Benjamin Franklin’s 200-year bet

On 23 June 1789, Benjamin Franklin added a codicil, a late amendment, to his will. It left £1,000 each to Boston and Philadelphia, with instructions.

The money was to be lent at 5% a year to young married tradesmen, under 25, who had served an apprenticeship in the town. Franklin died in Philadelphia on 17 April 1790.

He did the arithmetic in the codicil itself. If the loans were repaid, Boston’s £1,000 would be £131,000 after 100 years. £100,000 of it was then to go on public works. The other £31,000 was to be lent out for a second century, at the end of which it would be, in his words, “four millions and sixty one thousand pounds”: £4,061,000. Nobody was to add a penny.

Here is the puzzle. 5% of £1,000 is £50 a year. Over a century that is £5,000 of interest. So where do the other £125,000 come from?

And a second one. When the second century ran out, around 1990, Boston’s fund held about $4.5–5 million and Philadelphia’s about $2 million: well short of Franklin’s arithmetic. What went wrong?

The idea

Interest is the price of borrowing: each year the borrower pays the lender a percentage of the money. The whole question is, a percentage of what?

Simple interest is paid on the original sum only. Franklin’s £1,000 at 5% earns £50, then £50, then £50, every year. After 100 years: £1,000 + £5,000 = £6,000.

Compound interest adds each year’s interest to the balance, and next year’s 5% is paid on the bigger balance. Year 1 earns £50, so the balance is £1,050. Year 2 earns 5% of £1,050, which is £52.50. That extra £2.50 is interest earned on interest.

Like a snowball rolling downhill, the bigger it gets, the more it picks up. By year 100, a single year’s interest is £6,261.96: more than the whole century of simple interest.

A quick picture: at 5%, money roughly doubles every 14 years (1.0514 = 1.9799). A century holds about seven doublings: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. That is why £1,000 heads for six figures.

Why it works

Build it one year at a time. There is only one rule: a year’s interest = the rate × the balance at the start of that year.

  1. Today you have PV.
  2. After 1 year: PV + r × PV = PV × (1 + r). Adding 5% is the same as multiplying by 1.05.
  3. After 2 years: year 2’s interest is on PV × (1 + r), so you multiply by (1 + r) again: PV × (1 + r)2.
  4. After 3 years: one more multiplication: PV × (1 + r)3.
  5. After N years: N multiplications by the same number, which is a power: PV × (1 + r)N.

Check it on Franklin: 1,000 × 1.05 = 1,050, and 1,050 × 1.05 = 1,102.50, the same as adding £50, then £52.50.

Where is the interest on interest hiding? Multiply out two years: (1 + r)2 = 1 + 2r + r2, so for Franklin 1,000 + 100 + 2.50 = 1,102.50. The 1 is his money back, the 2r two years of simple interest (£100), and the r2 the first year’s £50 earning 5% (£2.50).

Simple interest is the same sum with every interest-on-interest piece thrown away: the interest is PV × r × N, so the total is PV × (1 + r × N). Compounding multiplies; simple interest adds.

In one sentence: every year your money is multiplied by the same number, 1.05, so after 100 years it has been multiplied by 1.05 a hundred times, and that comes to 131.50.

FVN = PV × (1 + r)N

(1 + r)N
the growth factor: what 1 grows to
Memorize
  • Future value of a single sum: FVN = PV × (1 + r)N
  • Simple interest over N periods: Interest = PV × r × N (no interest on interest)

Worked example

You deposit $2,000 at 6% a year, compounded annually. What is it worth after 3 years, and how much more is that than simple interest would give?

By hand
  1. Year 1: interest = 2,000.00 × 0.06 = 120.00; balance = 2,120.00
  2. Year 2: interest = 2,120.00 × 0.06 = 127.20; balance = 2,247.20
  3. Year 3: interest = 2,247.20 × 0.06 = 134.83; balance = 2,382.03
  4. Formula check: FV = 2,000 × 1.063 = 2,000 × 1.191016 = 2,382.03
  5. Simple interest instead: 2,000 + 2,000 × 0.06 × 3 = 2,360.00
  6. Interest on interest = 2,382.03 − 2,360.00 = 22.03
  7. Where the 22.03 comes from: in year 2, year 1’s 120.00 of interest earns 7.20; in year 3, the 247.20 of earlier interest earns 14.83. 7.20 + 14.83 = 22.03.

Answer: FV = $2,382.03, which is $22.03 more than simple interest ($2,360.00).

On your calculator

The exam rule. Only the TI BA II Plus (or BA II Plus Professional) and the HP 12C family are allowed in the exam room; results earned with a TI-84 are voided. Practise on both, but sit the exam with the BA II Plus.

The sign rule. Money you pay out is negative, money you receive is positive. Franklin’s fund lends out £1,000, so PV = −1,000, and the FV it gets back comes out positive. When you later solve for a rate or for N, the inputs need a sign change or the calculator refuses (BA II Plus Error 5, TI-84 ERR:NO SIGN CHNG).

Back to the story

Interest earns interest. FV = 1,000 × 1.05100 = £131,501.26. Franklin’s £131,000 is this figure, rounded down. His £4,061,000 is £31,000 × 131: the same 100-year growth factor, applied again (the full factor, 1.05100, gives £4,076,539).

Simple interest would have given £6,000. Of the £131,501.26, £1,000 is the original sum and only £5,000 is simple interest: the other £125,501.26 is interest on interest. That is the puzzle’s missing £125,000.

The real funds fell short because they never earned a steady 5%. Borrowers were hard to find, some never repaid, and the managers eventually put much of the money into ordinary investments. Over a century a lost point is enormous: 1.04100 = 50.50 and 1.03100 = 19.22, against 131.50 at 5%. One point less leaves 38% of the result; two points less, under 15%. (The 1990 totals are in dollars and Franklin counted in pounds, so they do not compare one for one.)

Exam traps
  • Leaving the BA II Plus at its factory P/Y = 12: an annual problem is silently computed monthly. Franklin’s problem would show FV = 1,515.5843. Set P/Y = 1 once and leave it.
  • Typing 0.05 in I/Y. Rates go in as percent: type 5. With 0.05 you get £1,051.26 (that is 0.05% a year).
  • Entering PV as +1,000, getting FV = −131,501.2578, and reporting a negative future value. Money paid out goes in negative; then the FV you receive comes out positive.
  • Using simple interest (PV × r × N) when the question compounds: £6,000 instead of £131,501.26. “Compounded annually” means (1 + r)N.

TI-84 Plus: TVM Solver N=40, I%=4.5, PV=−2500, PMT=0, FV=0, P/Y=1, C/Y=1, END; cursor on FV=, ALPHA ENTER → FV=14540.9113.

By formula: 2,500 × 1.04540 = 2,500 × 5.816365 = 14,540.91. Keep the factor in the calculator: rounded to 5.8164 it gives 14,541.00, 9 cents off. Answer: €14,540.91.

2. Why. $1,000 earns 6% a year, compounded annually. If you leave it 20 years instead of 10, the total interest earned (FV − PV), compared with 10 years, is:

  • That is simple interest, where every year earns the same PV × r, so twice the years gives twice the interest ($600 → $1,200). Compounding does not work that way.

  • Right: $790.85 after 10 years, $2,207.14 after 20, about 2.79 times as much. The second decade starts from $1,790.85, not $1,000, so its 6% a year is paid on a bigger balance.

  • This assumes growth slows down as the balance grows. It is the opposite: each year’s interest is 6% of a larger balance, so each decade earns more than the one before.

Explanation

Doubling N squares the growth factor: 1.0610 = 1.790848 and 1.0620 = (1.0610)2 = 3.207135. The first decade turns $1,000 into $1,790.85 (interest $790.85). The second decade grows all of that, interest included, to $3,207.14, so it alone earns $3,207.14 − $1,790.85 = $1,416.29. Total interest $2,207.14, more than twice $790.85.

3. Twist. $10,000 is invested at 8% a year, compounded annually, for 10 years. How much of the final value is interest on interest, the part simple interest would not have paid? Answer in dollars, to the nearest cent.

dollars
Hint You need three numbers: the FV (from the calculator), the original $10,000, and the simple interest, 10,000 × 0.08 × 10. Take the last two away from the first.
Solution

BA II Plus: 2ND CLR TVM 10 N 8 I/Y 10000 +|− PV 0 PMT CPT FV → FV = 21,589.2500.

TI-84 Plus: TVM Solver N=10, I%=8, PV=−10000, PMT=0, FV=0, P/Y=1, C/Y=1, END; cursor on FV=, ALPHA ENTER → FV=21589.2500.

All interest = 21,589.25 − 10,000 = 11,589.25. Simple interest = 10,000 × 0.08 × 10 = 8,000.00. Interest on interest = 11,589.25 − 8,000.00 = $3,589.25, almost a third of all the interest.

4. Exam style. An investor deposits $15,000 in an account paying 6% a year, compounded annually. The value of the account after 8 years is closest to:

  • P/Y left at the factory 12: the BA II Plus divides 6 by 12 and computes 8 months at 0.5%.

  • Simple interest: 15,000 × (1 + 0.06 × 8). It leaves out the interest on interest.

  • Right: 15,000 × 1.068 = 15,000 × 1.593848 = 23,907.72.

Explanation

BA II Plus (P/Y = 1): 2ND CLR TVM 8 N 6 I/Y 15000 +|− PV 0 PMT CPT FV → FV = 23,907.7211.

TI-84 Plus: N=8, I%=6, PV=−15000, PMT=0, FV=0, P/Y=1, C/Y=1, END; ALPHA ENTER on FV= → FV=23907.7211.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why does a compounded sum grow faster than a simple-interest sum?

Model answer

With compounding, each period’s interest is added to the balance, so the next period’s interest is earned on a bigger amount: interest earns interest. Simple interest pays the same PV × r every period, so the gap widens the longer the money is left and the higher the rate.

A complete answer has: (1) each period’s interest is added to the balance; (2) the next period’s interest is earned on that larger balance (interest on interest); (3) the gap grows with time and with the rate.
