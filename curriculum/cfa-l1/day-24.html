From Day 17. A 5% corporate bond pays its coupon semiannually. Settlement is 60 days into the 180-day coupon period (30/360), and the bond is quoted at 101.20 per 100. What does the buyer pay per 100?

  • Right. One period’s coupon is 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5, so AI = (60/180) × 2.5 = 0.8333, and the full price is 101.20 + 0.8333.

  • This uses the whole annual coupon: (60/180) × 5 = 1.6667. A semiannual period pays only half of it.

  • This subtracts the accrued interest. The quote is the flat price; the buyer pays flat plus AI, the seller’s share of the coming coupon.

Why

Bonds are quoted flat. The invoice is the full price: flat + AI, with AI = (t/T) × one period’s coupon.

The pilot who wasn’t there

Around 1950, Lieutenant Gilbert S. Daniels was a young physical anthropologist at the Air Force’s Aero Medical Laboratory, at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio. He had body measurements for 4,063 pilots.

Cockpits were built to fit the “average” pilot. So Daniels asked a plain question: how many pilots actually were average?

He took ten body dimensions used in design, such as height, chest circumference and sleeve length. On each one he kept only the men inside a narrow “approximately average” band, and dropped the rest.

Height alone left 1,055 of the 4,063. Add chest circumference: 302. Sleeve length: 143. Crotch height: 73. Torso circumference: 28.

By the tenth dimension, nobody was left. Not one of the 4,063 was average all over.

He published the result in December 1952, in a technical note with a pointed title: “The ‘Average Man’?”

The average height, the average chest and the average sleeve length were all perfectly good numbers. So why did a cockpit built to them fit no one? And what number was missing?

The idea

An average answers one question: where is the middle? It says nothing about how far things stray from the middle. That second number is dispersion, and for an investor it is the first measure of risk.

Picture two funds that both average 5% a year. Fund A earns 5% every year. Fund B earns +30%, then −20%. Same average; one is a smooth ride, the other a roller coaster.

And the swings cost money. Fund B turns $1 into 1.30 × 0.80 = $1.04 over the two years: only 1.98% a year compounded (Day 12’s geometric mean).

Today’s measures, from crude to precise:

  • Range: highest minus lowest. Quick, but it looks at only two numbers.
  • Mean absolute deviation (MAD): the average distance from the mean.
  • Variance and standard deviation (SD): the average squared distance, and its square root. The SD is the workhorse of risk.
  • Coefficient of variation (CV): SD per unit of mean return, to compare funds with different averages.
  • Target downside deviation: like the SD, but it counts only the returns that fall short of a target.

Why it works

Start with the deviations. Five returns, 6, −2, 10, 3, 8 (%), have mean 25/5 = 5 (Day 12). Each return’s deviation, Xi − X̄, is its signed distance from 5: +1, −7, +5, −2, +3.

Averaging them fails: they always add to 0. The mean is the balance point, so misses above it cancel misses below.

Fix 1: drop the signs. Average the sizes: (1 + 7 + 5 + 2 + 3)/5 = 3.6. That is the MAD. Finance mostly uses fix 2.

Fix 2: square them. Squares are never negative, and big misses count extra: a 7-point miss scores 49, a 1-point miss 1. Here they add to 88. Their average is the variance, in %² (percent squared).

Undo the square. Its square root, back in percent, is the standard deviation. In plain words: variance is the average squared miss, and the SD is the size of a typical miss.

Why n − 1 for a sample. X̄ sits in the very middle of its own sample: no other point gives a smaller sum of squares (about 5 it is 88; about 4 or 6, 93). The true mean μ is almost never exactly X̄, so 88 understates the real scatter. Dividing by n − 1 = 4, not 5, corrects that on average.

Another view: the deviations must add to 0, so once four are known (+1, −7, +5, −2), the fifth is forced (+3). Only n − 1 are free: n − 1 degrees of freedom.

Population. With every member of the group you know μ itself, so divide by N.

Risk per unit of return. CV = s/X̄ puts funds with different means on one scale. Lower CV, less risk per point of return.

Only the bad misses. Upside surprises hurt nobody, so keep only the returns at or below a target B and measure each from B, not from the mean. Square, add, divide by n − 1 with n counting every observation (a year above B is a zero shortfall), and take the root.

Memorize
  • Sample variance: s² = Σ (Xi − X̄)² / (n − 1)
  • Population variance: σ² = Σ (Xi − μ)² / N
  • Mean absolute deviation: MAD = Σ |Xi − X̄| / n
  • Coefficient of variation: CV = s / X̄ (risk per unit of mean return)
  • Target downside deviation (target semideviation): sTarget = √[ Σ(Xi ≤ B) (Xi − B)² / (n − 1) ] — sum over observations at or below the target B, divide by n − 1 using all n

Worked example

Problem. Five annual returns: 6%, −2%, 10%, 3%, 8%. Find the range, the MAD, the sample variance and standard deviation (and the population versions), the CV, and the target downside deviation for a target B = 4%.

By hand
  1. Mean: X̄ = (6 − 2 + 10 + 3 + 8)/5 = 25/5 = 5%. Range: 10 − (−2) = 12 percentage points.
  2. Deviations from 5: +1, −7, +5, −2, +3 (they add to 0).
  3. MAD = (1 + 7 + 5 + 2 + 3)/5 = 18/5 = 3.6%.
  4. Squared deviations: 1, 49, 25, 4, 9. Sum = 88.
  5. Sample: s² = 88/(5 − 1) = 22.0 (%²); s = √22 = 4.6904%.
  6. Population (if these five years were the whole group): σ² = 88/5 = 17.6; σ = 4.1952%.
  7. CV = s/X̄ = 4.6904/5 = 0.9381.
  8. Downside, B = 4%: only −2 and 3 are at or below 4. Shortfalls: −2 − 4 = −6 and 3 − 4 = −1. Squares: 36 + 1 = 37.
  9. 37/(5 − 1) = 9.25; √9.25 = 3.0414%.

Back to the story

The missing number was the spread. On height alone, only 1,055 of the 4,063 pilots, 26%, fell inside Daniels’s “average” band: real people scatter widely around the mean. Each extra dimension cut the group again, 302, 143, 73, 28, until nobody was left. A design sized to ten averages was sized for a man who did not exist.

Variance and standard deviation measure that scatter: the typical distance of an observation from its mean.

Returns behave the same way. The calculator fund averaged 5% a year, yet no year came closer than 2 points to 5% (its nearest was 3%). Its sample standard deviation of 13.18% says a typical year misses the average by roughly 13 points. Its downside deviation of 6.45% measures only the shortfalls below 0%.

An average return, like the average pilot, describes almost no actual year.

Exam traps
  • Dividing by n when the sample variance is asked. Sample means n − 1; only a whole population divides by N.
  • Reading σx (population) instead of Sx (sample). Both calculators show them one line apart; return data are almost always a sample, so read Sx.
  • In downside deviation, dividing by the number of below-target observations instead of n − 1. In the walkthrough, dividing by the 2 shortfalls gives 10.20% instead of 6.45%.
  • Reporting variance in %² as if it were a percentage. A variance of 173.6 is not 173.6%; the risk figure in percent is the SD, 13.18%.

2. Why. Why does the sample variance divide by n − 1 rather than n?

  • n − 1 is not a cushion. Dividing by n would understate the true variance on average; n − 1 removes that bias, so the estimate is right on average, not deliberately high.

  • No observation is special: every one, the first included, has a deviation from X̄. The lost degree of freedom comes from estimating X̄ from the same data.

  • Right. X̄ gives the smallest possible sum of squares: in the worked example, 88 about 5 but 93 about 4 or 6. One degree of freedom is used up by X̄.

Explanation

Because the deviations from X̄ must add to zero, only n − 1 of them are free to vary. Dividing the sum of squares by those n − 1 degrees of freedom makes the sample variance right on average. With every member of a population, μ is known exactly, nothing is used up, and you divide by N.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what does a fund’s standard deviation of returns tell you, and how does its target downside deviation differ?

Model answer

The standard deviation is the typical distance of the fund’s returns from their mean: the deviations are squared so their signs cannot cancel, averaged (dividing by n − 1 for a sample) and square-rooted back to percent. The target downside deviation keeps only the returns at or below a target B, measures them from B and still divides by n − 1, so upside surprises are not counted as risk.

A complete answer says: (1) the SD is the typical distance of returns from their mean, squared so signs cannot cancel, then rooted back to %; (2) the sample version divides by n − 1; (3) downside deviation counts only returns at or below the target B, still dividing by n − 1 using all n, so returns above the target add no risk.
