From Day 46. A fund’s prospectus claims a monthly standard deviation of 2.0%. Over 16 months the sample SD is 2.5%. To test whether the true variance is higher than claimed, what is the χ2 statistic? To two decimals.

Hint χ2 = (n − 1)s2 / σ02. Square both SDs first.
Solution

χ2 = (16 − 1) × 2.52 / 2.02 = 15 × 6.25 / 4 = 93.75 / 4 = 23.44, with 15 df.

The 5% upper critical value for 15 df is 24.996, so 23.44 falls short: fail to reject (TI-84 p = 0.0753). The sample variance is 56% above the claim, but 16 months cannot prove it.

BA II Plus: 15 × 2.5 x² ÷ 2 x² = → 23.4375. TI-84 Plus: 15 × 2.5 x² ÷ 2 x² ENTER → 23.4375.

From Day 25. Two funds’ returns have a sample covariance of 12 (%2). Their standard deviations are sX = 4% and sY = 6%. Their correlation is:

  • Right: r = sXY / (sX sY) = 12 / (4 × 6) = 12 / 24.

  • That divides by the variances, 16 × 36 = 576, instead of the SDs.

  • That is 12 / 16: sX twice, no sY. It is Cov / Var(X), which is today’s slope, not the correlation.

Why

r = 12 / 24 = 0.50. Keep option C in mind: covariance divided by the variance of X, 12 / 16 = 0.75, is exactly the least-squares slope you build today.

Galton and the not-quite-as-tall children

In 1886 Francis Galton published a paper with a blunt title: “Regression towards mediocrity in hereditary stature”, in the Journal of the Anthropological Institute.

His data were family heights: 930 adult children of 205 sets of parents, by the count in his text. His summary table adds up to 928.

Two choices made the families comparable. To put men and women on one scale, he multiplied every female height by 1.08. And he summarized each couple by one number, their average: the “mid-parent”.

Then he set each child against its mid-parent. Tall parents had tall children, as you would expect. But on average the children were less extreme than their parents. The children of short parents were short, but less short.

How much less? The children’s distance from the average was about two-thirds of the mid-parent’s: “as 2 to 3”, in his words.

He called it regression towards mediocrity, and his word stuck: the method is still called regression.

Why should the children of exceptional parents be less exceptional? And of all the lines you could draw through 928 scattered points, which one is the “best”?

The idea

Plot each family as a dot: mid-parent X along the bottom, child Y up the side. You get a tilted cloud. The simplest rule for predicting Y from X is a straight line: Ŷ = b̂0 + b̂1X.

Y is the dependent variable, the one being explained. X is the independent (explanatory) variable. Ŷ, “Y-hat”, is the prediction; hats mark numbers estimated from a sample.

Every dot misses the line by a vertical gap, its residual: e = Y − Ŷ. Vertical, because the job is predicting Y.

Least squares picks the one line whose squared residuals add up to the least. Squared for Day 24’s reasons: misses above and below cannot cancel, and a big miss counts extra.

Reading the line: the slope b̂1 is how much the predicted Y changes when X rises by 1. The intercept b̂0 is the predicted Y when X = 0, which only means something if X = 0 is near the data.

When to trust it. The tests and forecasts built on the line assume four things about the errors:

  • Linearity: the true relation is a straight line.
  • Homoskedasticity: the errors spread equally at every X.
  • Independence: one error says nothing about the next.
  • Normality: the errors are normally distributed.

Check them with a residual plot (residuals against X): you want a shapeless band around zero. A curve means not linear; a widening fan, heteroskedasticity; long same-sign runs over time, dependent errors.

Why it works

Score a line. For any candidate line, point i misses by ei = Yi − (b̂0 + b̂1Xi). Score the line by SSE = Σei2, the sum of squared errors. Lowest score wins.

Step 1: the intercept is Day 24. Hold the slope fixed. Then ei = (Yi − b̂1Xi) − b̂0: one number, b̂0, taken from each of the numbers Yi − b̂1Xi. Day 24: the single number with the smallest sum of squared deviations is the mean. So b̂0 = mean of (Y − b̂1X) = Ȳ − b̂1X̄.

Two results follow. The residuals add to zero, as deviations from a mean do. And at X = X̄ the line gives Ŷ = Ȳ − b̂1X̄ + b̂1X̄ = Ȳ: it passes through the point of means.

Step 2: measure from the means. Put that b̂0 back: ei = (Yi − Ȳ) − b̂1(Xi − X̄). Call the deviations yi and xi: ei = yi − b̂1xi. Only the slope is left.

Step 3: find the bottom. Square and add: SSE = Σy2 − 2b̂1Σxy + b̂12Σx2. As the slope changes, that traces a U (a parabola). The bottom of Ab2 + Bb + C sits at b = −B / (2A), so b̂1 = 2Σxy / 2Σx2 = Σxy / Σx2.

Test it on the worked example below (Σx2 = 10, Σxy = 9, Σy2 = 10): SSE = 10 − 18b + 10b2. Slope 0.8 scores 2.0, slope 0.9 scores 1.9, slope 1.0 scores 2.0. The bottom: 18 / 20 = 0.9.

Step 4: recognize it. Divide top and bottom by n − 1 (it cancels): b̂1 = Cov(X, Y) / Var(X), Day 25’s covariance over Day 24’s variance. And Cov = r sX sY (Day 25), so b̂1 = r sY / sX.

Another view. Σxy / Σx2 = Σx2(y/x) / Σx2: each point’s own slope from the centre, y/x, averaged with weights x2. Points far out in X get the most say; a point at X̄ gets none. Worked example: own slopes 1, 1, 0, 1, weights 4, 1, 1, 4, so (4 + 1 + 0 + 4) / 10 = 0.9.

In plain words: put the line through the middle of the dots, and tilt it until the up-and-down misses, squared, are as small as they can be.

Memorize
  • Least squares slope: b̂1 = Cov(X, Y) / Var(X) = Σ(Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ) / Σ(Xi − X̄)2 = r × sY / sX
  • Least squares intercept: b̂0 = Ȳ − b̂1 × X̄
  • Residual: ei = Yi − Ŷi = Yi − (b̂0 + b̂1 Xi)
  • Four assumptions of simple linear regression: Linearity; homoskedasticity (constant error variance); independence of errors; normally distributed errors
  • BA II Plus LIN output names: a = intercept b̂0; b = slope b̂1; r = correlation

Worked example

Problem. Five countries’ money-supply growth X (%) and inflation Y (%): (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 6). Fit Ŷ = b̂0 + b̂1X by least squares, predict inflation at X = 6%, and find the residual of the third country.

By hand
  1. Means: X̄ = 15 / 5 = 3; Ȳ = 20 / 5 = 4.
  2. Deviations: x = −2, −1, 0, +1, +2; y = −2, −1, +1, 0, +2.
  3. Σxy = (−2)(−2) + (−1)(−1) + (0)(1) + (1)(0) + (2)(2) = 4 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 4 = 9.
  4. Σx2 = 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10.
  5. b̂1 = 9 / 10 = 0.9. (Same as Cov / Var = 2.25 / 2.5: the n − 1 cancels.)
  6. b̂0 = Ȳ − b̂1X̄ = 4 − 0.9 × 3 = 1.3. The line: Ŷ = 1.3 + 0.9X.
  7. Prediction at X = 6: 1.3 + 0.9 × 6 = 6.7%.
  8. Residual of country 3: Y − Ŷ = 5 − (1.3 + 0.9 × 3) = 5 − 4.0 = +1.0: one point more inflation than the line predicts.
  9. All five residuals: −0.2, −0.1, +1.0, −0.9, +0.2. They add to 0, and their squares add to 1.9: the bottom of the U above.

On your calculator

Problem. Five families (illustrative, not Galton’s records). Mid-parent heights X (inches) = 64, 66, 68, 70, 72; adult child heights Y = 66.0, 66.4, 68.6, 68.2, 70.3. Find the intercept, slope and correlation, then predict the child’s height for a mid-parent of 75 inches.

  1. Clear the old data and open the data screen

    BA II Plus 2ND DATA 2ND CLR WORK (the screen shows X01)
    TI-84 Plus STAT 1:Edit…; cursor up onto the L1 title, CLEAR ENTER; then onto the L2 title, CLEAR ENTER

  2. Enter the families: the mid-parent (what you predict from) as X, the child as Y

    BA II Plus 64 ENTER ↓ 66 ENTER ↓ 66 ENTER ↓ 66.4 ENTER ↓ 68 ENTER ↓ 68.6 ENTER ↓ 70 ENTER ↓ 68.2 ENTER ↓ 72 ENTER ↓ 70.3 ENTER (X01, Y01, X02, Y02 … Y05)
    TI-84 Plus down L1 from its first row: 64 ENTER 66 ENTER 68 ENTER 70 ENTER 72 ENTER; then down L2 from its first row: 66 ENTER 66.4 ENTER 68.6 ENTER 68.2 ENTER 70.3 ENTER

    Check: Y05 = 70.3000 (BA II Plus) / five rows in each list (TI-84).

  3. Run the regression: read the intercept a, the slope b and r

    BA II Plus 2ND STAT, 2ND SET until LIN shows; ↓ eight times, past n, x̄, Sx, σx, ȳ, Sy and σy, to a; ↓ to b; ↓ to r
    TI-84 Plus 2nd MODE (QUIT), STAT ► (CALC) 8:LinReg(a+bx) 2nd 1 , 2nd 2 ENTER (the line reads LinReg(a+bx) L1,L2). Not 4:LinReg(ax+b): there a is the slope. No r on screen? Turn on DiagnosticOn as on Day 25.

    Display: on the way x̄ = 68.0000 and ȳ = 67.9000, then a = 32.5400, b = 0.5200, r = 0.9416 (BA II Plus) / a=32.5400, b=.5200, r²=.8866, r=.9416 (TI-84; r² is Day 48). The line: Ŷ = 32.54 + 0.52X.

  4. Predict the child of a 75-inch mid-parent

    BA II Plus from r, ↓ to X', 75 ENTER, ↓ to Y', CPT
    TI-84 Plus VARS 5:Statistics… ► ► (EQ) 2:a + VARS 5:Statistics… ► ► (EQ) 3:b × 75 ENTER (the line reads a+b*75)

    Display: Y' = 71.5400 (BA II Plus) / 71.5400 (TI-84). By hand: 32.54 + 0.52 × 75 = 71.54.

  5. Check that the line runs through the means: predict at X = X̄ = 68

    BA II Plus ↑ to X', 68 ENTER, ↓ to Y', CPT
    TI-84 Plus 2nd ENTER (ENTRY) brings the last line back; ◄ ◄, type 68 over the 75, ENTER

    Display: Y' = 67.9000 (BA II Plus) / 67.9000 (TI-84): exactly Ȳ. That is b̂0 = Ȳ − b̂1X̄ at work.

Answer: Ŷ = 32.54 + 0.52X, r = 0.9416. A 75-inch mid-parent predicts a 71.54-inch child.

Back to the story

Of all the lines through Galton’s cloud, the best is the least-squares one: smallest sum of squared vertical misses, through the point of means, slope r × sY / sX.

That answers his puzzle too. Subtract the means and divide by the SDs: (Ŷ − Ȳ) / sY = r × (X − X̄) / sX. With r below 1, every prediction sits fewer SDs from the average than its parent. Parents explain only part of a child’s height, so the best guess leans back toward the middle.

Galton’s two-thirds is his slope, not r. A mid-parent averages two people, so it varies less than single children (sY > sX), and his r was lower still. A slope of two-thirds means a mid-parent 3 inches above average predicts a child about 2 inches above.

Today’s five families: slope 0.52. A 75-inch mid-parent, 7 above the mean of 68, predicts 71.54: only 3.64 above the children’s mean of 67.9. In SDs: 2.21 for the parent, 0.9416 × 2.21 = 2.08 for the prediction.

Exam traps
  • Swapping X and Y. You predict the child from the parent, so the parent is X. Enter the children as X and you fit a different line: slope 1.7049 (Cov / Var(Y) = 5.2 / 3.05), not 0.52, and not 1 / 0.52 = 1.9231 either. Each line minimizes misses in its own Y.
  • Reading a as the slope. On the LIN screen a = 32.5400 is the intercept and b = 0.5200 the slope. Swap them and a 75-inch mid-parent “predicts” a 2,441.02-inch child. On the TI-84, 8:LinReg(a+bx) uses the same letters as the BA; 4:LinReg(ax+b) swaps them.
  • Taking the intercept literally. a = 32.54 is the predicted child of a 0-inch mid-parent: nonsense, because X = 0 is far outside the data (64 to 72). It anchors the line; it is not a fact about anyone. The same goes for predictions beyond the data: even today’s 75 is a step past 72.
  • Mixing sample and population SDs. r × Sy / Sx and r × σy / σx both give 0.5200, since the n − 1 cancels. Mix them, r × Sy / σx, and you get 0.5814.

2. Why. Why does the least-squares line always pass through the point of means (X̄, Ȳ)?

  • It always passes through them, whatever r is: walkthrough step 5 had r = 0.9416 and still got Ȳ back at X̄.

  • Standardized means are both 0, but that is not the reason. The line runs through the means in the original units, as step 5 showed with 68 and 67.9.

  • Right. Minimizing Σe2 over the intercept is Day 24’s problem: the best single number is a mean. That forces the residuals to sum to zero and puts (X̄, Ȳ) on the line.

Explanation

With the slope fixed, each point offers the number Y − b̂1X, and the intercept that minimizes the squared misses is their mean, Ȳ − b̂1X̄. Put X = X̄ into Ŷ = (Ȳ − b̂1X̄) + b̂1X and the slope terms cancel, leaving Ŷ = Ȳ.

3. Twist: no data, only summary statistics. The correlation between a fund’s return (Y) and the market’s (X) is 0.6. Their standard deviations are sY = 8% and sX = 5%, and their means are Ȳ = 3% and X̄ = 2%. What return does the least-squares line predict when the market returns 4%? In percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint Build the slope from r and the two SDs (Why it works, step 4). Then use the fact that the line runs through the means.
Solution

b̂1 = r × sY / sX = 0.6 × 8 / 5 = 0.96. b̂0 = Ȳ − b̂1X̄ = 3 − 0.96 × 2 = 1.08. Ŷ = 1.08 + 0.96 × 4 = 4.92%.

Quicker, from the means: Ŷ = Ȳ + b̂1(X − X̄) = 3 + 0.96 × (4 − 2) = 4.92%. Using r as the slope gives 4.20; flipping the SDs (5 / 8) gives 3.75.

BA II Plus (AOS): 3 + 0.6 × 8 ÷ 5 × ( 4 − 2 ) = → 4.9200. TI-84 Plus: the same keys, ending ENTER → 4.9200.

4. Exam style. An analyst regresses a stock’s return Y (%) on an economic indicator X. After entering the data, the BA II Plus LIN screen shows a = 1.20 and b = 0.45. The predicted return when X = 4 is closest to:

  • That is 0.45 + 1.20 × 4: a read as the slope and b as the intercept. On the BA II Plus, a is the intercept.

  • Right: Ŷ = a + bX = 1.20 + 0.45 × 4 = 3.00.

  • That is (4 − 1.20) / 0.45: the line solved backwards for X, as if 4 were a value of Y.

Explanation

Ŷ = 1.20 + 0.45 × 4 = 3.00. On the calculator itself: in LIN, ↓ to X', 4 ENTER, ↓ to Y', CPT → Y' = 3.0000.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what does least squares minimize, and what does the slope tell you?

Model answer

Least squares picks the line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical misses, Σ(Y − Ŷ)2, and that line passes through the means (X̄, Ȳ). Its slope, Cov(X, Y) / Var(X), is how much the predicted Y changes when X rises by one unit.

A complete answer says: (1) it minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals, Y − Ŷ; (2) the slope is the change in predicted Y per one-unit change in X, equal to Cov / Var(X); (3) the line passes through the means.
