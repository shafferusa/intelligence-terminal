Sleeping Beauty’s hundred-year promise

July 1993. The Walt Disney Company borrowed $300 million by selling bonds that would not mature until 15 July 2093.

The coupon was 7.55%, and the bonds were sold at par: buyers paid $100 for each $100 of face value. In return Disney promised $7.55 a year on every $100, for up to a century, and the $100 back at the end.

Wall Street called them “Sleeping Beauty” bonds, after the princess who slept for a hundred years. They were the first 100-year bonds sold by an American company since 1954. Within days Coca-Cola sold century bonds of its own.

Paid in full, that is $755 of coupons for every $100 lent, plus the $100 itself: $855 in all. Yet buyers paid just $100, and the last of it would not arrive for a hundred years.

So what is a century of coupons, plus $100 repaid in 2093, worth today? And how much of that value comes from the $100 at the end?

The idea

A plain bond makes two promises. It pays a fixed coupon every period, and it repays its par value (face value, quoted as 100) at maturity.

You already price both. The coupons are an ordinary annuity (Day 5). The par value is a single sum (Day 2). The bond’s price is the two PVs added.

The rate to discount at is the market yield (the market discount rate): the return investors demand today on a bond like this one. Not the coupon rate. The coupon rate was fixed when the bond was sold, and it sets the cash. The yield changes every day, and it sets the price.

Picture owning an apartment rented out with the rent fixed for good. If rents on new leases rise, it is worth less to anyone buying it from you. If they fall, it is worth more. Bonds work the same way: yield up, price down.

That gives three cases. Coupon rate equal to the yield: the price is par, 100. Coupon below the yield: a discount, under 100. Coupon above it: a premium, over 100.

Two market habits. Prices are quoted per 100 of par. And US bonds usually pay twice a year, so you work in half-years: half the coupon, half the yield, twice the years.

Why it works

Build it from Days 2 and 5 with a 2-year bond: 8% annual coupon, par 100.

  1. List the cash. A coupon, PMT, at the end of each period, and par, FV, with the last coupon. Here: 8 at t = 1, then 8 + 100 = 108 at t = 2.
  2. Price each payment (Day 2). Discount each one at the market yield r. At r = 10%: 8/1.10 = 7.2727 and 108/1.102 = 89.2562. Price = 96.5289.
  3. Group them (Days 5 and 2). The coupons are an annuity: 8 × [1 − 1.10−2]/0.10 = 8 × 1.735537 = 13.8843. The par is a single sum: 100/1.21 = 82.6446. Total: 96.5289 again. TVM with both PMT and FV filled in does exactly this.
  4. Coupon = yield gives par. 100 in a bank at 8% pays 8 a year and gives the 100 back after two years: the bond’s cash exactly. So at r = 8% the bond is worth the deposit: 8/1.08 + 108/1.1664 = 7.4074 + 92.5926 = 100.0000.
  5. Higher yield, lower price. The cash is fixed; only r moves. A bigger r shrinks every discount factor 1/(1 + r)t, so the sum shrinks. At 10% the bond is a discount (96.5289); at 6% it is a premium: 7.5472 + 96.1196 = 103.6668.
  6. Where a discount buyer’s return comes from. The coupon pays 8 where the buyer wants 10. Paying less than 100 makes up the gap: at a steady 10% the price climbs to 108/1.10 = 98.1818 with a year left, and to 100 at maturity. This drift toward 100, up for a discount bond and down for a premium one, is the pull to par (the curriculum’s constant-yield price trajectory).
  7. Semiannual and zero-coupon bonds. Twice-yearly coupons: PMT = annual coupon/2, r = annual yield/2, N = 2 × years. A zero-coupon bond has PMT = 0, which leaves only Day 2’s single sum.

In plain words: a bond’s price is what its coupons and its final 100 are worth today, at the return you could get elsewhere.

PV = PMT/(1 + r)1 + PMT/(1 + r)2 + … + (PMT + FV)/(1 + r)N

PV = PMT × [1 − (1 + r)−N]/r + FV/(1 + r)N

Zero-coupon: PV = FV/(1 + r)N

PV
the bond’s price today, quoted per 100 of par
PMT
coupon per period: coupon rate × par ÷ payments per year
FV
par (face) value, repaid at maturity with the last coupon; 100 in a quote
r
market discount rate (yield) per period, as a decimal; never the coupon rate
N
number of coupon periods to maturity
[1 − (1 + r)−N]/r
Day 5’s annuity factor: what 1 a period for N periods is worth today
Memorize
  • Price of a fixed-rate bond: PV = PMT/(1 + r)1 + PMT/(1 + r)2 + … + (PMT + FV)/(1 + r)N
  • Price of a zero-coupon bond: PV = FV / (1 + r)N
  • Semiannual-pay bond inputs (P/Y = 1): PMT = annual coupon/2, rate = annual yield/2, N = 2 × years
  • Price vs par: coupon rate > yield → premium; coupon rate < yield → discount; equal → par

Worked example

Problem. Price per 100 of par: (a) a 3-year bond with a 5% annual coupon when the market yield is 6%; (b) a 3-year zero-coupon bond at 6%.

By hand
  1. Year 1 coupon: 5/1.06 = 4.7170.
  2. Year 2 coupon: 5/1.062 = 5/1.1236 = 4.4500.
  3. Year 3 coupon + principal: 105/1.063 = 105/1.191016 = 88.1600.
  4. (a) Price = 4.7170 + 4.4500 + 88.1600 = 97.3270: a discount, because the 5% coupon is below the 6% yield.
  5. Same by the formula: coupons 5 × 2.673012 = 13.3651, plus par 100/1.191016 = 83.9619, makes 97.3270.
  6. (b) Zero: 100/1.063 = 83.9619, the par piece of (a) on its own.
  7. Check the direction: at a 4% yield the same coupon bond is worth 102.7751, a premium.
  8. Pull to par: a year later, with 2 years left and the yield still 6%, it is worth 98.1666; with 1 year left, 105/1.06 = 99.0566.

Back to the story

The $855 of promised cash was never worth $855, because most of it lay decades away. At Disney’s own 7.55% the whole bond was worth exactly 100.00: coupon equal to yield, so par.

And the $100 due in 2093? Worth six cents in 1993. The coupons carried 99.94 of the 100.00. The worked example’s 3-year bond is the other way round: its principal is 83.96 of its 97.33.

So a century bond is nearly a perpetuity (Day 8). At 9%: 3.775/0.045 = 83.8889, within a quarter of a cent of the 200-period price, 83.8913. With the coupons fixed, the yield alone moves the price: 187.06 at 4%, 83.89 at 9%.

A coda. The 2093 repayment will never happen. The bonds could be called from 15 July 2023, and on that date Disney redeemed the $201 million still outstanding, 70 years early.

Exam traps
  • Discounting at the coupon rate. Always discount at the market yield. At its own coupon rate every bond prices at exactly 100, whatever its maturity, so a “100.00” answer to a question whose yield differs from the coupon is a red flag.
  • Annual inputs on a semiannual bond. Halve the coupon, halve the yield, double N. Type the full 7.55 as PMT with N = 200 and I/Y = 4.5, and Disney’s bond “costs” 167.7676 instead of 83.8913.
  • Mixed signs. Coupons and par are both money you receive: enter PMT and FV positive, and PV comes back negative, the price you pay. Enter PMT = −5 with FV = 100 and the worked example’s bond shows PV = −70.5969 instead of −97.3270.
  • Premium and discount swapped. Ask whether the coupon is generous next to today’s yield. Coupon above the yield: premium. Below: discount.

2. Why. Market yields rise from 5% to 6%. Why does the price of a bond already issued with a fixed 5% coupon fall?

  • No: a fixed-rate bond’s coupon is written into its terms and never changes. The cash stays the same; only what it is worth today changes.

  • Right: price = Σ CF/(1 + r)t with every CF fixed, so a larger r shrinks every term.

  • A downgrade is one reason a bond’s yield can rise, but it is not why price and yield move in opposite directions. A default-free government bond falls too when market yields rise.

Explanation

The numerators of the price formula are fixed by contract; only the denominators move. A 10-year 5% annual-pay bond is worth 100.0000 at a 5% yield and 92.6399 at 6%. In a buyer’s terms: nobody pays 100 for 5 a year when new bonds pay 6 a year for 100, so the old bond’s price falls until it too returns 6%.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what is a bond’s price, and when is it above par?

Model answer

A bond’s price is the present value of its coupons (an annuity) plus the present value of its par value (a single sum), all discounted at the market yield rather than the coupon rate; because that cash is fixed, the price falls when the yield rises, and it sits above par (a premium) when the coupon rate is higher than the yield, below par (a discount) when it is lower, and at par when they are equal.

A complete answer says: (1) price = PV of the coupons + PV of the principal, discounted at the market yield; (2) coupon rate > yield → premium, < → discount, = → par; (3) price and yield move in opposite directions, because the cash flows are fixed.
