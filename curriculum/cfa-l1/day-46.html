Volmageddon: the day the fear gauge more than doubled, and how to test whether a variance really rose

__DATELINE__ · 20 min

Risk is a variance, and you test a variance with a ratio: F = s12 / s22 compares two of them, χ2 = (n − 1)s2 / σ02 checks one against a claimed value.

Volmageddon: the day the fear gauge doubled

Friday, 2 February 2018. The Cboe Volatility Index, the VIX, closed at 17.31. The VIX is Wall Street’s “fear gauge”: a measure of how much the S&P 500 is expected to swing, computed from S&P 500 option prices.

Monday, 5 February. It closed at 37.32, up 115.6% in one day: its largest one-day percentage rise on record. The next biggest on Cboe’s list, on 27 February 2007, was 64.2%.

Some investors had bet on calm. XIV, an exchange-traded note built to profit when volatility stayed low, lost more than 90% of its value.

On 5 February its intraday indicative value fell to 20% or less of the prior day’s close. Under the note’s terms that was an “acceleration event”, and on 6 February Credit Suisse said it would redeem the note early.

It expected trading to end on 20 February. Nasdaq halted the note sooner, after the close on Thursday 15 February.

Everyone could see that volatility had jumped. But a few wild days happen even in calm markets. How do you test, with numbers, whether one period’s variance is really bigger than another’s, or bigger than the value a risk model assumed, and not just noise?

The idea

Days 44 and 45 tested means: is the average return different from a value, or from another fund’s? Today tests risk: the variance.

One variance against a claimed value. A risk model assumes a daily SD of 1.5%. Do the data agree? That is the chi-square (χ2) test.

Two variances against each other. Was the fund riskier after the shock than before? Were Day 45’s two funds really equally volatile, as the pooled t-test assumed? That is the F-test.

Both use a ratio, not a difference. With variances, proportion is what matters: 4 against 1 is four times the risk; 104 against 101 is the same gap of 3 but a ratio of only 1.03. Equal variances give a ratio of 1. The test asks how far from 1 is too far to be luck.

When the assumptions fail. t, χ2 and F are parametric tests: they are about a parameter (μ, σ2) and assume a distribution, usually normal. A non-parametric test assumes less, often working on ranks. Use one when the data are ranks, when the sample is small or has outliers, when normality fails, or when the question is not about a parameter at all. Day 25’s Spearman rank correlation is one. The price: less power when the parametric assumptions do hold.

Counts in categories (value or growth; beat or missed) get a χ2 test of independence: observed counts against those expected if the categories were unrelated. Its 2027 coverage is uncertain: insurance.

Why it works

Start from Day 24. The sample variance is s2 = Σ(X − X̄)2 / (n − 1). Multiply both sides by n − 1: (n − 1)s2 = Σ(X − X̄)2, the sum of squared deviations.

Measure each deviation in claimed SDs. Divide by the claimed variance σ02: (n − 1)s2 / σ02 = Σ[(X − X̄) / σ0]2. Each term is a squared z-score (Day 30): how many claimed SDs an observation sits from the mean, squared.

Name the sum. If returns are normal and σ0 is the truth, a sum of squared z-scores follows the chi-square distribution. It has n − 1 degrees of freedom, not n: the deviations from X̄ must add to zero, so the last one is forced (Day 24’s reason for n − 1).

See what it measures. A squared z-score averages 1, so χ2 averages n − 1. Rearranged, χ2 / (n − 1) = s2 / σ02: the statistic is the variance ratio, scaled up by n − 1. If s2 equals the claim, χ2 = n − 1 exactly (20 for 21 days). Far above: more variance than claimed. Far below: less.

Two samples: divide one ratio by the other. Take s12 / σ2 and s22 / σ2 and divide. If both populations share one σ2, it cancels: F = s12 / s22. No claimed value is needed, but there are two samples, so F has two df: n1 − 1 on top, n2 − 1 below.

Why the larger goes on top. Squares are never negative, so both distributions are skewed right and their two tails are not mirror images. With the larger variance on top, F ≥ 1 and only the upper tail matters, which is the tail F tables print. Two-tailed at 5%? Compare with the 2.5% upper value.

Counts. If style and beating the benchmark are independent, P(value and beat) = P(value) × P(beat) (Day 26). Times the N funds: E = N × (row/N) × (column/N) = row × column / N. χ2 = Σ(O − E)2 / E adds each cell’s squared surprise, scaled by what was expected. Once the totals are fixed, only (r − 1)(c − 1) cells are free to vary (one, in a 2 × 2 table): those are its df.

In plain words: divide one variance by another and ask whether the answer is too far from 1 to be luck.

χ2 = (n − 1) s2 / σ02, df = n − 1

F = s12 / s22 (s12 ≥ s22), df = n1 − 1 and n2 − 1

s2
the sample variance: the sample SD squared, in %2
σ02
the variance claimed in H0 (a claimed SD of 1.5% means σ02 = 2.25)
n
the number of observations; n − 1 is the degrees of freedom
s12, n1
the larger sample variance and its sample size (numerator)
s22, n2
the smaller sample variance and its sample size (denominator)
χ2, F
test statistics, never negative; compare each with a critical value from its own table
Memorize
  • Chi-square test of a single variance: χ2 = (n − 1) s2 / σ02, df = n − 1
  • F-test of two variances: F = s12 / s22 (larger sample variance on top), df = n1 − 1 and n2 − 1
  • When to use a non-parametric test: data are ranks, or parametric assumptions (e.g. normality) fail: small samples, outliers; or the hypothesis is not about a parameter
  • Expected count in a contingency table: Eij = (row i total × column j total) / overall total
  • Chi-square test of independence (2027 coverage uncertain): χ2 = Σ (Oij − Eij)2 / Eij, df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

Worked example

Problem. (a) Manager A’s monthly returns have s = 5.2% and manager B’s s = 3.9%, each over 25 months. Test at 5% whether their variances differ. (b) Insurance: of 50 value funds, 30 beat their benchmark and 20 did not; of 70 growth funds, 28 beat it and 42 did not. Test at 5% whether beating the benchmark is independent of style.

By hand
  1. (a) H0: σA2 = σB2; Ha: σA2 ≠ σB2 (two-tailed).
  2. F = larger variance / smaller = 5.22 / 3.92 = 27.04 / 15.21 = 1.7778; df = (24, 24).
  3. Two-tailed at 5%, larger on top: compare with the 2.5% upper value, F(24, 24) = 2.27. 1.78 < 2.27: fail to reject equal variances.
  4. (b) Totals: rows 50 (value) and 70 (growth); columns 58 beat and 62 missed; overall 120.
  5. Expected = row × column / overall: value-beat 50 × 58 / 120 = 24.1667; value-missed 50 × 62 / 120 = 25.8333; growth-beat 70 × 58 / 120 = 33.8333; growth-missed 70 × 62 / 120 = 36.1667.
  6. Every cell is 5.8333 from its expected count, so each (O − E)2 = 34.0278. χ2 = 34.0278/24.1667 + 34.0278/25.8333 + 34.0278/33.8333 + 34.0278/36.1667 = 1.4080 + 1.3172 + 1.0057 + 0.9409 = 4.6719 (the rounded terms add to 4.6718).
  7. df = (2 − 1)(2 − 1) = 1; critical value 3.841. 4.67 > 3.841: reject independence. Value funds beat 60% of the time, growth funds 40%.

Back to the story

The VIX is an annualized implied standard deviation, in percent. For a daily figure divide by √252 = 15.8745 (about 252 trading days in a year): 17.31 / 15.8745 = 1.09% before and 37.32 / 15.8745 = 2.35% after.

The F-test compares variances, not SDs: F = (37.32 / 17.31)2 = 2.15602 = 4.6482.

Suppose a month of 21 trading days before and 21 after had shown those daily SDs. With (20, 20) df the 5% one-tailed critical value is 2.12. 4.65 is more than twice that (TI-84 p = 0.0006): the rise is not noise.

The χ2 test asks the companion question: does one period fit a single assumed value, such as the calm volatility a note like XIV was in effect betting on? Twenty-one days at 2.35% against an assumed 1.09%: χ2 = 20 × 4.6482 = 92.96, far above 31.41.

One caution: shock days are fat-tailed (Day 30), exactly where the normality behind χ2 and F is weakest. A non-parametric check is a sensible second opinion.

Exam traps
  • Smaller variance on top. 1.21 / 4 = 0.3025 looks like nothing next to an upper critical value of 1.93. Put the larger on top so F ≥ 1, and compare with the upper tail (the α/2 value for a two-tailed test).
  • SDs instead of variances. 2.0 / 1.1 = 1.8182 is below 1.93, so you would wrongly fail to reject; 4 / 1.21 = 3.3058 rejects. Same in χ2: 20 × 2 / 1.5 = 26.6667 misses 31.410, while the right 35.5556 clears it. Square first.
  • Treating χ2 and F as symmetric. With 20 df, χ2 averages 20, but its 5% cut-offs are 10.851 (9.149 below) and 31.410 (11.410 above). Look each tail up on its own; never mirror one.
  • n instead of n − 1. 21 × 4 / 2.25 = 37.3333 is wrong; the multiplier and the df are both n − 1 = 20.

2. Why. Why does the F-test put the larger sample variance on top?

  • A test of equal variances (Ha: ≠) stays two-tailed: with the larger on top you compare with the α/2 upper value, 2.5% at a 5% level. The convention picks which tail to look in; it does not remove the other one.

  • Right. A ratio below 1 is just the same comparison upside down, so ordering the variances lets one tail of the table cover both cases.

  • F is skewed right. With df (20, 30) its 2.5% cut-offs are 0.43 and 2.20: 0.57 below 1 but 1.20 above it.

Explanation

F is a ratio of two variances, so it can never be negative and its distribution leans right. The lower-tail critical value is the reciprocal of an upper-tail value with the df swapped: for df (20, 30) at 2.5%, 0.4258. Putting the larger variance on top makes F ≥ 1, and then the upper tail, the one the tables give, is all you need.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: when would you choose a non-parametric test (for example, a rank correlation) over a parametric one?

Model answer

When the data are ranks, or the parametric test’s assumptions fail (a small sample, outliers, returns that are clearly not normal), or when the question is not about a parameter at all; a non-parametric test works on ranks or signs and assumes little about the distribution, at the cost of less power when the parametric assumptions do hold.

A complete answer says: (1) the data are ranks, or the assumptions such as normality fail: small samples, outliers; (2) the hypothesis may not concern a parameter; (3) the trade-off: less power than the parametric test when its assumptions hold.
