Black Monday, 1987

On Friday 16 October 1987 the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 108.35 points, or 4.6%, to 2,246.74. A bad day.

On Monday 19 October the selling began in Asian markets, moved through Europe and reached New York. By the close the Dow had lost 508 points, or 22.6%, finishing at 1,738.74. Friday and Monday together took about 26% off the index.

About 604 million shares changed hands on the New York Stock Exchange, roughly three times a normal day. It is still the largest one-day percentage fall in the Dow’s history.

Now suppose daily returns follow a normal curve, the familiar bell, with a mean of 0.05% and a standard deviation of about 1%. How many standard deviations was that Monday? How long should you expect to wait for a day like it? And what does it mean that it happened at all?

The idea

Many things pile up around an average and thin out evenly on both sides: heights, measurement errors and, as a first guess, daily returns. Drawn as a curve, that is the bell: the normal distribution.

Two numbers pin it down completely: the mean μ, where the peak sits, and the standard deviation σ (Day 24), how wide it spreads. So for any result only one question matters: how many standard deviations from the mean is it? That count is the z-score.

Once you have z, the odds are the same on every normal curve. About 1 result in 6 lands more than 1σ below the mean; about 1 in 44 more than 2σ below; about 1 in 741 more than 3σ below.

Real returns bend the bell in two ways. Skewness: one tail is longer than the other. Kurtosis: more of the action sits far out in the tails than the bell allows, which is called fat tails.

Value at risk (VaR) lives in that left tail: the loss you should expect to exceed only on, say, the worst 5% of days.

Why it works

Start from Day 24. The standard deviation is the typical distance from the mean: the natural ruler for “how unusual?”

Step 1: measure from the middle. Subtract the mean. A −2% day, with a mean of 0.05%, sits −2 − 0.05 = −2.05 points from average.

Step 2: count in standard deviations. Divide by σ. With a daily σ of 1%, that is −2.05 standard deviations.

Step 3: why one table fits every normal curve. Every normal curve is the same bell, slid to sit at μ and stretched to width σ. Subtracting μ slides it back to 0; dividing by σ squeezes it to width 1. (Day 24 confirms it: subtracting μ leaves every deviation unchanged; dividing by σ divides every deviation, and so the SD, by σ: σ/σ = 1.)

Every point moves together, so no area changes; only the axis labels do: P(X < x) = P(Z < z).

Step 4: the bands. On the standard curve, 68% of outcomes lie within ±1, 90% within ±1.645 (5% per tail), 95% within ±1.96 (2.5% per tail), 99% within ±2.58 (0.5% per tail). By symmetry, P(Z < −a) = 1 − P(Z ≤ a), so a table of positive z’s covers losses too. Back to returns: X = μ + zσ.

Skew and kurtosis use the same z. Average z3 over the data and you get (roughly) skewness: cubes keep the sign, so a long left tail makes it negative. Average z4 for kurtosis: the fourth power lets the tails dominate (z = 3 counts 81 times as much as z = 1). For a normal curve it is exactly 3, hence excess kurtosis = kurtosis − 3.

Mean, median, mode (Day 23). A long tail drags the mean toward it; the median barely moves; the mode stays at the peak. Long left tail: mean < median < mode.

In plain words: z counts how many “usual-sized steps” a result is from average, and the bell tells you how often results land that far out.

X = μ + z × σ

Memorize
  • Standard normal z-value: z = (X − μ) / σ
  • Normal confidence bands: 68% ≈ μ ± 1σ; 90% = μ ± 1.645σ; 95% = μ ± 1.96σ; 99% = μ ± 2.58σ
  • One-tailed critical z values: 5% one tail: 1.645; 2.5%: 1.96; 1%: 2.33
  • Skewness and the mean–median–mode order: Positive skew (long right tail): mean > median > mode; negative skew (long left tail): mean < median < mode
  • Excess kurtosis: Excess kurtosis = sample kurtosis − 3; > 0 (leptokurtic) means fatter tails than the normal
  • Value at risk (describe only): The minimum loss expected over a period a given small % of the time (e.g. on the worst 5% of days): a left-tail percentile of returns

Worked example

Problem. Annual returns on an equity portfolio are normal with mean 8% and standard deviation 16%. (a) What is the probability of a loss, P(return < 0)? (b) What interval holds 95% of one year’s outcomes? (c) A sample of daily returns has mean 0.04%, median 0.07% and kurtosis 5.1. Describe its skew and its tails.

By hand
  1. (a) Standardize: z = (0 − 8) / 16 = −0.50. A loss means landing more than 0.5 SD below the mean.
  2. The z-table gives P(Z ≤ 0.50) = 0.6915. By symmetry, P(Z < −0.50) = 1 − 0.6915 = 0.3085: a 30.85% chance of a losing year.
  3. (b) 95% band: μ ± 1.96σ = 8 ± 1.96 × 16 = 8 ± 31.36, so −23.36% to 39.36%.
  4. (c) The mean (0.04%) is below the median (0.07%): a longer left tail of large losses has dragged the mean down. That is negative skew.
  5. Excess kurtosis = 5.1 − 3 = 2.1 > 0: leptokurtic. Fatter tails than the normal, so extreme days happen more often than a z-table implies.

Back to the story

Standardize the crash: z = (−22.6 − 0.05) / 1 = −22.65. On this normal curve, a day at least that bad has a probability of about 7 × 10−114: written out, a decimal point and 113 zeros before the first nonzero digit.

At 252 trading days a year, you would expect to wait about 6 × 10110 years for one; the universe is about 1.4 × 1010 years old. Even Friday’s −4.6% (z = −4.65) was a once-in-about-2,400-years day on this curve, and Monday was nearly five times worse.

Yet it happened. The normal curve is the wrong model for the tails: real daily returns have excess kurtosis (fat tails) and negative skewness, and huge falls come far more often than the bell says.

It is a fair guide to ordinary days and a dangerous one to extreme days. A VaR built on it understates exactly the losses that matter most.

Exam traps
  • Using 1.96 for a one-tailed 5% cutoff. 1.96 leaves 2.5% in each tail (the 95% band); 5% in one tail is 1.645. In the walkthrough, 0.05 − 1.96 = −1.91% is the 2.5% cutoff, not the 5% one (−1.595%).
  • Dividing by the variance instead of the standard deviation. In the worked example, (0 − 8) / 256 = −0.031 would put the chance of a loss near 49% instead of 30.85%. z divides by σ, which is in the same units as X.
  • Getting the mean–median order backwards. The mean is dragged toward the long tail: negative skew (long left tail) means mean < median < mode; positive skew means mean > median > mode.
  • Calling a kurtosis of 3 “fat-tailed”. 3 is the normal curve’s own kurtosis (excess kurtosis 0). Fat tails (leptokurtic) means kurtosis above 3, excess kurtosis above 0.

2. Why. Why can one z-table serve every normal distribution, whatever its mean and standard deviation?

  • Normal curves come in every width: the walkthrough’s σ is 1%, c1’s is 4.5%. Standardizing exists precisely because they differ.

  • Right. Sliding the bell back to 0 and squeezing it to width 1 moves every point together, so no area changes: P(X < x) = P(Z < z).

  • z divides by σ, not σ2. The variance is in %2, so dividing a distance in % by it does not even cancel the units, and it gives the wrong z: −0.03 instead of −0.50 in the worked example.

Explanation

Every normal curve is one bell, slid to μ and stretched by σ. Undo both and you have the standard normal, whose table works for all of them. That is why a daily −1.95% (mean 0.05%, σ 1%) and a monthly −9% (mean 1%, σ 5%) both sit at z = −2.00 and both have probability 2.28%.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why is Black Monday evidence that daily returns have fat tails, and what does that mean for a risk measure built on the normal curve?

Model answer

With a daily σ of about 1%, a −22.6% day is z ≈ −22.65, and under a normal curve its probability is about 7 × 10−114: it should essentially never happen, yet it did. So real returns have far more extreme outcomes than the normal allows (excess kurtosis above 0, often with negative skew), and a value at risk computed from the normal curve understates how big and how frequent the worst losses are.

A complete answer says: (1) under the normal model a 22-standard-deviation day has essentially zero probability; (2) it happened, so the tails are fatter than normal: excess kurtosis > 0 (leptokurtic), often with negative skew; (3) normal-based risk measures such as VaR understate tail risk.
