From Day 50. A fund’s 30 monthly returns are regressed on the market’s. The slope (beta) is 0.85 with a standard error of 0.10. Testing H0: β = 1 at 5%, two-tailed (critical t = 2.048 with 28 df):

  • Right: t = (0.85 − 1)/0.10 = −1.50, and |−1.50| is below 2.048.

  • 0.85/0.10 puts 0 in the numerator: it tests β = 0, not β = 1.

  • Divided 0.10 by √30 again, as if it were a standard deviation. The regression’s standard error of the slope is already sb̂1.

Why

t = (b̂1 − B1)/sb̂1 = (0.85 − 1)/0.10 = −1.50, with n − 2 = 28 df. |t| < 2.048, so fail to reject. That is not proof that beta is 1: the 95% interval, 0.85 ± 2.048 × 0.10, still runs from 0.65 to 1.05.

Minus $37.63 a barrel

On Monday 20 April 2020, the May contract for West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil on NYMEX had one day left before it expired. It settled at −$37.63 a barrel: the first negative price since the contract was listed 37 years earlier. During the session it traded lower still; the widely reported low is −$40.32.

The pandemic had collapsed demand for fuel. The contract ends in real barrels, delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma, and working storage at Cushing was near capacity. Anyone still long at expiry had to take oil there, with almost nowhere to put it. So holders paid others to take it off their hands.

The same day, the June contract, for the same oil delivered a month later, settled at +$20.43. The physical spot price at Cushing was −$36.98.

How can oil for May delivery be worth −$37.63, and the same oil a month later +$20.43? What ties today’s price to the price for later delivery?

The idea

A forward contract is a deal made today to buy something later, at a price fixed today: the forward price F. Signing costs nothing. At delivery the buyer (the long) pays F and gets the asset.

Two ways to own a barrel of oil in three months. Route 1: buy it today at the spot price S, with borrowed money, and store it. Route 2: sign a forward today and pay F on delivery.

Both end with the same barrel on the same day, so they must cost the same. If the forward were dearer, you would buy spot, store it and sell forward: a riskless profit (cash-and-carry). Cheaper, and you would do the reverse. That is no arbitrage, the logic of Day 37’s forward rates.

What separates F from S is the cost of carry: interest on the money, plus storage and insurance, minus any income the owner collects (dividends, coupons, the convenience of having oil on hand).

F above S is contango; F below S is backwardation. Futures are exchange-traded forwards, like the WTI contract, marked to market daily: each day’s gains and losses are settled in cash.

A commodity futures investor earns three returns: price (the spot price’s change), roll (from selling each expiring contract and buying the next; negative in contango) and collateral (interest on the cash posted).

Why it works

1. No carry: Day 1. Route 1 costs S0 today. Borrow it at r and at T you repay S0(1 + r)T. Route 2 must cost the same: F0(T) = S0(1 + r)T. T is in years: three months is 0.25. Oil at $20, r = 1%: 20 × 1.010.25 = 20.0498.

2. Add the costs. Storage must be paid too. Add its present value, PV(C) (Day 2), to the price and finance the lot: F0(T) = [S0 + PV(C)](1 + r)T. With $2 of storage: 22 × 1.010.25 = 22.0548.

3. Take off the income. If the asset pays a dividend or coupon before T, the owner gets it; the forward buyer does not. Its present value, PV(I), comes off: F0(T) = [S0 − PV(I) + PV(C)](1 + r)T.

4. Arbitrage holds it there. If the forward traded at 23: borrow 22, buy and store the barrel, sell forward at 23. At T, repay 22.0548 and keep 0.9452, risk-free. At 21, a holder sells her oil, invests the 22 (20 plus the storage saved) and buys it back forward: 1.0548 a barrel.

5. Continuous version: Day 4. With continuous compounding, costs and income become yields c and i that add to or come off the rate: F0(T) = S0e(r + c − i)T.

6. Price is not value. F0 is chosen so the contract is worth zero when signed. Later, at time t, a new forward for the same date costs Ft. The long can lock in the difference by selling one: at T she buys at F0, sells at Ft and keeps Ft − F0, for sure. A sure amount at T is worth its PV now (Day 2), discounted over the time left, T − t.

7. Check at delivery. At T a forward for immediate delivery costs the spot price ST, and nothing is left to discount: VT = ST − F0, the long’s payoff.

In plain words: the forward price is today’s price plus what it costs to hold the thing until delivery. A forward’s value is how far the price has moved since you locked yours in, brought back to today.

F0(T) = S0 × (1 + r)T

F0(T) = [S0 − PV0(I) + PV0(C)] × (1 + r)T

F0(T) = S0 × e(r + c − i)T

Vt(T) = [Ft(T) − F0(T)] / (1 + r)(T − t)

F0(T)
forward price agreed at time 0 for delivery at T
S0
spot price today
r
risk-free rate per year, as a decimal (continuously compounded in the e formula)
T
time to delivery, in years (3 months = 0.25)
PV0(I)
present value of the income the asset pays before T (dividends, coupons)
PV0(C)
present value of the costs of holding it to T (storage, insurance)
c, i
continuously compounded cost and income (or convenience) yields, per year
t
time since the contract was signed, in years; T − t is the time left
Ft(T)
the forward price at time t for the same delivery date T
Vt(T)
value at t of the long forward; the short’s value is the same number with the sign flipped
Memorize
  • Forward price with no carry: F0(T) = S0 × (1 + r)T
  • Forward price with income and costs: F0(T) = [S0 − PV0(I) + PV0(C)] × (1 + r)T
  • Forward price, continuous compounding: F0(T) = S0 × e(r + c − i) T
  • Value of a long forward at time t: Vt(T) = [Ft(T) − F0(T)] / (1 + r)(T − t)
  • Commodity futures return components: Total return = price return + roll return + collateral return

Worked example

Problem. A stock trades at 100 and will pay a dividend of 2 in three months. The risk-free rate is 4% a year. Find the no-arbitrage price of a six-month forward. Then: over a year, a long commodity futures position had a price (spot) return of +5%, a roll return of −3% (contango) and a collateral return of 4%. What was its total return?

By hand
  1. Times in years: the dividend comes at 0.25, delivery at T = 0.5.
  2. PV(I) = 2 / 1.040.25 = 2 / 1.009853 = 1.9805.
  3. S0 − PV(I) = 100 − 1.9805 = 98.0195.
  4. F0 = 98.0195 × 1.040.5 = 98.0195 × 1.019804 = 99.9607, about 99.96.
  5. Check another way: carry the price, 100 × 1.040.5 = 101.9804, and take off the dividend carried from month 3 to month 6, 2 × 1.040.25 = 2.0197. 101.9804 − 2.0197 = 99.9607. Same.
  6. Ignoring the dividend gives 101.98: too high, because the forward buyer never gets the dividend.
  7. Commodity futures: total = 5% + (−3%) + 4% = 6%.

Back to the story

Run the formula backwards. With interest rates near zero, (1 + r)T is close to 1, so F − S ≈ PV(C) − PV(I): the gap between later and now is the cost of carry.

On 20 April 2020, June minus May was 20.43 − (−37.63) = $58.06 a barrel; June minus spot, 20.43 + 36.98 = $57.41. That was the market’s price for holding a barrel about a month.

Anyone with an empty tank could be paid $37.63 to take a May barrel, sell it forward for June at $20.43 and lock in $58.06, less the storage: the cash-and-carry trade that normally closes the gap. Almost no one had an empty tank. Storage, not oil, was the scarce good, so the near price fell until someone with space was paid to take the barrels.

Contango, at its most extreme. Anyone rolling into the next contract sold cheap and bought dear: a negative roll return.

Exam traps
  • Getting the signs backwards. Costs (storage, insurance) are added to the spot; income (dividends, coupons, convenience yield) is subtracted. Oil at 20 with storage of 2: finance 22 (F = 22.0548), not 18 (18.0448).
  • Using months for T. Three months is T = 0.25. Typing 3 gives 22 × 1.013 = 22.6666 instead of 22.0548.
  • Confusing the forward price with its value. F0 = 22.0548 is a price, fixed at the start; the contract’s value then is 0. A month later the long is worth −4.0481.
  • Discounting the value over the wrong time. Use the time left, T − t (2/12 here): −4.0481. Over the full 0.25 you get −4.0447; not discounting at all, −4.0548.

2. Why. Why must storage costs raise the forward price of a commodity?

  • The forward price is not a forecast. Nothing in F0(T) = [S0 − PV(I) + PV(C)](1 + r)T is a guess about the future spot price; it is the cost of the route that delivers the same barrel.

  • A seller who buys spot and stores it has no price risk at all: the barrel is already hers. No risk premium appears in the no-arbitrage price.

  • Right. Whoever delivers can buy today and store, so her cost is [S0 + PV(C)](1 + r)T; the storage is part of the price of getting a barrel to T.

Explanation

Route 1 (buy, store, finance) and route 2 (the forward) deliver the same barrel on the same day. Storage is a cost of route 1, so it must be in the price of route 2. In the walkthrough, $2 of storage lifts the forward from 20.0498 to 22.0548. Price the forward lower and everyone takes the forward; higher, and cash-and-carry traders sell it until the gap closes.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what is the difference between a forward’s price and its value, and what does the cost of carry say about the −$37.63 day?

Model answer

The forward price is the delivery price fixed at the start, set at the spot price carried forward, [S − PV(I) + PV(C)](1 + r)T, so the contract is worth zero when signed; its value later is the present value of how far the forward price has moved since, [Ft − F0]/(1 + r)(T − t). Because the gap between later and near prices is the cost of carry, when storage ran out in April 2020 the price of carrying a barrel for a month jumped to about $58, and the near price had to fall far below the later one, even below zero.

A complete answer says: (1) the forward price is set at inception so the contract’s value is zero; (2) its value later is the PV of (new forward price − old); (3) F = [S − PV(I) + PV(C)](1 + r)T, so a huge cost of storage can push the near price far below the later one, even below zero.
