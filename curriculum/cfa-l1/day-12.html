The fund whose average year was +20.7%

In 2020, Cathie Wood’s ARK Innovation ETF, ticker ARKK, was the star fund of the year. Investors piled into its bets on disruptive technology, and it gained about 152.5%.

Then the bets turned. ARKK lost about 23.4% in 2021. In 2022, as rising interest rates hammered growth stocks, it lost about 67.0%, and in late December it hit a five-year low.

Now average the three years the way you would average test scores: (152.5 − 23.4 − 67.0) ÷ 3 = +20.7% a year. That looks like a healthy fund.

Follow real money instead. $10,000 put in at the start of 2020 was worth about $25,250 a year later. Two years after that it was worth about $6,383 (using the rounded yearly returns): a loss of about 36%.

How can a fund average +20.7% a year and still lose more than a third of your money? And which average tells the truth?

The idea

An average squeezes many numbers into one. The real question is: one number that keeps what the same? Today’s three means keep different things, so each answers a different question.

The arithmetic mean keeps the sum. Add the returns, divide by how many. It answers “what does a typical single year look like?” It is the best guess for next year.

The geometric mean keeps the ending wealth. It answers “what one steady return, earned every year, would turn my starting money into what I actually ended with?” That is the honest record of compound growth.

The harmonic mean keeps the number of shares when you put the same money in each time. It answers “what did I really pay per share?”

An everyday picture for that one: drive 60 miles at 30 mph, then 60 miles back at 60 mph. Your average speed is not 45. The trip took 2 hours plus 1 hour: 120 miles in 3 hours, 40 mph. You spent more time going slowly. Equal money works the same way: it buys more shares when the price is low.

For positive numbers that are not all equal, the order never changes: harmonic < geometric < arithmetic.

Why it works

Arithmetic: keep the sum. You want one return R̄ that, used for every year, gives the same total: n × R̄ = R1 + R2 + … + Rn. Divide both sides by n. For ARKK: 62.1 ÷ 3 = 20.7.

But wealth multiplies. From Day 11, $1 becomes (1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + Rn). The sum of the returns appears nowhere in that.

Geometric: keep the wealth. You want one return RG that, earned every year, ends in the same place: (1 + RG)n = (1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + Rn).

Undo the power. Take the n-th root of both sides and subtract 1: the formula below. It is Day 2’s r = (FV/PV)1/N − 1, with FV/PV = the product of the growth factors.

Why the geometric mean sits lower. +50% then −50% averages 0%, but $100 → $150 → $75. The loss hit the bigger base. Every swing costs compound growth, and bigger swings cost more.

A rough rule. RG ≈ R̄ − σ2/2, where σ is how far returns typically sit from their mean. For ±10%: 0 − 0.102/2 = −0.50%, against a true −0.5013%. For ±50%: −12.5%, against a true −13.40%. Rough when swings are big.

Harmonic: keep the shares. Put the same money M in at each of n prices P1…Pn. Shares bought: M/P1 + … + M/Pn. Money spent: n × M. Cost per share = money ÷ shares = nM ÷ (M/P1 + … + M/Pn). The M cancels, leaving n ÷ (1/P1 + … + 1/Pn). Low prices buy more shares, so they weigh more: that is why it sits lowest.

In plain words: the arithmetic mean is the typical year; the geometric mean is the steady pace that gets your money to the same place; the harmonic mean is what you really paid per share.

Memorize
  • R̄ = (R1 + R2 + … + Rn) / n
  • RG = [(1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + Rn)]1/n − 1
  • X̄H = n / Σ(1 / Xi) (average cost per share when equal money is invested each period)
  • Positive values, not all equal: harmonic < geometric < arithmetic
  • RG ≈ R̄ − σ2/2 (rough; poor when swings are large)

Worked example

Problem. (a) You invest $1,200 in a fund each month for three months, at share prices of $20, $25 and $40. What is your average cost per share? (b) A stock gains 50% and then loses 50%. Find its arithmetic and geometric mean returns.

By hand
  1. (a) Shares bought: 1,200 ÷ 20 = 60; 1,200 ÷ 25 = 48; 1,200 ÷ 40 = 30. Total: 138 shares for $3,600.
  2. Average cost = 3,600 ÷ 138 = $26.0870.
  3. Harmonic mean of the prices: 3 ÷ (1/20 + 1/25 + 1/40) = 3 ÷ 0.115 = 26.0870. The same number.
  4. Arithmetic mean of the prices: (20 + 25 + 40) ÷ 3 = 28.3333. Too high: equal money bought twice as many shares at $20 as at $40.
  5. (b) Arithmetic mean = (50% − 50%) ÷ 2 = 0.00%.
  6. Geometric mean = (1.50 × 0.50)1/2 − 1 = 0.751/2 − 1 = 0.8660 − 1 = −13.40%. You really are down: $100 → $150 → $75.

Back to the story

Both averages are correct arithmetic. They answer different questions.

The arithmetic mean, +20.70%, answers “what was a typical single year?” Pick one of the three years at random and +20.70% is what you should expect. It says nothing about money that stayed for all three.

Wealth compounds, so the honest three-year summary is the geometric mean, −13.90% a year. Three steady years at −13.90% take $10,000 to $8,610, then $7,413, then $6,383: the same place as the real path through $25,250 and $19,341.50.

The gap between the two, 34.60 percentage points, is the cost of volatility. A 152.5% gain and a 67.0% loss sound like a net gain, yet together they turn $1 into $0.83: the loss struck the much bigger base the gain had built.

So the +20.7% “average year” was not false. Quoting it as the fund’s growth rate would be. (All of this uses the rounded yearly returns.)

Exam traps
  • The arithmetic mean as compound growth. ARKK’s +20.70% “average” lost 36% of the money. Past multi-year growth: geometric. Next year’s expected return: arithmetic.
  • A geometric mean of the returns themselves. Always use the growth factors (1 + R), and subtract 1 at the end. The cube root of 152.5 × (−23.4) × (−67.0) is 62.07, which is nonsense: the two minus signs cancel and the losses turn into gains.
  • The harmonic mean in the wrong place. It is for prices paid with the same money each time, not for returns. Buy the same number of shares each time (10 at each of $20, $25 and $40) and your cost per share is the plain average, $28.33.
  • A leftover entry in the data list. Read n before x̄. n = 4 on a three-year problem means an old X04 (or a fourth number in L1) is still there.

2. Why. A fund’s yearly returns are not all equal. Why is its geometric mean return always below its arithmetic mean return?

  • Every year goes in, a loss as a growth factor below 1: ARKK’s −67% went in as 0.33. Leaving losses out would push the geometric mean up, not down.

  • Right. +50% then −50% averages 0%, but $100 → $150 → $75; the other order goes $100 → $50 → $75. Either way 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75. The bigger the swings, the bigger the gap.

  • Both means are computed from the same returns, so dividends cannot open a gap between them. Price-only returns of +50% and −50% show the gap with no dividend anywhere.

Explanation

The arithmetic mean treats +50 and −50 as cancelling. Wealth does not, because each return multiplies whatever the earlier ones left. That is why the geometric mean falls short by roughly σ2/2. Only when every return is the same do the two means match.

5. Say it back. A fund has a 10-year record. Which mean would you report as its past growth rate, and which as your estimate of next year’s return? Say why, in one or two sentences.

Model answer

For the past ten years I would report the geometric mean, because it is the one steady yearly return that compounds the starting money into the actual ending wealth; for next year I would use the arithmetic mean, because it is the expected return of a single year. The more the returns swing, the further the geometric mean falls below the arithmetic one.
