From Day 3. A deposit pays 8% a year. The interest is taxed at 25% and inflation is 3%. The after-tax real return is closest to:

  • This taxes the real return: (1.08 ÷ 1.03 − 1) × 0.75. Tax falls on the whole 8% of nominal interest, so take it off first.

  • The subtraction shortcut, 6% − 3%. Rates multiply: you must divide by 1.03.

  • Right. After tax: 8% × 0.75 = 6%. Then 1.06 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 2.91%.

Why

Two steps, in this order: tax the nominal interest (8% → 6%), then remove inflation exactly, 1.06 ÷ 1.03 = 1.029126.

Irene Triplett’s $73.13

On 31 May 2020, Irene Triplett died in a nursing home in Wilkesboro, North Carolina, aged 90. She was the last person drawing a pension from the American Civil War, a war that had ended in 1865, 155 years earlier.

The pension came through her father. Mose Triplett fought first for the Confederacy and then for the Union. Irene was born in 1930; he died in 1938, aged 92.

Because of her cognitive disability, the Department of Veterans Affairs classed her as a “helpless child” of a veteran. It paid her $73.13 a month.

A small cheque, arriving every month for decades. Now suppose someone had offered her one lump sum in place of the next ten years of cheques: 120 payments, each a month after the last. What would be a fair price? Surely not the full 120 × $73.13 = $8,775.60. And what does the answer depend on?

The idea

Day 2 priced one future payment. Today prices a whole row of them.

An annuity is a series of equal payments, A each, one per period, for N periods. A pension, a car-loan payment, a bond’s coupons, $500 saved every month: all annuities.

In an ordinary annuity each payment comes at the end of its period. The first arrives at t = 1, one period from now, and the last at t = N. That is the calculator’s END setting. (Payments at the start of each period make an annuity due: Day 6.)

Two questions. What is the whole stream worth today (its PV)? And what would it grow to if every payment were banked until the last one (its FV)?

Nothing here is new. Each payment is a single sum from Days 1 and 2; the annuity formula is just a fast way to add N of them.

One rule carries over from Day 4: the rate and N must match the payment. Monthly payments need a monthly rate (stated rate ÷ 12) and N counted in months. P/Y stays 1.

On the calculator, N, I/Y, PV, PMT and FV are tied by one equation: know any four, and CPT finds the fifth.

Why it works

Build it from Day 2, with small numbers alongside: $100 at the end of each of 2 years, at 10%.

  1. Each payment is a single sum. From Day 2, A paid at time t is worth A / (1 + r)t today. Here: 100 / 1.10 = 90.91 and 100 / 1.21 = 82.64.
  2. The annuity is their sum. PV = A/(1 + r) + A/(1 + r)2 + … + A/(1 + r)N. Here: 90.91 + 82.64 = 173.55. That is the whole idea; the rest is a quicker way to add.
  3. A deposit that pays A for ever. Put A/r in a bank paying r. Each period it earns r × A/r = A; take that out and the deposit never shrinks. Here: $1,000 at 10% pays $100 a year.
  4. It buys two things. The N payments, plus the A/r still in the bank at time N.
  5. Price the leftover. A/r due at N is a single sum, so today it is worth (A/r) / (1 + r)N. Here: 1,000 / 1.21 = 826.45.
  6. Subtract. The payments are worth the rest: PV = A/r − (A/r)(1 + r)−N = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r. Here: 1,000 − 826.45 = 173.55, the same as step 2.
  7. Roll it forward. Day 1 moves any sum forward: multiply step 6 by (1 + r)N to get FVN = A × [(1 + r)N − 1] / r. Here: 100 × 0.21 / 0.10 = 210.00, which is 110 + 100.

In plain words: the PV is the amount you would need in the bank today to pay yourself A at the end of every period, with the account hitting zero on the last payment. Check it: 173.55 grows to 190.91; pay 100, leaving 90.91; that grows to 100.00; pay 100, leaving 0.

PV = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r

FVN = A × [(1 + r)N − 1] / r

Memorize
  • PV = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r
  • FVN = A × [(1 + r)N − 1] / r
  • Ordinary annuity: payments at the END of each period; first payment at t = 1 (calculator END mode)
  • Monthly payments with P/Y = 1: N = years × 12; periodic rate = annual stated rate / 12

Worked example

Problem. An ordinary annuity pays $1,000 at the end of each of the next 4 years. At 6%, find its PV by adding the four single sums, then with the formula. Then find its FV at year 4.

By hand
  1. Each payment’s PV (Day 2): 1,000 / 1.06 = 943.40; 1,000 / 1.062 = 890.00; 1,000 / 1.063 = 839.62; 1,000 / 1.064 = 792.09.
  2. Add them: 943.40 + 890.00 + 839.62 + 792.09 = 3,465.11.
  3. Formula: 1.064 = 1.262477, so 1.06−4 = 0.792094.
  4. PV = 1,000 × (1 − 0.792094) / 0.06 = 1,000 × 3.465106 = 3,465.11. Same answer, one line.
  5. FV = 1,000 × (1.262477 − 1) / 0.06 = 1,000 × 4.374616 = 4,374.62.
  6. Check (Day 1): 3,465.11 × 1.262477 = 4,374.62.
  7. Check by single sums: 1,191.02 + 1,123.60 + 1,060.00 + 1,000.00 = 4,374.62. The year-1 payment earns 3 years of interest; the year-4 payment earns none.

On your calculator

Problem. Ten years of Irene Triplett’s cheques: 120 monthly payments of $73.13, the first one month from now. At a 4% stated annual rate, what is the stream worth today? Then: banked at the same rate, what would the cheques grow to by the last one? P/Y stays 1, so you feed the calculator monthly numbers yourself: N = 10 × 12 = 120 and a rate of 4 ÷ 12 percent a month.

  1. Make sure the calculator is in END mode, then clear the old numbers

    BA II Plus If BGN shows in the display, press 2ND BGN 2ND SET 2ND QUIT (no BGN means END). Then 2ND CLR TVM. P/Y is still 1 from Day 1.
    TI-84 Plus APPS 1:Finance 1:TVM Solver; on the bottom row, PMT: highlight END and press ENTER

  2. Number of payments: 120 months

    BA II Plus 120 N
    TI-84 Plus N=120 (type 120 ENTER)

  3. The monthly rate: type 4 ÷ 12 as a division, so no digits are lost

    BA II Plus 4 ÷ 12 = I/Y
    TI-84 Plus on I%= type 4 ÷ 12 ENTER

    Display: I/Y = 0.3333 (BA II Plus) / I%=.3333 (TI-84): a third of a percent a month. Both keep every digit behind the display.

  4. The payment (received, so positive) and FV = 0

    BA II Plus 73.13 PMT 0 FV (PV is the unknown; CLR TVM already set it to 0)
    TI-84 Plus PV=0, PMT=73.13, FV=0, P/Y=1, C/Y=1

  5. Solve for the present value

    BA II Plus CPT PV
    TI-84 Plus cursor on PV=, then ALPHA ENTER

    Display: PV = −7,223.0629 (BA II Plus) / PV=−7223.0629 (TI-84). Negative because it is what you would pay today to receive the cheques.

  6. The future value of the same stream: set PV back to 0, solve for FV

    BA II Plus 0 PV CPT FV
    TI-84 Plus PV=0, then cursor on FV=, ALPHA ENTER

    Display: FV = −10,768.3782 (BA II Plus) / FV=−10768.3782 (TI-84). The size is what the banked cheques grow to; the minus sign only balances 120 cheques in. Check (Day 1): 7,223.0629 × 1.490833 = 10,768.38.

  7. What the rate does: same stream at 8% stated (FV back to 0 first)

    BA II Plus 0 FV 8 ÷ 12 = I/Y CPT PV
    TI-84 Plus FV=0, I%= 8 ÷ 12 ENTER, then cursor on PV=, ALPHA ENTER

    Display: PV = −6,027.4829 (BA II Plus) / PV=−6027.4829 (TI-84).

Answer: at 4% a year, ten years of $73.13 a month are worth $7,223.06 today. Banked at the same rate, the cheques grow to $10,768.38. At 8%, the same stream is worth only $6,027.48.

Back to the story

A fair lump sum for the next ten years of cheques, at 4% a year, is $7,223.06. That is only 82% of the $8,775.60 they add up to, because later cheques are worth less today: the 120th, ten years out, is worth 67 cents on the dollar.

The bank picture gives the same answer. $21,939.00 at 4% a year (0.3333% a month) earns exactly $73.13 a month. Pay the cheques from the interest for ten years and the $21,939.00 is still there, worth $14,715.94 today. The cheques are worth the difference: $7,223.06.

And what does the price depend on? The rate: at 8%, the same ten years are worth $6,027.48. The length: thirty years at 4% are worth $15,317.90, only 2.12 times ten years, because the extra cheques are far away. Even for ever, the stream is worth just $21,939.00, which Day 8 will call a perpetuity.

Exam traps
  • Mixing periods: a monthly payment with an annual rate, or with N in years. Monthly payments need N = years × 12 and r = stated annual rate ÷ 12. Expect the mixed-up answer among the options.
  • Giving PV and PMT the same sign when PV is the price paid for the stream. Paid out is negative, received is positive; with both positive, CPT I/Y gives Error 5 (TI-84: ERR:NO SIGN CHNG).
  • Counting a payment made today as part of an ordinary annuity. Its first payment is one period away; if one comes today, it is an annuity due (Day 6).
  • Stale settings: BGN left on, or an old FV still in the calculator. Press 2ND CLR TVM, check that no BGN shows, and on the TI-84 set every line.

2. Why. An annuity pays $1,000 at the end of each year for 20 years. At 6% its PV is $11,469.92. If it paid for 40 years instead, by about how much would the PV rise?

  • That counts every payment the same, as if money had no time value. The added payments arrive in years 21 to 40, and each is worth far less today.

  • Right. The annuity factor goes from 11.4699 to 15.0463: PV $15,046.30, up 31.18%. The 20 added payments are worth only $3,576.38 together.

  • Far-off payments are worth less, not nothing: even the $1,000 in year 40 is worth $97.22 today.

Explanation

Years 21 to 40 are the same 20-year annuity, pushed 20 years out. So they are worth today’s 20-year PV times Day 2’s discount factor 1.06−20 = 0.311805: 11,469.92 × 0.311805 = 3,576.38. That is why the PV rises by 31.18%, not 100%: each added payment is a single sum discounted 21 to 40 years.

4. Exam style. An annuity pays $2,000 at the end of each month for 20 years. If the stated annual rate is 6%, compounded monthly, the present value of the annuity is closest to:

  • Right: N = 20 × 12 = 240 and I/Y = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5, with PMT = 2,000.

  • Treats the year’s 12 payments as one $24,000 payment at each year-end (N = 20, I/Y = 6). Monthly cash arrives sooner, so it is worth a little more.

  • A monthly payment with N = 20 and I/Y = 6: it values just 20 payments of $2,000 at an annual rate. Rate and N must match the payment.

Explanation

BA II Plus: 2ND CLR TVM 240 N 6 ÷ 12 = I/Y 2000 PMT 0 FV CPT PV → PV = −279,161.5434.

TI-84 Plus: TVM Solver N=240, I%= 6 ÷ 12 ENTER, PV=0, PMT=2000, FV=0, P/Y=1, C/Y=1, END; cursor on PV=, ALPHA ENTER → PV=−279161.5434.

By formula: 2,000 × [1 − 1.005−240] / 0.005 = 2,000 × 139.580772 = 279,161.54.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what does PV = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r add up, and why does the first payment sit at t = 1?

Model answer

It adds up the present values of N separate payments, each a single sum A / (1 + r)t for t = 1 to N. In an ordinary annuity each payment comes at the end of its period (END mode), so the first is a full period away; and r and N must be counted in the same periods as the payments.

A complete answer says: (1) the formula is the sum of N single-sum present values; (2) an ordinary annuity pays at the end of each period, so the first payment is at t = 1; (3) the rate and N must match the payment period (monthly payments: monthly rate, N in months).
