The best fund of the decade, and its losing investors

On 31 December 2009 the Wall Street Journal named the best-performing US stock fund of the decade: CGM Focus, run by the stock-picker Ken Heebner. Over the ten years to late November 2009 it had returned about 18% a year.

At that pace, money grows roughly fivefold in ten years.

The paper also reported a second number. Morningstar had estimated what the fund’s typical shareholder earned over the same decade: a loss of about 11% a year. At that pace, money shrinks to less than a third.

Neither number is a misprint. Much of the money had simply arrived at the wrong moment. In 2007 the fund gained about 80%. In 2008 investors poured about $2.6 billion into it. That year it fell about 48%.

Same fund, same ten years. How can the fund earn about +18% a year while the people who owned it lost about 11% a year?

The idea

A fund’s return and its investors’ return answer different questions.

The time-weighted return (TWR) follows one dollar that went in at the start and was never touched. It asks: how well did the manager invest whatever money he had? Money coming in or going out does not move it.

The money-weighted return (MWR) follows your actual dollars: how many, and when. It is the IRR of your own deposits and withdrawals (Day 10), so a year when you had a lot in counts for more than a year when you had a little.

An everyday picture: a bus runs fast on the highway and crawls through town. Its timetable is the same whoever rides. But if most riders board just before the traffic jam, the typical rider’s trip is slow, though the bus kept its timetable.

Managers rarely choose when clients add or pull money, so the TWR is the standard for judging them. The MWR is the honest number for what an investor actually earned.

Why it works

Two kinds of change. An account moves because its investments rise or fall (return) and because the owner adds or withdraws cash (an external cash flow). A deposit makes it bigger without earning anything.

A small case. She deposits 100 today. Year 1 earns 20%: 120. She deposits another 100: 220. Year 2 earns 5%: 231.

Money-weighted: Day 10’s IRR, from her side. Deposits leave her pocket: −100 today, −100 at year 1. The ending value comes back, as if she cashed out: +231 at year 2. The MWR is the rate that makes their NPV zero.

What that rate means. Try 10%. The first 100 grows for two years: 100 × 1.102 = 121. The second grows for one: 100 × 1.10 = 110. Together 231, exactly her ending value. So her MWR is 10.00%: the one rate that, earned on every dollar for as long as it was in, rebuilds her account.

Why timing counts. The second 100 was there only for year 2, so year 2 had 220 at work against year 1’s 100, and the MWR leans toward its 5%.

Time-weighted: Day 11’s chain, cut at each flow. Split the time line at every deposit or withdrawal. Inside each piece no money comes or goes, so the change is pure investment result: 120/100 − 1 = 20%, then 231/220 − 1 = 5% (year 2 starts at 220, after the deposit).

Link, then annualize. $1 left alone becomes 1.20 × 1.05 = 1.26. The deposit changed how many dollars there were, not what each one earned, so it drops out. Two years: take the square root, Day 12’s geometric mean. √1.26 − 1 = 12.25% a year.

Why they differ. More money sat in the weaker year, so MWR (10.00%) < TWR (12.25%). Money arriving before a strong year flips it. With no flows after the first deposit, the two are equal.

In plain words: the TWR asks how well one dollar did in the manager’s hands; the MWR asks how well your dollars did, given when you put them in.

CFt
the investor’s own cash flow at time t: deposits negative, withdrawals and the ending value positive
t
when each flow happens, in periods (usually years) from the first deposit
MWR
money-weighted return per period: the IRR of those cash flows
Vstart
value at the start of a sub-period, after that date’s deposit or withdrawal
Vend
value at the end of the sub-period, just before the next flow, plus any income paid out in it
HPRi
holding-period return of sub-period i
n
number of sub-periods (cut at every external cash flow)
N
number of years the whole span covers (with yearly periods, also the last t)
Memorize
  • Money-weighted return: the IRR of the investor’s cash flows: Σ CFt / (1 + MWR)t = 0
  • Time-weighted return over n sub-periods: TWR = (1 + HPR1)(1 + HPR2)…(1 + HPRn) − 1, annualized geometrically
  • When is TWR the right measure? When the manager does not control the timing of external cash flows (manager evaluation)
  • Effect of timing on MWR: MWR > TWR if more money is invested just before strong returns; MWR < TWR if before weak returns

Worked example

Problem. A client gives a manager $1,000,000. After one year the account is worth $1,150,000, and the client withdraws $400,000. A year later the remaining account is worth $690,000. Find the annualized TWR and the MWR, by hand.

By hand
  1. Cut at the withdrawal. Sub-period 1: HPR1 = 1,150,000 ÷ 1,000,000 − 1 = 15.00%.
  2. Sub-period 2 starts after it, at 1,150,000 − 400,000 = 750,000: HPR2 = 690,000 ÷ 750,000 − 1 = −8.00%.
  3. Chain: 1.15 × 0.92 = 1.058, a TWR of 5.80% over two years. Annualized: √1.058 − 1 = 1.028591 − 1 = 2.86% a year.
  4. MWR: the client’s flows are −1,000,000 today, +400,000 at year 1, +690,000 at year 2. With v = 1/(1 + r), NPV = 0 reads 690,000v2 + 400,000v − 1,000,000 = 0.
  5. Two years make it a quadratic: v = [−400,000 + √(400,0002 + 4 × 690,000 × 1,000,000)] ÷ (2 × 690,000) = (−400,000 + 1,708,800.7) ÷ 1,380,000 = 0.948406.
  6. MWR = 1/v − 1 = 1.054400 − 1 = 5.44% a year. (CF worksheet: −1,000,000, 400,000, 690,000, IRR CPT → IRR = 5.4400; TI-84: irr(−1000000,{400000,690000}) → 5.4400.)
  7. MWR above TWR: 1,000,000 worked through the good year (+15%), only 750,000 through the bad one (−8%).

Back to the story

The fund’s +18% a year is time-weighted: one dollar followed through the decade, so it measures Heebner’s stock picking. The shareholders’ −11% is money-weighted: it counts how many dollars were in each year, and a great many arrived just in time for 2008.

Take a hypothetical investor. She puts $10,000 in at the start of 2007, the +80% year: $18,000. She adds $20,000 at the start of 2008: $38,000. The fund falls 48%; she ends with $19,760, having put in $30,000.

Time-weighted: 1.80 × 0.52 − 1 = −6.40% over two years, −3.25% a year. Money-weighted: the IRR of −10,000, −20,000 and +19,760 is −27.49% a year. Two-thirds of her money arrived just before the bad year.

It is not a one-off. Morningstar’s “Mind the Gap” study found US fund investors earned about 6.0% a year in the ten years to December 2022, against 7.7% for the funds themselves.

Exam traps
  • Treating a deposit or withdrawal as a return. In the TWR, cut the time line at every external cash flow and start the next sub-period from the value after the flow. The worked example’s account fell from 1,000,000 to 690,000, but it did not lose 31.00%: 400,000 was withdrawn.
  • Signs in the IRR. Take the investor’s side: deposits negative, withdrawals and the ending value positive. Enter the walkthrough’s year-1 purchase as +118 and the “MWR” jumps to 131.86%. (Flipping every sign changes nothing: still 13.86%.)
  • Annualizing arithmetically. A two-year TWR of 5.80% is √1.058 − 1 = 2.86% a year, not 5.80 ÷ 2 = 2.90%. The bigger the returns, the bigger the gap.

2. Why. In question 1 the money-weighted return comes out far below the time-weighted return. Why?

  • They are in it: the year-1 flow nets the 1.00 dividend (−119), and the year-2 flow includes 3.00 of dividends (+174).

  • The opposite: the TWR cuts the time line at the purchase and measures year 2 from the 60 price, so new money never counts as return.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what do the money-weighted and the time-weighted return each measure, and who should use which?

Model answer

The money-weighted return is the IRR of the investor’s own deposits, withdrawals and ending value, so it counts how much money was in during each period and shows what the investor actually earned, timing included; the time-weighted return chain-links the returns of the sub-periods between cash flows, so deposits and withdrawals drop out, which makes it the standard for judging a manager who does not control when money comes and goes.

A complete answer says: (1) MWR = the IRR of the investor’s own cash flows, so the size and timing of deposits and withdrawals count (the investor’s experience); (2) TWR = sub-period returns chain-linked, with the time line cut at every cash flow, so the flows have no effect; (3) use the TWR to evaluate managers and the MWR for what the investor earned.
