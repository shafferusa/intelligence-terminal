Lending to the Treasury for nothing

On 9 December 2008, less than three months after Lehman Brothers collapsed, the US Treasury auctioned $30 billion of four-week bills. The rate came out at 0.000%.

It was the first zero rate since the Treasury began selling four-week bills in 2001. And buyers were not scarce: bids came to more than four times the amount on offer.

The slide had been fast. A week earlier the same bills had sold at 0.04%. The day before, three-month bills had gone for 0.005%, reportedly the lowest since the Treasury began auctioning bills in 1929.

A Treasury bill pays no coupon. You buy it below its face value and collect the face value when it matures; the gap is your interest. At 0.000% there was no gap.

So why would anyone lend money for four weeks for nothing? And how does a bill’s “discount rate” turn into the price an investor pays?

The idea

Money-market instruments are debts that come due within a year: Treasury bills, commercial paper, bank certificates of deposit (CDs), repos. Most pay all their interest in one lump at the end.

Their rates are quoted in two ways.

  • Discount basis (T-bills, commercial paper): the rate is applied to the face value. The discount comes off the top and you pay what is left.
  • Add-on basis (CDs, repos): the rate is applied to the money you put in, and the interest is added on top.

And the “year” can be 360 days (US T-bills, CDs and repos) or 365.

An everyday picture: a $100 jacket at 20% off costs $80. Measured against the $80 you paid, the $20 you saved is 25%. Same $20, two percentages. A discount rate is the 20%; an add-on rate is the 25%.

So two quotes that look alike can describe different deals. Before comparing, put every rate on one yardstick: the bond-equivalent yield (BEY), an add-on rate on a 365-day year.

One contrast with Day 11: money-market rates are annualized simply, scaling the period’s return by Year/Days, with no compounding.

Why it works

Start from Day 2. One period back is PV = FV / (1 + r). For part of a year, money markets use simple interest: the period’s rate is (Days/Year) × the annual rate. 90 days at 4% on a 360-day year: 90/360 × 4% = 1%.

Add-on. Put that into Day 2’s formula: PV = FV / [1 + (Days/Year) × AOR]. $100 deposited for 90 days at a 4% add-on rate comes back as 100 × 1.01 = $101.

Discount. Now the rate is applied to the face value: the discount is FV × (Days/Year) × DR, and you pay the rest, PV = FV × [1 − (Days/Year) × DR]. A $100 bill, 90 days, a 4% discount rate: $1 off, price $99.

Read both rates from the price. Solve each formula for its rate:

DR = (Year/Days) × (FV − PV) / FV
AOR = (Year/Days) × (FV − PV) / PV

Same top: FV − PV, the dollars you earn. Different bottom: the discount rate divides by the face value, the add-on rate by what you actually paid. Year/Days stretches the period’s return to a year.

So the discount rate looks smaller: the same gain divided by a bigger number. The $99 bill: DR = 4 × 1/100 = 4.0000%, but AOR = 4 × 1/99 = 4.0404%.

The yardstick. The BEY is the add-on rate with Year = 365: (365/90) × 1/99 = 4.0965% for the same bill. Any quote converts this way: price first, then the BEY.

Repos. A repurchase agreement is a short loan dressed as a sale: a dealer sells securities and agrees to buy them back, often the next day, a little higher. The difference is the interest, add-on on a 360-day year: principal × repo rate × days/360. The lender lends less than the collateral is worth; the gap is the haircut: loan = collateral value × (1 − haircut).

In plain words: a discount rate measures your profit against the number on the label; the bond-equivalent yield measures it against the money you actually paid, over a full 365-day year.

PV = FV × [1 − (Days/Year) × DR]

PV = FV / [1 + (Days/Year) × AOR]

BEY = (365/Days) × (FV − PV) / PV

Repo interest = principal × repo rate × (days/360)

Memorize
  • Discount basis price: PV = FV × [1 − (Days/Year) × DR]
  • Add-on price: PV = FV / [1 + (Days/Year) × AOR]
  • Add-on rate from price: AOR = (Year / Days) × (FV − PV) / PV
  • Bond-equivalent yield, a 365-day add-on rate: BEY = (365 / Days) × (FV − PV) / PV
  • Repo interest = principal × repo rate × (days / 360)
  • Haircut = 1 − loan / collateral value, so loan = collateral value × (1 − haircut)

Worked example

Problem. Which pays more: a 90-day T-bill quoted at a 4.90% discount rate, or a 90-day CD quoted at a 4.95% add-on rate on a 360-day year? Compare them on a bond-equivalent basis.

By hand
  1. Bill discount per 100: 100 × (90/360) × 0.049 = 1.2250.
  2. Bill price: PV = 100 − 1.2250 = 98.7750.
  3. Return on the money paid, for 90 days: 1.2250 / 98.7750 = 0.0124019.
  4. Bill BEY = (365/90) × 0.0124019 = 5.0297%.
  5. CD: already add-on, but on 360 days. Its 90-day return is (90/360) × 4.95% = 1.2375%; restated over 365 days, × 365/90 = 5.01875%. (Shortcut: 4.95% × 365/360.)
  6. The bill wins, 5.0297% to 5.01875%, although its quoted rate is lower: a discount rate is figured on face value, not on the money you pay.

Answer: bill 5.0297% vs CD 5.01875% (BEY): the bill pays more.

Back to the story

At 0.000% the formula gives PV = 100 × [1 − (28/360) × 0] = 100.00. Investors paid $100 to get $100 back four weeks later. For four weeks’ use of $30 billion, the Treasury paid nothing.

Run the week-earlier rate, 0.04%, through the same formula for 28 days: a $100,000 bill costs $99,996.89, so $3.11 of interest for four weeks, a 0.0406% bond-equivalent yield. On $30 billion, that comes to about $933,333.

So why lend for nothing? In December 2008 the return that mattered was getting the money back. Next to a bank or a fund that might fail, a Treasury bill paying nothing was still the safest place to wait. Bids for more than four times the bills on offer show how many people thought so.

Exam traps
  • Using a 365-day year for a US T-bill discount rate: it is 360. At 5.25% for 28 days, a 365-day year gives a price of 99.5973 instead of 99.5917.
  • Treating a discount rate as the return on money invested. It is figured on face value, so it understates the return: the 5.25% bill really yields 5.3447%. Always go through the price.
  • Comparing a 360-day rate with a 365-day rate without converting: a 360-day add-on rate × 365/360 is the BEY.
  • Charging repo interest on the collateral’s value. Interest is on the loan, collateral × (1 − haircut), over a 360-day year.

2. Why. A 28-day T-bill quoted at a 5.25% discount rate gives its buyer a 5.3447% bond-equivalent yield. Why is the quoted rate lower than the return?

  • Right. Both rates use the same gain, 0.4083 per 100. Dividing by 99.5917 instead of 100 lifts 5.25% to 5.2715%; a 365-day year lifts that to 5.3447%.

  • Nothing is added: both rates describe the same price and the same 100 at maturity. They differ only in what the gain is divided by and how long a year is.

  • T-bills pay no coupon. The whole return is the gap between the price and the face value.

Explanation

DR = (360/Days) × gain / FV; BEY = (365/Days) × gain / PV. The price is below the face value and 365 is more than 360, so whenever the rate is above zero the BEY is the larger of the two. At 0.000%, as in December 2008, both are zero.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why must money-market rates be put on a common basis before you compare them?

Model answer

The same-looking quote can mean different things: a discount rate is figured on face value and an add-on rate on the price paid, and the year can be 360 or 365 days. Converting each to the bond-equivalent yield, a 365-day add-on rate worked out from the price, measures every one as dollars earned per dollar invested over the same year.

A complete answer says: (1) a discount rate is on face value, an add-on rate on the price, so a discount rate understates the return; (2) quotes use 360- or 365-day years; (3) convert both to the BEY, the 365-day add-on rate, before comparing.
