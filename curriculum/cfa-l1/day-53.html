From Day 46. Fund X’s monthly returns have a sample standard deviation of 6% over 21 months; fund Y’s, 4% over 25 months. For a test of equal variances, the F-statistic and its degrees of freedom are:

F = s1² / s2² with the larger variance on top, so F ≥ 1 and the upper-tail table applies; df = (21 − 1, 25 − 1) = (20, 24).

A Sharpe ratio too good to be true

On Monday 7 November 2005 the financial analyst Harry Markopolos e-mailed the SEC a revised version of his memo, later known as “The World’s Largest Hedge Fund is a Fraud”. His case: Bernard Madoff’s firm was most likely a Ponzi scheme.

He wrote that the whole report tied into Fairfield Sentry, a fund of funds with over $5 billion invested with Madoff. Within days, an SEC examiner who had read it wrote to a colleague that he felt “much better that he is incorrect”.

Fairfield kept marketing the fund. Its sheet dated August 2007 showed a sixteen-year record with only 14 down months: a compound annual return of 10.85%, with an annual standard deviation of just 2.50%.

Over the same years, the sheet showed the S&P 100 earning 11.21% a year with a standard deviation of 14.11%. It even printed each one’s Sharpe ratio: 2.75 for Sentry, 0.51 for the index.

On 11 December 2008 the FBI arrested Madoff.

How can returns be too good? And which single number, printed on the fund’s own sheet, should have made any analyst suspicious?

The idea

A return on its own says little. Did a fund earn 12% by being clever, or by taking wild swings? To compare managers you need return per unit of risk.

Two adjustments do it. First, subtract the risk-free rate Rf, which anyone can earn in T-bills. What is left, the excess return, is what the risk bought. Second, compare that excess return with the risk taken.

The measures differ only in what they call risk:

Total risk, σ (Sharpe ratio, M²): right when the fund is all you hold, because every swing hits you.

Beta, β (Treynor ratio, Jensen’s alpha): right when the fund is one slice of a diversified portfolio. Its own (unsystematic) swings largely cancel against the other slices; only market risk is left (Day 52).

Downside only (Sortino ratio) or the worst fall (Calmar ratio): for investors who mind losses, not upside surprises.

Think of fuel economy. Miles driven doesn’t tell you which car is efficient; miles per gallon does. Return is the miles, risk is the gallons.

Why it works

Start with Day 34’s line. Mix T-bills with portfolio P, and every blend lies on a straight line: E(R) = Rf + [(Rp − Rf) / σp] × σ. Its slope is the extra return you get for each point of σ. That slope is the Sharpe ratio.

The steeper line wins. You can slide along any line by holding more or fewer T-bills. So the steeper line gives more return at every level of risk. Rank by slope, not by raw return.

M²: read the line at the market’s risk. Slide P’s line out to σ = σm. There it earns Rf + (Rp − Rf) × σm/σp. The market, at the same risk, earns Rm. Subtract: M² = (Rp − Rf) × σm/σp − (Rm − Rf). Same ranking as Sharpe, but in percentage points.

Treynor: swap σ for β. In a diversified portfolio only beta is left, so divide the excess return by βp. The market has β = 1, so its Treynor ratio is Rm − Rf: the slope of Day 52’s security market line (SML).

Jensen’s alpha: height above the SML. CAPM says a portfolio with beta βp should earn Rf + βp(Rm − Rf). Alpha is what it actually earned minus that.

Why Treynor and alpha can disagree. Factor βp out of alpha: αp = βp × (Treynorp − Treynorm). Alpha is the Treynor gap scaled up by beta (our shortcut, not a formula to memorize). A high-beta fund can rank first on alpha and second on Treynor.

Downside versions. Sortino keeps Sharpe’s shape but uses a minimum acceptable return (MAR) instead of Rf and Day 24’s target downside deviation instead of σ. Calmar divides the annualized return by the maximum drawdown.

In plain words: every measure asks how much extra return you got for each unit of risk. They only disagree about what counts as risk.

Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) / σp

M² = (Rp − Rf) × σm/σp − (Rm − Rf)

Treynor = (Rp − Rf) / βp

αp = Rp − [Rf + βp(Rm − Rf)]

Sortino = (Rp − MAR) / downside deviation

Calmar = annualized return / maximum drawdown

Rp
the portfolio’s return over the period
Rf
the risk-free rate (T-bills)
Rm
the market’s (benchmark’s) return
σp, σm
standard deviation of the portfolio’s and the market’s returns: total risk
βp
the portfolio’s beta: systematic (market) risk
MAR
minimum acceptable return, the investor’s target (often Rf or 0%)
downside deviation
Day 24’s target downside deviation with MAR as the target: square each shortfall below MAR, add them, divide by n − 1 (all n periods count), take the square root
maximum drawdown
the worst peak-to-trough fall in value
Memorize
  • Sharpe ratio: Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) / σp
  • Treynor ratio: Treynor = (Rp − Rf) / βp
  • M-squared (M²): M² = (Rp − Rf) × (σm / σp) − (Rm − Rf); equivalently, the risk-adjusted return Rf + (Rp − Rf) × σm/σp compared with Rm
  • Jensen’s alpha: αp = Rp − [Rf + βp (Rm − Rf)]
  • Sortino ratio: Sortino = (Rp − MAR) / downside deviation
  • Calmar ratio: Calmar = annualized return / maximum drawdown

Worked example

Problem. Fund A returned 14% with σ = 20% and β = 1.2. Fund B returned 10% with σ = 12% and β = 0.7. The market returned 9% with σ = 16%, and Rf = 2%. Find each fund’s Sharpe ratio, Treynor ratio, M² and Jensen’s alpha. Which fund does each measure rank first?

By hand
  1. Excess returns: A 14 − 2 = 12%; B 10 − 2 = 8%; market 9 − 2 = 7%.
  2. Sharpe: A 12 / 20 = 0.60; B 8 / 12 = 0.67; market 7 / 16 = 0.4375.
  3. Treynor: A 12 / 1.2 = 10.00; B 8 / 0.7 = 11.43; market 7 / 1 = 7.00.
  4. M²: A 12 × 16/20 − 7 = 9.60 − 7 = 2.60%; B 8 × 16/12 − 7 = 10.67 − 7 = 3.67%.
  5. Jensen’s alpha: A 14 − (2 + 1.2 × 7) = 14 − 10.4 = 3.60%; B 10 − (2 + 0.7 × 7) = 10 − 6.9 = 3.10%.
  6. Calmar (Alternatives): if A’s worst peak-to-trough fall was 25%, Calmar = 14 / 25 = 0.56.
  7. Verdict: Sharpe, M² and Treynor rank B first. Jensen ranks A first, because alpha is an amount, not a ratio: 1.2 × (10.00 − 7) = 3.60 beats 0.7 × (11.43 − 7) = 3.10. Fund held as the whole portfolio: use Sharpe or M². One piece of a diversified portfolio: Treynor or Jensen.

The inputs are possible (ρ = β × σm/σp = 0.2 × 15/4 = 0.75), but a Sharpe ratio nearly five times the market’s is a number to question before you admire it.

Back to the story

Use the sheet’s own numbers. Its ratios imply a risk-free rate of about 3.975%: 10.85 − 2.75 × 2.50.

The S&P 100 earned 11.21 − 3.975 = 7.24 points of excess return for 14.11 points of volatility: 0.51 per unit. Sentry claimed 6.88 points for only 2.50: 2.75 per unit. That is about 5.4 times the index, for sixteen years, on a slightly lower raw return.

M² turns it into a return. Levered up to the index’s 14.11% risk, Sentry’s record would have earned 3.975% + 6.875% × 14.11/2.50 = 42.78% a year: an M² of 31.57 points over the index. Its worst drawdown on the sheet, −0.64%, gives a return-to-drawdown (Calmar-style) ratio of about 17.

Markopolos argued that numbers like these were not possible for the strategy Madoff described: they were the signature of invented returns. The flag was printed on the sheet itself.

Exam traps
  • Using Treynor or Jensen’s alpha for a portfolio that is not well diversified. Beta ignores unsystematic risk, so a concentrated fund looks better than it is. Judge it with Sharpe or M², which use σ.
  • Forgetting to subtract (Rm − Rf) in M². In the walkthrough, 9 × 15/4 = 33.75 and the risk-adjusted return of 36.75% are steps, not the answer: M² = 36.75 − 10 = 26.75%.
  • Ranking by raw return. Fund A earned 14% to B’s 10%, yet B has the higher Sharpe ratio, Treynor ratio and M².
  • In the Sortino ratio’s downside deviation, dividing by the number of shortfalls. As on Day 24, divide by n − 1, counting every period.

2. Why. A fund holds only six stocks. Why can its Treynor ratio or Jensen’s alpha make it look better than it is?

  • If beta overstated the risk, these measures would make the fund look worse. The problem runs the other way: beta leaves risk out.

  • Right. Beta counts only market risk. A six-stock fund’s own swings have not been diversified away, so σ is the honest measure of what its holder bears.

  • Every measure here uses the same market risk-free rate, Sharpe included. Rf is a T-bill rate, not a property of the fund.

Explanation

Treynor divides by β and alpha compares the return with Rf + β(Rm − Rf). Both assume the fund’s unsystematic risk has been diversified away by the rest of your holdings. For a six-stock fund held on its own, that risk is still there: σ is high while β may be modest, so Treynor and alpha flatter it. Sharpe and M² divide by σ and charge for all of it.

3. Twist: only the bad years count. A fund’s five annual returns: 12%, −4%, 9%, −8%, 15%. Its investor’s minimum acceptable return (MAR) is 0%. What is the fund’s Sortino ratio? To two decimals.

ratio
Hint Numerator: the mean return minus the MAR. Denominator: Day 24’s target downside deviation. Square only the returns below 0%, add the squares, divide by n − 1 = 4 (all five years count), then take the square root.
Solution

Mean = (12 − 4 + 9 − 8 + 15) / 5 = 24 / 5 = 4.8%. Below 0%: −4 and −8. Squares: 16 + 64 = 80; 80 / 4 = 20; √20 = 4.4721%.

Sortino = (4.8 − 0) / 4.4721 = 1.07.

BA II Plus (a square ignores the sign, so type 4 and 8): 4 x² + 8 x² = ÷ 4 = √x STO 1 → 4.4721; 12 − 4 + 9 − 8 + 15 = ÷ 5 = → 4.8000; ÷ RCL 1 = → 1.0733.

TI-84 Plus: 2nd x² (√) ( 4 x² + 8 x² ) ÷ 4 ) STO→ ALPHA D ENTER → 4.4721; ( 12 − 4 + 9 − 8 + 15 ) ÷ 5 ÷ ALPHA D ENTER → 1.0733.

Dividing 80 by the 2 losing years instead gives a downside deviation of 6.3246 and a Sortino of 0.76: wrong. And the mean over the full SD of all five returns, 10.18%, is only 0.47: Sortino does not punish the fund for its 12% and 15% years.

4. Exam style. A portfolio returned 11% with a beta of 1.3. The market returned 9% and the risk-free rate was 3%. The portfolio’s Jensen’s alpha is closest to:

  • Right: CAPM required return = 3 + 1.3 × (9 − 3) = 3 + 7.8 = 10.8%, and α = 11 − 10.8 = 0.20%.

  • Beta was multiplied by the market return instead of the market risk premium: 11 − (3 + 1.3 × 9) = 11 − 14.7.

  • That compares raw returns, 11 − 9, which is alpha only if β = 1. With β = 1.3 the portfolio was expected to beat the market.

Explanation

αp = Rp − [Rf + βp(Rm − Rf)] = 11 − [3 + 1.3 × 6] = 11 − 10.8 = 0.20%.

Check with Treynor: the portfolio’s is 8 / 1.3 = 6.15 against the market’s 6, and 1.3 × (6.15 − 6) = 0.20. Barely above the SML.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: which number on Fairfield Sentry’s sheet should have raised a flag, and why?

Model answer

The Sharpe ratio of 2.75. It measures excess return per unit of total risk, and Sentry claimed 2.75 for sixteen years against 0.51 for the S&P 100, an M² of about 31.6 points; a stock-like return with almost no volatility implies a risk–return trade-off no real strategy offers, so it called for investigation, not admiration.

A complete answer says: (1) Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) / σp, excess return per unit of total risk; (2) 2.75 against 0.51, about 5.4 times the index, sustained for sixteen years; (3) a record that smooth is a reason to investigate, not to invest.
