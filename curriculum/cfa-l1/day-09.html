The 18.63% mortgage

In the week of 9 October 1981, Freddie Mac’s weekly survey put the average US 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 18.63%. That was the highest since the survey began in 1971, and no week since has topped it: the record still stands.

Behind it: the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker had pushed interest rates to around 20% to break double-digit inflation. Banks’ prime rate, the rate they charge their best business borrowers, had reached 21.5%.

Home sales collapsed. Builders mailed chunks of two-by-four lumber to Volcker and to members of Congress in protest. One addressed to Volcker is catalogued in the St. Louis Fed’s FRASER digital library.

So take a $100,000, 30-year loan at 18.63%, paid monthly. Day 5’s annuity formula gives the payment. The harder question is what that payment buys. How much of the first one actually pays off the house? And how many years pass before principal outweighs interest?

The idea

A loan is Day 5’s annuity seen from the borrower’s side. The bank hands you the PV today; you pay back the same amount, A, every month. The payment never changes. What it buys does.

Each payment does two jobs, in a fixed order. First it pays this month’s interest on what you still owe. Whatever is left repays principal: it makes the debt itself smaller.

An everyday picture: a credit card with $1,000 on it, charging 1.5% a month. Pay $20, and $15 of it is interest; only $5 of the debt goes away. You now owe $995.

So at the start, when you owe the most, interest eats most of the payment. As the balance falls, so does the interest, and more of the same payment goes to principal: slowly at first, then faster and faster. The month-by-month table of those splits is the amortization schedule.

And at any moment, what you still owe is simply the PV of the payments you have left.

Why it works

Step 1: the payment, from Day 5. The lender hands you PV today and receives A at the end of each of N periods. A fair loan means those payments, discounted at the loan rate, are worth exactly the cash lent:

PV = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r

Solve for A (multiply both sides by r, divide by the bracket):

A = PV × r / [1 − (1 + r)−N]

Step 2: one period of the loan. At the start of period t you owe Bt−1. Interest is charged on that, and your payment covers it first. Only the rest cuts the debt:

Interestt = r × Bt−1
Principalt = A − Interestt
Bt = Bt−1 − Principalt

Step 3: why principal snowballs. Repaying Principalt cuts next period’s interest by r × Principalt. The payment stays the same, so that saving moves to principal:

Principalt+1 = Principalt + r × Principalt = Principalt × (1 + r)

Principal grows like money in a bank at rate r. At 18.63%: 6.0828 in month 1, then 6.0828 × 1.015525 = 6.1772 in month 2. Start tiny, and it stays tiny for years.

Step 4: the balance is the PV of what is left. Put step 2’s lines together: Bt = Bt−1 × (1 + r) − A.

Now let Vt be the PV of the payments still to come after period t. One period earlier, they were the next payment plus all the rest, discounted one period: Vt−1 = (A + Vt) / (1 + r). Rearranged: Vt = Vt−1 × (1 + r) − A.

Same rule, same start (B0 = V0 = PV, by step 1), so they stay equal every period. After k payments you owe the PV of the N − k payments left.

In one sentence: each payment first pays rent on the money you still owe, and only what is left over buys back a piece of the loan.

A = PV × r / [1 − (1 + r)−N]

Interestt = r × Bt−1; Principalt = A − Interestt

Bk = A × [1 − (1 + r)−(N − k)] / r

A
the level payment, the same every period
PV
the amount borrowed (the balance at t = 0)
r
the loan rate per period, as a decimal (monthly loan: the annual stated rate ÷ 12)
N
the number of payments (monthly loan: years × 12)
Bt−1
the balance owed at the start of period t, right after payment t − 1
Interestt, Principalt
the two parts of payment t: interest first, principal is the rest
k
payments already made; N − k are left
Memorize
  • A = PV × r / [1 − (1 + r)−N]
  • Interestt = r × Balancet−1
  • Principalt = A − Interestt
  • Balancek = PV of the remaining N − k payments at the loan rate
  • Solve the loan in TVM first, then 2ND AMORT: P1, P2, ↓ BAL, ↓ PRN, ↓ INT

Worked example

Problem. A $10,000 loan at 6% is repaid with 3 equal payments, one at the end of each year. Find the payment and build the schedule.

By hand
  1. Payment: A = 10,000 × 0.06 / [1 − 1.06−3] = 600 / 0.1603807 = 3,741.10.
  2. Year 1: interest = 0.06 × 10,000.00 = 600.00. Principal = 3,741.10 − 600.00 = 3,141.10. Balance = 6,858.90.
  3. Year 2: interest = 0.06 × 6,858.90 = 411.53. Principal = 3,741.10 − 411.53 = 3,329.57. Balance = 3,529.33.
  4. Year 3: interest = 0.06 × 3,529.33 = 211.76. Principal = 3,741.10 − 211.76 = 3,529.34, one cent more than the 3,529.33 owed (rounding). So the last payment is trimmed to 3,741.09, and the balance is 0.00.
  5. See the pattern: interest falls (600.00, 411.53, 211.76) while principal grows by 6% a year (3,141.10 × 1.06 = 3,329.57).
  6. Check: the balance after year 1 is the PV of the 2 payments left: 3,741.098 × [1 − 1.06−2] / 0.06 = 3,741.098 × 1.833393 = 6,858.90.

Back to the story

At 18.63% the monthly rate is 18.63 ÷ 12 = 1.5525%. Month 1’s interest is 100,000 × 0.015525 = $1,552.50, out of a $1,558.58 payment. That leaves $6.08 to pay off the house.

A full year of payments, $18,702.99, brought the balance down only to $99,920.44: $79.56 repaid.

Principal grows by just 1.5525% a month from that $6.08 start, so it first outweighs interest in payment 317, 26 years and 5 months in. Over the 30 years the borrower pays about $461,090 of interest on a $100,000 loan.

For more than 26 years, most of every payment was rent on the money, not a piece of the house.

Exam traps
  • The annual rate with monthly payments. I/Y = 18.63 with N = 360 treats 18.63% as a monthly rate: PMT = 18,630.00, a full year’s interest every month. Monthly loan: I/Y = annual rate ÷ 12 and N = years × 12 (P/Y stays 1).
  • P1 to P2 is a range. AMORT, ΣPrn( and ΣInt( add up payments P1 through P2. Interest in payment 12 alone: P1 = P2 = 12 gives 1,551.38. Interest in the first year: P1 = 1, P2 = 12 gives 18,623.43. Read whether the question wants one payment or a span.
  • Principal is not repaid evenly. 100,000 ÷ 360 = 277.78 is not what each payment repays: payment 1 repays $6.08, and principal grows by (1 + r) every period.
  • Balance is not the loan minus the payments. 100,000 − 18,702.99 (a year of payments) = 81,297.01 ignores interest. What you owe is the PV of the payments left: 99,920.44.

2. Why. On question 1’s loan, the first payment of $2,036.36 is $1,800.00 of interest and only $236.36 of principal. Why is the first payment mostly interest?

  • Right. 0.006 × 300,000 = 1,800.00 must be paid first; only the rest repays principal. As the balance falls, interest falls and principal grows.

  • The rate is 0.6% every month; nothing is front-loaded. Each period’s interest is exactly r × that period’s balance, so the big early interest is arithmetic, not a contract term.

  • Equal slices would be 833.33 a month, and payment 1 would be 833.33 + 1,800.00 = 2,633.33, not 2,036.36. Principal starts at 236.36 and grows.

Explanation

Interestt = r × Bt−1, and the payment covers it first. At the start B is the whole loan, so interest is at its peak and principal at its smallest. Each repaid dollar cuts the next interest charge, so principal grows by (1 + r) every month: 236.3646 × 1.006 = 237.78 in month 2.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: where does each loan payment go, and why does that split change over the life of the loan?

Model answer

Each payment first pays interest, the rate times the balance owed at the start of the period, and the rest repays principal. Since the balance keeps falling, the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows, and at any time the balance is just the PV of the payments still to come.

A complete answer says: (1) interest = r × the balance at the start of the period, and it is paid first; (2) the rest of the payment repays principal; (3) the balance falls, so interest falls and principal rises; (4) the balance at any time is the PV of the remaining payments at the loan rate.
