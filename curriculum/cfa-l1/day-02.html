From Day 1. On the BA II Plus you enter N = 10, I/Y = 6, PV = 2,000 (typed positive, no +|−) and PMT = 0, then press CPT FV. What does the screen show?

  • Right. A positive PV is cash you receive today, so the FV that settles it at the end is cash going out: negative. The size is still correct.

  • You get a positive FV only when PV is entered as −2,000: you pay the money out today and receive the FV back at the end.

  • Error 5 comes only when solving for I/Y or N with no sign change among PV, PMT and FV. Solving for FV, PV or PMT never errors.

Why

Out is negative, in is positive. Today it matters more: to solve for a rate, PV and FV must have opposite signs, or you will meet Error 5 for real.

Sixty guilders for Manhattan

On 5 November 1626, Pieter Schaghen, a Dutch official in Amsterdam, wrote to the States General in The Hague. The colonists of New Netherland, he reported, “have purchased the Island Manhattes from the Indians for the value of 60 guilders.”

The letter is the only known contemporary record of the deal, and it does not say what changed hands. In the 1840s, American historians converted the sum at the exchange rate of their day, about 40 cents to the guilder. That made it $24, and “$24” entered the schoolbooks.

Now jump 388 years. In a 2018 paper, the economists Jason Barr, Fred Smith and Sayali Kulkarni estimated that Manhattan’s developable land alone, not counting the buildings on it, was worth about $1.74 trillion in 2014. (An earlier version of their study, widely reported in 2016, had said about $1.4 trillion.)

So was $24 a steal? Not obviously: money left to compound grows too. There is exactly one annual rate at which $24 in 1626 and $1.74 trillion in 2014 are worth the same. What is it, and how would you find it?

The idea

Day 1 ran money forward: a sum today, grown at r for N years, becomes FV. Today runs it backward.

Present value asks: how much would I need today, invested at r, to have FV in N years? That amount is what the future sum is worth now. Finding it is called discounting, and r is the discount rate.

An everyday picture: you want $1,000 for a trip in two years, and the bank pays 5%. You don’t need to deposit $1,000, because less will grow into it. Present value says exactly how much less: $907.03.

Two rules follow, and they never change. A higher rate means you need less today. A longer wait means you need less today.

The equation holds four numbers: PV, FV, r and N. Know any three and you can find the fourth. Day 1 found FV. Today finds PV, the rate r (Manhattan’s question) and the number of periods N.

Why it works

Start from Day 1. FVN = PV × (1 + r)N. Each year multiplies the money by (1 + r).

Go back one year. What grows into $105 in a year at 5%? Times 1.05 it gives 105, so it is 105 ÷ 1.05 = $100. One year back means dividing by (1 + r).

Go back N years. Divide once per year, which is dividing by (1 + r)N. Check: 110.25 ÷ 1.052 = 110.25 ÷ 1.1025 = 100.

So PV = FVN / (1 + r)N: Day 1’s formula divided through by (1 + r)N. In plain words: the amount to put in the bank today to end up with the future sum.

The discount factor. DF = 1 / (1 + r)N is what $1 due in N years is worth today, so PV = FV × DF. At 5% over 6 years DF = 0.7462: a dollar promised in 6 years is worth about 75 cents now.

Solve for the rate. Divide Day 1’s formula by PV: FV/PV = (1 + r)N, the number of times the money multiplied. Undo the power N with the Nth root (raise to 1/N): (FV/PV)1/N = 1 + r. Subtract 1. In plain words: how fast the money must grow each year to turn PV into FV. Only the ratio FV/PV matters, not the units.

Solve for N. Now the unknown is the exponent. The LN key brings exponents down: ln(aN) = N × ln(a). So ln(FV/PV) = N × ln(1 + r); divide by ln(1 + r).

The Rule of 72. To double, FV/PV = 2, and ln 2 = 0.6931. For small r, ln(1 + r) ≈ r, so N ≈ 69.3 / (rate in percent). At ordinary rates the exact constant creeps up (71.71 at 7%, 72.05 at 8%), and 72 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12.

  • PV = FVN / (1 + r)N
  • DF = 1 / (1 + r)N
  • r = (FVN / PV)1/N − 1
  • N = ln(FVN / PV) / ln(1 + r)

Worked example

Problem. A savings bond pays $5,000 in 6 years. (a) At 5% a year, what is it worth today? (b) If it sells for $3,500, what annual return does it offer? (c) At 5%, how many years does $3,500 take to grow to $5,000?

By hand
  1. Growth factor: 1.056 = 1.340096.
  2. Discount factor: DF = 1 ÷ 1.340096 = 0.746215.
  3. (a) PV = 5,000 × 0.746215 = $3,731.08.
  4. (b) Growth needed: 5,000 ÷ 3,500 = 1.428571.
  5. Spread it over 6 years: 1.4285711/6 = 1.061248, so r = 0.061248 = 6.12%.
  6. (c) N = ln(1.428571) ÷ ln(1.05) = 0.356675 ÷ 0.048790 = 7.31 years.
  7. Sense check: $3,500 is below the 5% value of $3,731.08, so the buyer earns more than 5% (6.12%). At only 5%, the money needs longer than 6 years (7.31).

Back to the story

The break-even rate is 6.66% a year. If $24 could have been invested at more than that for 388 years, it would have outgrown the island; at less, the buyers got the bargain.

Now see how hard the rate swings the value. Discount $1.74 trillion back 388 years: at 6% it was worth $264.16 in 1626, at 7% $6.91, at 8% about 19 cents. Each extra point divides the answer by more than 35.

The rate itself barely moves. The authors’ range of $1.54–1.95 trillion gives 6.62% to 6.69%, and the older $1.4 trillion gives 6.60%. A 388th root flattens any error in the price.

The inputs are rough (an 1840s exchange rate set against 2014 dollars), but the lesson is exact: over long horizons, the rate decides everything.

Exam traps
  • PV and FV entered with the same sign when solving for I/Y or N: the BA II Plus shows Error 5, the TI-84 ERR:NO SIGN CHNG. One of them must be negative.
  • Thinking a higher discount rate raises the present value. It lowers it, and the longer the wait, the harder it falls.
  • Reading I/Y = 6.6573 as a decimal (it is 6.6573%), or as an annual rate when the periods are months or quarters: I/Y is the rate per period, whatever the period is.
  • Discounting with simple interest, FV / (1 + r × N): it ignores interest on interest and overstates the PV.

2. Why. Two promises of $1,000 each: one paid in 5 years, one in 30 years. The discount rate rises from 4% to 5%. What happens to their present values?

  • That treats the change as one step. But the rate is applied once per year: the 5-year PV divides by (1 + r) five times, the 30-year PV thirty times.

  • Right. 5-year: 821.93 → 783.53 (−4.67%). 30-year: 308.32 → 231.38 (−24.96%). The extra point is compounded N times inside (1 + r)N.

  • This mixes up size and sensitivity. The 5-year PV is bigger, but it has only five periods for the higher rate to compound over, so it moves less.

Explanation

PV = 1,000 / (1 + r)N. Going from 4% to 5% multiplies each year’s divisor by 1.05/1.04 = 1.009615. The new PV is the old one times (1.04/1.05)N: 0.9533 for N = 5, but 0.7504 for N = 30. The longer the wait, the more a rate change hits the present value.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why is present value compounding run backwards, and why does a higher rate or a longer wait make it smaller?

Model answer

Compounding multiplies by (1 + r) each period, so going back divides by it: PV = FV / (1 + r)N is just FV = PV × (1 + r)N rearranged. A higher r or a longer N makes (1 + r)N bigger, so less money today is enough to grow into the same future sum.

A complete answer says: (1) PV = FV/(1 + r)N is the FV formula rearranged (divide instead of multiply); (2) the discount factor 1/(1 + r)N is what $1 due at N is worth now; (3) a larger r or N makes the denominator bigger, so the PV falls.
