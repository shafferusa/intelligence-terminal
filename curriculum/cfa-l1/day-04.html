Jacob Bernoulli and the interest that never runs away

In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli, a mathematician in Basel, Switzerland, asked a lender’s question.

Lend 1 at 100% a year. Credit the interest once, at the end of the year, and the debt doubles to 2.

Now credit half the interest, 50%, after six months, and let it earn interest too. The debt is multiplied by 1.5 twice: 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25. Credit 25% every three months and it reaches 2.4414. Crediting more often always earns more.

So keep going. Credit it monthly and the debt reaches 2.6130; daily, 2.7146. Every hour, every instant: the debt keeps growing. Yet Bernoulli showed it never runs away: it stays between 2 and 3.

Leonhard Euler later identified the limit as the number now called e, and in 1748 published its value to well over a dozen decimal places: 2.71828…

Which leaves a puzzle. If crediting interest more often always earns more, why doesn’t crediting it every instant earn an infinite amount?

The idea

A quoted rate has two parts: a number, and how often it is credited. “12% a year, compounded monthly” is a stated annual rate of 12%, paid as 1% a month. CFA calls the 1% the periodic rate.

The frequency matters. January’s interest joins the balance, so February’s 1% is paid on slightly more money, and so on all year. By December the money is up 12.68%, not 12%. That true one-year growth is the effective annual rate, or EAR.

Same stated rate, more frequent credits, higher EAR: 12% is 12.36% compounded semiannually, 12.68% monthly and 12.75% continuously, which means credited every instant.

So rates are compared only as EARs. A deposit at 8% compounded quarterly (EAR 8.24%) pays more than one at 8.2% compounded once a year.

And the climb has a ceiling. However often you compound, the EAR never passes ers − 1, its value when compounding is continuous. That ceiling is Bernoulli’s answer.

Why it works

Slice the year. From Day 1, each period multiplies money by (1 + that period’s rate). Compounded m times a year, a stated rate rs pays rs/m a period (12% monthly is 1% a month), so a year multiplies money by (1 + rs/m)m: 1.0112 = 1.126825.

Name it. The EAR is the once-a-year rate with the same growth: 1 + EAR = (1 + rs/m)m, so EAR = (1 + rs/m)m − 1, here 12.6825%. N years hold m × N periods: FVN = PV × (1 + rs/m)m×N.

Where the extra comes from. At 12% semiannually, 100 is 106 by midyear, and the second half earns 6% of 106 = 6.36, not 6: the extra 0.36 is interest on interest.

Why it stops. Split Bernoulli’s interest (1 at 100%) into generations: the 1 earns exactly 1 however you slice the year, that interest earns more, and so on. Each generation starts at zero and builds up, so it averages at most half its final size, and at 100% the next one it earns is at most half that. So the generations are at most 1, 1/2, 1/4…, and the debt stays below 1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + … = 3. With infinitely thin slices it is exactly 1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/6 + 1/24 + … = 2.71828…, the number e.

Any rate. At rs the generations are rs, rs2/2, rs3/6…, and 1 + rs + rs2/2 + … = ers. At 8%: 1 + 0.08 + 0.0032 + 0.0000853 + … = 1.0832871 = e0.08. So continuously, EAR = ers − 1 and FVN = PV × ers×N.

Going back. Undo the steps: the m-th root of (1 + EAR), minus 1, times m gives rs. For rcc, LN undoes eˣ: rcc = ln(1 + EAR).

In plain words: the EAR is how much your money really grows in a year. Crediting interest more often raises it, but never past ers − 1.

EAR = (1 + rs/m)m − 1

FVN = PV × (1 + rs/m)m×N

Continuous: EAR = ers − 1 and FVN = PV × ers×N

Back: rs = m × [(1 + EAR)1/m − 1] and rcc = ln(1 + EAR)

Memorize
  • EAR = (1 + rs/m)m − 1
  • FVN = PV × (1 + rs/m)m × N
  • FVN = PV × ers × N
  • EAR = ers − 1
  • rcc = ln(1 + EAR)
  • rs = m × [(1 + EAR)1/m − 1]

Worked example

Problem. A bank quotes 8% stated annual, compounded quarterly. (a) What is the EAR? (b) What does $10,000 grow to in 3 years? (c) If the same 8% were compounded continuously, what would the EAR and the 3-year value be?

By hand
  1. Periodic rate: 8% ÷ 4 = 2% a quarter.
  2. (a) A year is 4 quarters: 1.024 = 1.08243216, so EAR = 8.2432%.
  3. (b) Three years are 4 × 3 = 12 quarters: 1.0212 = 1.268242.
  4. FV = 10,000 × 1.268242 = $12,682.42.
  5. Same answer from the EAR: 10,000 × 1.082432163 = 12,682.42. (Using 1.083 instead gives 12,597.12: too little.)
  6. (c) Continuous: e0.08 = 1.083287, so EAR = 8.3287%.
  7. FV = 10,000 × e0.08 × 3 = 10,000 × e0.24 = 10,000 × 1.271249 = $12,712.49, $30.07 more than quarterly.

Back to the story

Bernoulli’s loan is a stated rate of 100%. As an EAR, (1 + 1/m)m − 1:

  • once a year: 100%
  • twice a year: 125%
  • quarterly: 144.14%
  • monthly: 161.30%
  • daily: 171.46%
  • continuously: e − 1 = 171.83%

Each step adds less. Monthly to daily adds 10.15 points; daily to every instant only 0.37. The extra is interest on interest, and each generation of it is at most half the one before, so the debt can never reach 3.

It tops out at 2.71828 times the loan: the limit Bernoulli pinned between 2 and 3, and why no amount of slicing triples your money. At real-world rates the ceiling is just as firm: 8% compounded as often as you like never beats an EAR of 8.33%.

Exam traps
  • Comparing stated rates that compound at different frequencies. 8% compounded quarterly beats 8.2% compounded annually; compare the EARs (8.24% against 8.20%).
  • Using years and the stated rate when the compounding is monthly. 12% monthly for 3 years is 36 periods at 1%, not 3 periods at 12% (and not 3 periods at 1%).
  • Dividing the rate yourself and leaving P/Y and C/Y at 12: the calculator divides by 12 a second time. With N = 36 and I/Y = 1, $1,000 then grows to only $1,030.44 instead of $1,430.77. Keep P/Y = C/Y = 1 and convert N and the rate yourself.
  • Thinking continuous compounding makes the rate infinite. It gives ers − 1: 8.33% for 8%, 12.75% for 12%.

2. Why. A bank compounds a stated 10% a year continuously, crediting interest every instant. Why is the effective annual rate not infinite?

  • The limit is in the math, not the bank. (1 + 0.10/m)m never passes e0.10 = 1.1052, however large m gets: daily gives 10.5156%, every instant 10.5171%.

  • Interest still earns interest, as with any compounding, so the EAR is above 10%: e0.10 − 1 = 10.52%.

  • Right. More slices always add something, but less each time, and the additions sum to a finite amount.

Explanation

Count the generations at 10%: 0.10 on the principal, then 0.102/2 = 0.0050 of interest on interest, then 0.103/6 = 0.000167, and so on. They shrink fast: 0.10 + 0.0050 + 0.000167 = 0.105167 already. Monthly gives 10.4713%, daily 10.5156%, continuously e0.10 − 1 = 10.5171%. The EAR creeps up to 10.52% and stops there.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why must you compare effective annual rates, not stated rates, when two quotes compound at different frequencies?

Model answer

A stated rate leaves out how often interest is credited, and interest credited more often starts earning interest sooner, so the same stated rate is worth more the more often it compounds. EAR = (1 + rs/m)m − 1 turns every quote into what it actually earns in one year, so the comparison is like for like.

A complete answer says: (1) a stated rate hides the compounding frequency; (2) the EAR puts every rate on one annual basis; (3) for the same stated rate, more frequent compounding gives a higher EAR.
