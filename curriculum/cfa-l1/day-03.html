Türkiye cut its rate to 14% in a year prices rose 36%. How much poorer is a saver at 14%, exactly?

__DATELINE__ · 20 min

Interest and inflation multiply, they don’t subtract: the exact real return is (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, and every required rate is a real risk-free rate plus a premium for each thing the lender bears.

Türkiye’s 14% that lost money

On 16 December 2021, the Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye cut its policy rate, the one-week repo rate, from 15% to 14%. It was the bank’s fourth cut since September: 500 basis points in all, or 5 percentage points (a basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point).

Eighteen days later, on 3 January 2022, the statistics office, TurkStat, reported that consumer prices had risen 36.08% in the year to December, the fastest pace since 2002. Over 2021 the lira lost about 44% of its value against the dollar, its worst year in two decades.

Now picture a saver who earns the policy rate, 14%, for a year while prices rise 36.08%. His balance grows by 14%. Is he richer or poorer at the end of the year, and by how much?

The quick answer is 14% − 36.08% = −22.08%. It looks obvious, and it is off by more than five percentage points. What is the true figure, and why does subtraction miss it?

The idea

An interest rate is the price of waiting. The curriculum reads one rate three ways: as a required rate of return (the least an investor will accept), as a discount rate (Day 2’s r, which brings future money back to today) and as an opportunity cost (what you give up by spending the money instead).

The price is built in layers. The real risk-free rate pays for waiting alone, with no inflation and no risk. The inflation premium pays for the money buying less when it comes back. Together they make the nominal risk-free rate.

A riskier loan adds three more: a default risk premium (the borrower may not pay in full and on time), a liquidity premium (you may have to sell in a hurry, below fair value) and a maturity premium (a longer bond’s price swings more when rates move).

Every return also comes in pairs. Nominal or real: measured in money, or in what the money buys. Pre-tax or after-tax. Gross or net: before or after the manager’s management, custody and administrative fees (trading costs are already out of the gross figure). Türkiye’s puzzle is the first pair.

Why it works

Build it from Day 1, and count in shopping baskets instead of money.

  1. Money grows by a factor. From Day 1, a year at the nominal rate r turns 1 unit of money into (1 + r).
  2. So do prices. A basket that costs 1 today costs (1 + π) in a year; π (pi) is the inflation rate.
  3. Count baskets. Start with enough money for 1 basket. A year later you hold (1 + r) and a basket costs (1 + π), so you can buy (1 + r) ÷ (1 + π) baskets.
  4. That is the real growth factor. You began with 1 basket, so the real return is (1 + r) / (1 + π) − 1.
  5. Multiply by (1 + π). (1 + r) = (1 + real) × (1 + π): two growth factors in a row, like two years on Day 1 (1.05 × 1.05 = 1.1025, not 1.10).
  6. Open the brackets. 1 + r = 1 + real + π + real × π, so r = real + π + (real × π).
  7. Drop the small piece. At a 2% real rate and 3% inflation, the cross term is 0.02 × 0.03 = 0.0006, six hundredths of a percent. Ignore it and nominal ≈ real + inflation, which is why a required rate can be stacked as a sum. At 36% inflation the cross term is not small.
  8. Another view. (1 + r) / (1 + π) − 1 = (r − π) / (1 + π). The shortcut’s r − π is counted in end-of-year money; dividing by (1 + π) restates it in today’s prices, much like Day 2’s discounting.
  9. Tax. A tax at rate t takes t of the interest, so you keep r × (1 − t). It is charged on the nominal interest: take the tax off first, then divide by (1 + π).

In plain words: your money grows, prices grow, and what you really gain is how much more your money can buy. That is one growth divided by the other.

Memorize
  • r = real risk-free rate + inflation premium + default risk premium + liquidity premium + maturity premium
  • (1 + nominal rate) = (1 + real rate) × (1 + inflation rate)
  • real = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1
  • nominal rf ≈ real rf + expected inflation
  • after-tax return = pre-tax return × (1 − tax rate)
  • Gross return: before management, custody and other administrative fees (trading costs already deducted); net return: after those fees

Worked example

Problem. A 5-year corporate bond: real risk-free rate 1.5%, inflation premium 2.5%, default risk premium 1.2%, liquidity premium 0.3%, maturity premium 0.5%. (a) What is its required return? (b) If it yields 6% and inflation is 2.5%, what is the exact real return? (c) After a 30% tax on the interest, what are the after-tax nominal and after-tax real returns?

By hand
  1. (a) Stack the layers: 1.5% + 2.5% + 1.2% + 0.3% + 0.5% = 6.0%. The first two, 4.0%, are the nominal risk-free rate.
  2. (b) Real growth factor: 1.06 ÷ 1.025 = 1.034146.
  3. Real return = 1.034146 − 1 = 0.034146 = 3.41%.
  4. The shortcut, 6% − 2.5% = 3.50%, is only 0.09 points off, because inflation is low.
  5. (c) After-tax nominal = 6% × (1 − 0.30) = 6% × 0.70 = 4.20%.
  6. After-tax real = 1.042 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 1.016585 − 1 = 1.66%.
  7. Sense check: tax, then inflation, shrink a 6% yield to a 1.66% real gain.

Back to the story

The saver got poorer, by 16.23%, not 22.08%. Follow 10,000 lira. At 14% it becomes 11,400 lira. Shopping that cost 10,000 lira now costs 13,608. So 11,400 ÷ 13,608 = 0.8377: his money buys about 16% less than a year earlier.

The shortcut overstates the loss by 5.85 points, and that gap is exactly the cross term: −0.1623 × 0.3608 = −0.0585. When rates are a few percent you can drop it. At 36% inflation you can’t.

What rate would have protected him? Just to stand still in real terms, 36.08%. To earn a 2% real return, 1.02 × 1.3608 − 1 = 38.80%, not the shortcut’s 38.08%. That is for a default-free lender; a riskier loan stacks default, liquidity and maturity premiums on top. And tax deepens the hole: with a hypothetical 15% tax on the interest, the real return falls to −17.77%.

Exam traps
  • Using the subtraction shortcut (nominal − inflation) when the question asks for the exact real rate. Use (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1, and expect the shortcut’s answer among the wrong options.
  • Adding a default risk premium to a default-free government bill. A short Treasury bill’s rate is the nominal risk-free rate: real risk-free rate plus inflation premium.
  • Taxing the real return instead of the nominal return. Tax is levied on nominal income: take the tax off first, then adjust for inflation.
  • Typing percents inside a formula: (1 + 14) ÷ (1 + 36.08) is nonsense. Inside brackets 14% is 0.14; only the TVM keys (I/Y) want 14.

2. Why. In the story, the exact real return, 1.14 ÷ 1.3608 − 1 = −16.23%, is a smaller loss than the shortcut, 14% − 36.08% = −22.08%. Why?

  • Rounding moves the last decimal, not 5.85 points. The two formulas differ by a genuine term, real × inflation, which is big at 36% inflation.

  • Neither figure includes tax. Taxing the interest makes the loss bigger, not smaller: −17.77% at a 15% tax.

  • Right. Real = (r − π) / (1 + π) = −0.2208 ÷ 1.3608 = −0.1623.

Explanation

(1 + r) / (1 + π) − 1 = (r − π) / (1 + π): the shortcut’s answer divided by one year’s price growth, 1 + π. Said the other way, r − π = real + real × π, and the cross term −0.1623 × 0.3608 = −0.0585 is the whole 5.85-point gap. When inflation is low, 1 + π is close to 1 and the two answers nearly agree.

4. Exam style. An analyst estimates the real risk-free rate at 1.0% and the inflation premium at 2.7%. A 10-year corporate bond carries a default risk premium of 1.6%, a liquidity premium of 0.4% and a maturity premium of 0.9%. The required rate of return on a 3-month Treasury bill is closest to:

  • Right. A T-bill has no default risk, is highly liquid and matures in months, so its rate is the nominal risk-free rate: 1.0% + 2.7%.

  • Adds the bond’s 1.6% default risk premium to a default-free government bill.

  • Adds every premium. That is the corporate bond’s required return, not the bill’s.

Explanation

Each premium pays for a risk the lender bears. Match the premiums to the instrument: the T-bill bears only the wait and inflation (1.0 + 2.7 = 3.7%); the 10-year corporate bond bears all five (1.0 + 2.7 + 1.6 + 0.4 + 0.9 = 6.6%).

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why is (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation) a product and not a sum?

Model answer

Inflation shrinks the buying power of everything you get back, the interest as well as the principal, so real growth and price growth compound on each other: money grows by (1 + real) × (1 + inflation). Adding the rates drops the cross term real × inflation, which is tiny at low rates but large when inflation is high.

A complete answer says: (1) inflation erodes the principal and the interest alike; (2) real growth and price growth are growth factors that multiply, like two years of compounding; (3) nominal ≈ real + inflation works only when the rates are small.
