Sally Clark and the 1-in-73-million figure

On 9 November 1999, at Chester Crown Court in England, the solicitor Sally Clark was convicted by a 10–2 majority of murdering her two baby sons. Both had died suddenly in infancy.

The paediatrician Sir Roy Meadow had told the jury that the chance of two cot deaths (sudden infant death syndrome, SIDS) in a family like hers was about 1 in 73 million. He had taken 1 in 8,543 for one such death and squared it: 8,543 × 8,543 = 72,982,849.

A number that small sounds like proof. If an innocent family had a 1-in-73-million chance of this, the innocent explanation looks dead.

Her first appeal was dismissed in October 2000. Statisticians objected. In October 2001 the Royal Statistical Society said publicly that the figure had “no statistical basis”. In January 2002 it wrote to the Lord Chancellor that “the calculation leading to 1 in 73 million is false”.

A second appeal quashed her convictions in January 2003. She was freed on 29 January, after about three years in prison.

So what was wrong with the number? What question should the jury have been asking, and how should the evidence of two deaths change a probability?

The idea

Two questions sound alike and are not. “If this is true, how likely is the evidence?” is P(Information | Event). “Given the evidence, how likely is it true?” is P(Event | Information). Decisions need the second. Data usually hands you the first.

A smoke alarm makes it obvious. It sounds at almost every kitchen fire, so P(alarm | fire) is close to 1. Yet when it sounds, it is usually burnt toast: P(fire | alarm) is small, because toast burns far more often than kitchens do.

Bayes’ formula turns one into the other. You start with a prior: your probability before the news. The information arrives. The posterior is your probability after it.

How far it moves depends on one comparison: how much more likely the information is when the event is true than when it isn’t. And the rarer the event to begin with, the more false alarms there are to wade through.

Why it works

Count it first. Take 1,000 firms. The prior says 5% will default within a year: 50 firms. The other 950 won’t.

Send the warnings. A warning goes to 70% of the 50 defaulters: 35 firms. It also goes to 10% of the 950 healthy ones: 95 firms. That makes 130 warned firms.

Look only at the warned. A warning arrived, so your firm is one of those 130. Only 35 of them default: 35 / 130 = 26.9%. That is all Bayes does: the answer is a share of the warned, not of the defaulters.

Swap counts for probabilities (divide each count by 1,000; W = warning, D = default). The 35 is a joint probability, Day 26’s multiplication rule: P(W and D) = P(W | D) × P(D) = 0.70 × 0.05. The 130 is Day 26’s total probability rule, one term per branch: P(W) = P(W | D)P(D) + P(W | not D)P(not D).

Divide. P(D | W) = P(W and D) / P(W) = P(W | D) × P(D) / P(W).

The same in one line. The joint probability can be written either way round: P(E | I) × P(I) = P(E and I) = P(I | E) × P(E). Divide both sides by P(I), and Bayes’ formula falls out.

The CFA layout groups it as [P(I | E) / P(I)] × P(E). Call the bracket the update factor (our label, not an exam term): how much more often the information shows up when the event is true than it does overall. Above 1, the probability rises; below 1, it falls; exactly 1, the information tells you nothing.

In plain words: out of all the ways you could see this clue, what share come from the thing you are asking about?

P(E | I) = [P(I | E) / P(I)] × P(E)

P(I) = P(I | E)P(E) + P(I | not E)P(not E)

E
the event (a default, an earnings beat, guilt)
I
the information that arrives (a warning, a signal, the evidence)
P(E)

P(E | I)

P(I | E)

P(I)

Memorize
  • Bayes’ formula: P(Event | Information) = [P(Information | Event) / P(Information)] × P(Event)
  • Unconditional probability of the information: P(I) = P(I | E)P(E) + P(I | not E)P(not E)
  • Prior and posterior: prior = P(Event) before the information; posterior = P(Event | Information) after it
  • The inverted conditional (prosecutor’s fallacy): P(Information | Event) is not P(Event | Information); Bayes converts one into the other using the prior and P(Information)

Worked example

Problem. An analyst puts a 5% prior probability on a company defaulting within a year. A credit-watch warning is issued before 70% of defaults, but also for 10% of companies that do not default. The warning is issued. What is the probability of default now, and what would it have been with no warning?

By hand
  1. Draw the tree. First split: default (0.05) or not (0.95). Second split: warning or not. Four ends: D and W = 0.70 × 0.05 = 0.035; D and no W = 0.30 × 0.05 = 0.015; no D and W = 0.10 × 0.95 = 0.095; no D and no W = 0.90 × 0.95 = 0.855. They add to 1.
  2. Joint: P(W and D) = P(W | D) × P(D) = 0.70 × 0.05 = 0.035.
  3. Total probability: P(W) = 0.70 × 0.05 + 0.10 × 0.95 = 0.035 + 0.095 = 0.13.
  4. Bayes: P(D | W) = (0.70 / 0.13) × 0.05 = 5.3846 × 0.05 = 0.035 / 0.13 = 26.92%.
  5. No warning: P(no W) = 1 − 0.13 = 0.87; P(D | no W) = (0.30 × 0.05) / 0.87 = 0.015 / 0.87 = 1.72%.
  6. Read it: the warning lifts the default probability from 5% to about 27%, a big jump, yet about 73% of warned companies still do not default.

Back to the story

Error one: multiplying as if independent. Squaring treats the two deaths as unconnected, but a family that has lost one baby to SIDS is more likely to lose another: the mathematician Ray Hill later put that dependency factor at about 5 to 10.

Error two: the inverted conditional. 1 in 73 million is P(two deaths | innocent). The jury needed P(innocent | two deaths). Bayes divides by P(two deaths), every way two deaths can happen, and the double murder of babies is also extraordinarily rare.

Hill estimated double SIDS at roughly 4.5 to 9 times as likely as double murder. Weigh just those two: at 9 to 1, P(guilty | two deaths) = 1/(1 + 9) = 10%; at 4.5 to 1, 1/(1 + 4.5) = 18%. The opposite of the impression the jury was given.

Statistics alone cannot settle guilt, which rests on the medical and other evidence. The point: the figure the jury heard answered the wrong question.

Exam traps
  • Confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A), the prosecutor’s fallacy. P(signal | beat) = 80%, but P(beat | signal) = 64%; P(warning | default) = 70%, but P(default | warning) = 26.92%.
  • Leaving a branch out of the denominator P(Information). It adds every way the information can arise: drop the “no beat” branch and you divide 0.32 by 0.32 and call the beat certain.
  • Multiplying probabilities as if events were independent when they are not. P(A and B) = P(B | A) × P(A); P(A) × P(B) works only when A tells you nothing about B. Squaring 1 in 8,543 was this mistake.
  • Taking P(I | not E) as 1 − P(I | E). 1 − 0.80 = 0.20 is P(no signal | beat), another branch. Use the false-alarm rate given, 0.30; the shortcut turns 64% into 72.73%.

2. Why. An expert tells a jury: “The chance of this evidence if the defendant is innocent is 1 in a million, so the chance he is innocent is 1 in a million.” What is wrong with the reasoning?

  • Right. 1 in a million says how surprising the evidence would be if he were innocent. Whether innocence is likely depends on how surprising the evidence is under guilt too, and on how rare guilt is to begin with.

  • Bayes says P(innocent | evidence) = P(evidence | innocent) × P(innocent) / P(evidence), so they are equal only if P(innocent) happens to equal P(evidence). Today’s walkthrough: P(signal | beat) = 80%, P(beat | signal) = 64%.

  • Squaring repeats the independence mistake from the Clark case, and the squared figure would still answer the wrong question.

Explanation

P(innocent | evidence) = P(evidence | innocent) × P(innocent) / P(evidence), and P(evidence) adds the guilty branch to the innocent one. If guilt is itself very rare, the guilty branch can be tiny too, and innocence can stay the likelier explanation. The expert reported the likelihood and called it the posterior.

4. Exam style. A credit screen flags 90% of firms that later default and 5% of firms that do not. 2% of firms default. The probability that a flagged firm defaults is closest to:

  • That is P(flagged | default), the conditional the wrong way round: the prosecutor’s fallacy.

  • Right: 0.90 × 0.02 = 0.018, divided by P(flagged) = 0.018 + 0.05 × 0.98 = 0.018 + 0.049 = 0.067.

  • That is 0.90 / (0.90 + 0.05): the 2% prior ignored, as if defaulters and survivors were equally common.

Explanation

Count 1,000 firms: 20 default, and 18 of them are flagged. 980 survive, and 49 of them are flagged. Of the 67 flagged firms, 18 default: 18 / 67 = 26.87%.

Formula: P(D | F) = [P(F | D) / P(F)] × P(D) = (0.90 / 0.067) × 0.02 = 26.87%. A screen that catches 90% of defaults still flags mostly survivors, because survivors are 49 times as common.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences, using the Sally Clark case or the earnings signal: what does Bayes’ formula do, and why is P(evidence | innocent) not the chance of innocence?

Model answer

Bayes’ formula updates a prior on new information: the posterior is P(information | event) × prior ÷ P(information), where P(information) adds up every way the information can arise. “1 in 73 million” was P(two deaths | innocent), not P(innocent | two deaths); double murder is also extremely rare, so on the deaths alone the innocent explanation stayed the likelier one.

A complete answer says: (1) posterior = P(I | E) × P(E) / P(I); (2) P(I) adds every branch, by the total probability rule; (3) P(A | B) is not P(B | A); (4) a rare event can stay fairly unlikely even after a fairly reliable signal, as the warned firm still defaults only about 27% of the time.
