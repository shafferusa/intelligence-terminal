From Day 48. A regression on 32 monthly observations has SST = 0.0800 and SSE = 0.0300. Its R² and standard error of estimate (SEE) are:

  • 0.375 = SSE/SST is the share the line does not explain. R² = SSR/SST = 1 − 0.375.

  • R² is right, but 0.0311 = √(0.03/31) divides by n − 1. A regression estimates two parameters, so divide by n − 2 = 30.

  • Right: SSR = 0.0800 − 0.0300 = 0.0500, R² = 0.05/0.08 = 0.625, and SEE = √(0.03/30) = √0.001 = 0.0316.

Why

R² = SSR/SST = (SST − SSE)/SST = 0.625. MSE = SSE/(n − 2) = 0.03/30 = 0.001, and SEE = √MSE = 0.0316.

8.625 cents on the dollar

In the second week of September 2008, Lehman Brothers was still rated A by Standard & Poor’s and A2 by Moody’s. Both agencies had just put it under review: S&P on 9 September, Moody’s on 10 September.

Early on Monday 15 September, Lehman filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

Lehman’s debt was also the reference for credit default swaps (CDS), contracts that work like insurance. The protection buyer pays a yearly premium. If the issuer defaults, the seller pays face value minus what the bonds are still worth. So someone had to say what Lehman’s bonds were worth.

On Friday 10 October 2008, an auction run by Creditex and Markit under an ISDA protocol did it. At 2 p.m. it set the final price: 8.625 per 100 of face value. Sellers of protection paid buyers 91.375 for every 100 they had insured.

Weeks earlier, those bonds had been rated A. What does a default actually cost a bondholder? And how big a yield spread should have paid for that risk in advance?

The idea

Lending to a company is a bet that it keeps paying. Each year there is some chance it defaults, and if it does you lose part of what you lent. Two numbers describe that risk.

Probability of default (POD): the chance it happens, usually over one year.

Loss given default (LGD): how much you lose if it does. Creditors rarely lose everything; they recover part of their claim, as Lehman’s holders recovered 8.625 per 100. LGD is the part not recovered.

Multiply them and you get the expected loss: what you lose on average, per year.

A corporate bond pays a credit spread, extra yield over a government bond of the same maturity (Day 38). Think of it as a yearly insurance premium paid to you for carrying that expected loss: spread ≈ POD × LGD.

The spread is not fixed. If the market decides the borrower got riskier, the spread widens and the price falls, before anyone defaults.

Where the inputs come from. POD from the borrower’s finances: coverage (EBITDA/interest, higher is safer) and leverage (debt/EBITDA and debt/capital, higher is riskier; FFO/debt, higher is safer). LGD from the bond’s place in the queue: secured debt is paid first, then senior unsecured, then subordinated, so recovery falls down the ladder. And ratings drift: a downgrade (credit migration) widens the spread.

Why it works

1. Start with Day 26’s expected value. Over one year, two things can happen. The bond defaults (chance POD) and you lose LGD. Or it doesn’t (chance 1 − POD) and you lose 0.

2. Weight each loss by its chance. E(loss) = POD × LGD + (1 − POD) × 0 = POD × LGD. That is the expected loss.

3. LGD from the recovery. Recover a fraction RR of the exposure and you lose the rest: LGD = exposure × (1 − RR). Lehman: 100 × (1 − 0.08625) = 91.375.

4. From expected loss to spread. Lend 100 to each of 100 such borrowers, with POD 2% and RR 40%. In a typical year about 2 default, and each costs 60: 120 lost out of 10,000, or 1.2%. To end the year level with a Treasury holder, each bond must pay 1.2% extra. So spread ≈ 2% × 60% = POD × LGD.

5. Why only “≈”. It ignores timing (recoveries come later) and the extra investors want for uncertainty and illiquidity. Real spreads usually exceed the expected loss; POD × LGD is the core.

6. From spread to price. A corporate bond’s yield = benchmark yield + spread. Move the spread by ΔSpread with the benchmark still, and the yield moves by exactly ΔSpread. So Day 41’s estimate applies, with ΔSpread in place of ΔYield.

7. Line, then bend. Duration draws the straight line: −ModDur × ΔSpread. Convexity bends it toward the true curve: ½ × Convexity × (ΔSpread)². The square is positive whichever way the spread moves, so for an option-free bond the correction always adds: losses a little smaller, gains a little bigger. (Day 54’s callables are the exception.)

In plain words: expected loss is the chance of losing times how much you would lose. The spread is the yearly fee that pays for it. When the market raises the fee, the bond’s price falls until its yield pays the new fee.

EL = POD × LGD

LGD = Exposure × (1 − RR)

Spread ≈ POD × LGD (per year, LGD as a share of exposure)

%ΔPV ≈ −ModDur × ΔSpread + ½ × Convexity × (ΔSpread)²

expected loss over the period (usually a year), in currency or as a % of the exposure

probability of default over the period, as a decimal
LGD

the amount owed when default happens (exposure at default); here the face value

recovery rate: the share of the exposure you get back, as a decimal
Spread

the change in the spread, as a decimal: 300 bp = 0.03, a narrowing is negative
Memorize
  • Expected loss: EL = probability of default × loss given default
  • Loss given default: LGD = exposure × (1 − recovery rate)
  • Credit spread approximation: Spread ≈ POD × LGD (as % of exposure per year)
  • Price change from a spread change: %ΔPV ≈ −ModDur × ΔSpread + ½ × Convexity × (ΔSpread)²
  • Leverage ratios used by credit analysts: Debt/EBITDA, FFO/debt, debt/capital; coverage: EBITDA/interest

Worked example

Problem. You hold $10 million of a senior unsecured bond. The annual probability of default is 2% and the expected recovery is 40%. Find the loss given default, the expected loss and the approximate spread. Then the spread narrows by 50 bp: estimate the price change for ModDur 4.2 and convexity 22.

By hand
  1. LGD = $10,000,000 × (1 − 0.40) = $6,000,000.
  2. EL = 0.02 × $6,000,000 = $120,000 a year, which is 1.2% of the exposure.
  3. Spread ≈ POD × LGD = 2% × 60% = 1.20% (120 bp).
  4. A narrowing is negative: ΔSpread = −0.005. Duration term: −4.2 × (−0.005) = +0.021 = +2.10%.
  5. Convexity term: ½ × 22 × (−0.005)² = 11 × 0.000025 = 0.000275 = +0.0275%.
  6. %ΔPV ≈ +2.10% + 0.0275% = +2.1275%, about +2.13%. The convexity term is positive whichever way the spread moves, because (ΔSpread)² is positive.

Back to the story

Lehman’s holders recovered 8.625 per 100, so the loss given default was 91.375: almost everything.

Spread ≈ POD × LGD prices that loss a year at a time. With Lehman’s LGD, each 1% of annual default probability needs about 91 bp of spread; an illustrative 3% chance needs 2.74%.

The recovery assumption matters as much as the default probability. A common modelling assumption is 40% recovery (LGD 60%); on it, a 1% spread implies a 1.67% annual chance of default. With Lehman’s actual LGD of 91.375%, the same spread covered only a 1.09% chance.

And a widening spread hurts before any default: for the illustrative bond above, 300 bp of widening costs about 17% of its price.

Exam traps
  • Using the recovery rate where the LGD is needed. With 40% recovery the LGD is 60%: the expected loss on $10 million at a 2% POD is $120,000, not 0.02 × 0.40 × $10 million = $80,000.
  • Giving the convexity term the wrong sign. (ΔSpread)² is positive whether the spread widens or narrows, so for an option-free bond the term is added: −19.50 + 2.475 = −17.025%, not −21.975%.
  • Squaring the change in percent. Square the decimal: 0.03² = 0.0009, so ½ × 55 × 0.0009 = 2.475%. Squaring 3 gives a convexity term of 247.5 and a “+228%” price change for a widening: the units slipped.
  • Treating a lower EBITDA/interest ratio as stronger credit. Coverage (EBITDA/interest, FFO/debt) is stronger when higher; leverage (debt/EBITDA, debt/capital) is stronger when lower.

2. Why. Why is a bond’s yield spread roughly POD × LGD?

  • Late or not, the recovery is money you get back. With 40% recovery only 60% is lost, so a 2% POD needs about 1.2% of spread, not 2%.

  • Right. Expected loss = POD × LGD (Day 26’s expected value with a zero loss in the no-default branch), and the spread is what covers it each year.

  • That multiplies by what is recovered, not what is lost: 2% × 40% = 0.8% instead of 2% × 60% = 1.2%.

Explanation

Lend to 100 such borrowers: on average POD × 100 of them default, and each costs LGD. Spread over the whole book, that is POD × LGD per bond per year. A spread that size leaves you level with a Treasury holder after the expected defaults; anything above it pays for uncertainty and illiquidity.

4. Exam style. An investor holds $5 million of a corporate bond. The annual probability of default is 2.5% and the expected recovery rate is 30%. The expected loss over the next year is closest to:

  • Right: LGD = $5,000,000 × 0.70 = $3,500,000, and EL = 0.025 × $3,500,000 = $87,500.

  • Used the recovery rate instead of the LGD: 0.025 × 0.30 × $5,000,000.

  • Ignored the recovery: 0.025 × $5,000,000 assumes every dollar is lost in a default.

Explanation

EL = POD × exposure × (1 − RR) = 0.025 × 5,000,000 × 0.70 = $87,500, or 1.75% of the exposure: roughly the spread this bond needs.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what did Lehman’s 8.625 teach about credit spreads?

Model answer

A default costs the loss given default, exposure × (1 − recovery), which for Lehman was 91.375 per 100; a spread only covers the expected loss, roughly POD × LGD a year, so if the recovery turns out far lower than assumed, the spread was too thin for the risk, and a widening spread cuts the price (about −ModDur × ΔSpread, softened by convexity) even before any default.

A complete answer says: (1) EL = POD × LGD, with LGD = exposure × (1 − recovery); (2) the spread compensates the expected loss, roughly POD × LGD a year; (3) a recovery lower than assumed means the spread was too thin; (4) widening hits the price through −ModDur × ΔSpread, softened by convexity.
