The Guinness brewer who could not sign his own name, and why four samples need wider error bands

__DATELINE__ · 20 min

An average of n returns strays from the true mean by only about σ/√n, so a confidence interval is the average plus or minus a few of those standard errors; when σ has to be estimated from a small sample, Student’s t makes the band honestly wider.

The brewer who published as “Student”

In the autumn of 1899 William Sealy Gosset left Oxford and joined the Guinness brewery in Dublin.

His work there included trials of barley and hops. The trouble was size. A trial could seldom afford more than a handful of samples, while the statistical methods of the day assumed large ones.

So in 1906–07 Guinness sent him to London, to study in Karl Pearson’s biometric laboratory at University College (for two terms, by one account).

In 1908 he published his answer in Pearson’s journal, Biometrika: “The Probable Error of a Mean”. The name on it was “Student”. Guinness did not let its staff publish under their own names, reportedly after an earlier employee had given away trade secrets.

Here is his problem in today’s terms. Say you have four measurements. Their average is your best guess at the truth. How far can you trust it? And why does the normal curve’s “plus or minus 1.96 standard errors” promise more precision than four numbers can deliver?

The idea

You want the true mean μ: a fund’s long-run average monthly return, say. You never see it. You see a sample of n returns and its mean X̄, your point estimate.

X̄ is never exactly μ, and another sample would give another X̄. The gap X̄ − μ is sampling error.

The standard error is the size of that gap on a typical draw: the standard deviation of X̄ itself. It equals σ/√n. Averages are steadier than single returns, and steadier still as n grows.

A confidence interval wraps the estimate in a band: X̄ ± reliability factor × standard error. The reliability factor is a z-value (1.96 for 95%) when σ is known, or a bigger t-value when you must estimate σ with s.

“95% confidence” means: build intervals this way sample after sample, and 95% of them catch the true μ.

An everyday picture: a bathroom scale whose reading is typically 1 kg off, with each reading’s error independent of the last. Average 4 readings and you are typically 0.5 kg off; average 16, 0.25 kg. Errors partly cancel. They never vanish.

Where samples come from (know the names; how far the 2027 readings cover them is uncertain). Probability samples: simple random (every member equally likely), systematic (every k-th), stratified (random draws inside each subgroup, in proportion), cluster (whole groups at random). Non-probability: convenience, judgmental. Biases: data snooping, sample selection (including survivorship: dead funds drop out of the database), look-ahead, time-period.

Why it works

1. One draw. A single return X has mean μ and variance σ² (Day 24).

2. The mean is a portfolio. X̄ = (1/n)X1 + (1/n)X2 + … + (1/n)Xn: n draws, each with weight 1/n. Each draw has mean μ, so X̄ has mean μ too.

3. Day 32’s portfolio variance. Each weight squared times its variance, plus covariance terms. Independent draws have no covariance, which leaves n terms of (1/n)²σ².

Var(X̄) = n × σ²/n² = σ²/n.

4. Take the root. SD(X̄) = σ/√n: the standard error. Why √n and not n? Variances add; standard deviations do not. Independent errors partly cancel, which is diversification again.

5. The shape. The central limit theorem: whatever the population looks like (if its variance is finite), X̄ from a large sample (n ≥ 30) is approximately normal. So Day 30’s band applies to X̄: 95% of the time it lands within 1.96σ/√n of μ.

6. Flip it. The distance from X̄ to μ is the distance from μ to X̄. So if X̄ lands within 1.96 standard errors of μ 95% of the time, μ lies within 1.96 standard errors of X̄ just as often: X̄ ± 1.96σ/√n.

7. σ unknown: Gosset’s step. Use s/√n instead. But s is only an estimate. With few observations it can easily come out too small, and dividing by a too-small number makes the ratio big. So (X̄ − μ)/(s/√n) swings wider than z.

Its distribution is Student’s t: fatter tails, with n − 1 degrees of freedom (one was spent on X̄, as in Day 24’s n − 1). For 95%: 3.182 at 3 df, 2.131 at 15, 2.045 at 29, closing in on 1.96. (For a small sample, t assumes a roughly normal population.)

In plain words: an average of many readings wobbles less than one reading, by the square root of how many you took. The interval is your average plus or minus about two of those wobbles, and a bit more when you had to guess the wobble from little data.

Memorize
  • Standard error of the sample mean: sX̄ = s / √n (σ / √n if σ known)
  • Confidence interval for the mean: X̄ ± (reliability factor) × standard error
  • Reliability factors for z: 90%: 1.645; 95%: 1.96; 99%: 2.58 (2.576)
  • When to use t: Population variance unknown (use s); df = n − 1
  • Central limit theorem: For any population with mean μ and finite variance σ², the sample mean of a large sample (rule of thumb n ≥ 30) is approximately normal with mean μ and variance σ²/n
  • Sampling biases to name (2027 coverage uncertain): Data snooping; sample selection (incl. survivorship); look-ahead; time-period bias

Worked example

Problem. An analyst has 9 quarterly returns on a small-cap fund: mean 2.0%, sample standard deviation 4.5%. Build a 90% confidence interval for the mean quarterly return (t-table, 8 df, 5% in each tail: 1.860). Then see what wrongly using z = 1.645 would do.

By hand
  1. Standard error: s/√n = 4.5/√9 = 4.5/3 = 1.50%.
  2. σ is unknown and n is small: t with 9 − 1 = 8 df. A 90% two-sided interval leaves 5% in each tail: t0.05 = 1.860.
  3. Half-width: 1.860 × 1.50 = 2.790%.
  4. Interval: 2.0 ± 2.790 = −0.79% to 4.79%.
  5. With z = 1.645 instead: 2.0 ± 2.4675 = −0.4675% to 4.4675%. About 12% narrower: it claims a precision that nine data points cannot give.
  6. Zero is inside the interval: these nine quarters cannot rule out a true mean of 0. Day 44 turns that into a test.

Back to the story

Gosset’s four samples leave 4 − 1 = 3 degrees of freedom. For 95% the t-table says 3.182, not 1.96: the honest band is 62% wider.

Use 1.96 anyway and a band that claims 95% catches the true mean only about 86% of the time (for normally distributed measurements).

More samples shrink the penalty: 2.262 with 10, 2.045 with 30, already close to 1.96. In the walkthrough, 16 months gave 2.131 instead of 1.96, a band about 9% wider.

That was Gosset’s answer: small samples are usable, provided the error bands are honestly wider. The distribution still carries his pen name: Student’s t.

Exam traps
  • Dividing by n instead of √n. The walkthrough’s standard error is 4/√16 = 1.00, not 4/16 = 0.25; the wrong one makes the interval four times too narrow.
  • Using z when σ is unknown and the sample is small. In the worked example z = 1.645 gives −0.47% to 4.47%; the right t (1.860, 8 df) gives −0.79% to 4.79%.
  • A one-tailed value for a two-sided interval. A 95% interval puts 2.5% in each tail: z = 1.96 (1.645 is the 90% value), and on the TI-84 invT(.975, df), not invT(.95, df).
  • Mixing up σ and σ/√n. μ ± 1.96σ (Day 30) is where 95% of single returns land; X̄ ± 1.96σ/√n is a 95% interval for the mean.

2. Why. A large sample gives a 95% confidence interval for a fund’s mean return, using z = 1.96. If the sample were four times as large and s came out the same, the interval’s width would:

  • That would be true if the standard error divided by n. It divides by √n, and √4 = 2.

  • Right. SE = s/√n: four times the data doubles √n, so the standard error, and with it the width, halves.

  • s measures how much single returns swing, and more data does not change that. But the mean of more returns swings less: s/√n falls.

Explanation

Step 3 of “Why it works”: Var(X̄) = σ²/n, so the standard deviation of X̄ is σ/√n. Halving an interval takes four times the data; cutting it to a tenth takes a hundred times. Precision is expensive.

3. Twist: solve for n. Monthly returns have a known population standard deviation of 4%. How many monthly observations do you need for the 95% confidence interval’s half-width to be at most 0.5 percentage points? A whole number.

observations
Hint σ is known, so z = 1.96. Set 1.96 × 4/√n = 0.5, solve for √n first, then square. Round up.
Solution

1.96 × 4/√n ≤ 0.5, so √n ≥ 1.96 × 4/0.5 = 15.68, so n ≥ 15.68² = 245.86. Round up: 246. (At 245 the half-width is 0.50088, just over; at 246 it is 0.49986.)

4. Exam style. A sample of 64 monthly returns has a mean of 1.5%. The population standard deviation is known to be 4%. The lower limit of the 95% confidence interval for the population mean is closest to:

  • Right. SE = 4/√64 = 0.50; 1.5 − 1.96 × 0.50 = 0.52%.

  • σ divided by n instead of √n: 4/64 = 0.0625, and 1.5 − 1.96 × 0.0625 = 1.3775.

  • The one-tailed 1.645, which is the 90% value: 1.5 − 1.645 × 0.50 = 0.6775.

Explanation

σ known, so z; 95% two-sided, so 2.5% per tail and 1.96. The interval is 0.52% to 2.48%. Had σ been unknown, you would use s and t with 63 df (1.998), barely different at this sample size.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why did Gosset need a new distribution for small samples, and when do you use t instead of z?

Model answer

When σ is unknown it has to be estimated by s, and with only a few observations s is itself unreliable, so (X̄ − μ)/(s/√n) has fatter tails than the normal; Student’s t, with n − 1 degrees of freedom, supplies the larger reliability factor that keeps a 95% interval honest. Use t whenever σ is unknown; as n grows, t shrinks toward z.

A complete answer says: (1) σ is unknown, so s is estimated from the data; (2) with few observations s is shaky, so t has fatter tails and a larger reliability factor (3.182 against 1.96 at 3 df); (3) df = n − 1, and t approaches z as n grows.
