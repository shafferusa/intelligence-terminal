From Day 45. Two funds are compared over the same 25 months. The mean monthly difference in their returns is 0.60% and the standard deviation of those differences is 1.50%. The t-statistic for H0: μd = 0 is closest to:

  • That is 0.60 / 1.50: divided by sd itself instead of the standard error sd / √n.

  • Right. Standard error = 1.50 / √25 = 0.30, so t = 0.60 / 0.30 = 2.00, with 25 − 1 = 24 degrees of freedom.

  • That is 0.60 / (1.50 / 25): the standard error divides by √n = 5, not by n = 25.

Why

The same months make the samples dependent, so this is the paired-comparisons test: t = d̄ / (sd / √n), df = n − 1 = 24.

The day gold went up

After the close on Friday 5 August 2011, Standard & Poor’s cut the United States’ credit rating from AAA to AA+.

On Monday 8 August the S&P 500 fell 79.92 points, or 6.66%, to 1,119.46.

Gold went the other way. December gold futures settled at a record $1,713.20 an ounce on Comex, up $61.40, or 3.7%. On 6 September spot gold touched a new record above $1,920.

Now the odd part. Gold is no calmer than stocks. Over the 30 years to 2019, State Street measured gold’s annualized volatility at 15.44%, against 14.32% for the S&P 500.

Yet over two centuries gold has barely beaten inflation. Jeremy Siegel’s figures for 1802–2012 give stocks a real return of about 6.6% a year, and gold about 0.7%.

If gold swings about as much as stocks, why have investors accepted roughly 6 percentage points a year less for holding it? Which kind of risk does the market actually pay for?

The idea

Split any stock’s risk in two.

Systematic (market) risk comes from the whole economy: recessions, rate shocks, a downgrade of the US government. Nearly every stock feels it, so no amount of diversifying removes it.

Unsystematic (specific) risk is news about one company: a failed product, a lawsuit, a chief executive who quits. Spread over many stocks, these surprises mostly cancel.

The second kind can be removed for free, just by owning many stocks. So nobody is paid to carry it. The market pays only for the first kind.

Beta measures the first kind: how far the stock moves, on average, when the market moves. β = 1.2: the market up 10%, the stock up 12% on average. β = 0: no link. β below 0: it tends to rise when the market falls.

The market model writes the split as an equation: Ri = αi + βiRm + ei. The βiRm part follows the market; ei is the stock’s own noise.

The CAPM (capital asset pricing model) puts a price on beta: required return = risk-free rate + beta × market risk premium. Draw it with beta across and return up and you get a straight line, the security market line (SML).

Why it works

Step 1: what one stock adds to a big portfolio. Day 32’s portfolio variance, with N stocks in equal weights, comes to: σp2 = (1/N) × average variance + (1 − 1/N) × average covariance.

As N grows, the first term melts away. Stocks with σ = 36% and pairwise ρ = 0.3: one stock, σp = 36.0%; thirty, 20.5%; a thousand, 19.7%, the floor √0.03888. Covariance is what stays. So inside a diversified portfolio, a stock’s risk is its covariance with the market.

Step 2: count it in market units. Divide by the market’s variance: βi = Cov(Ri, Rm) / σm2, so the market’s own beta is 1. Put in Day 25’s Cov = ρσiσm and one σm cancels: βi = ρimσi / σm. Day 50 found the same number as the slope of Ri on Rm.

Step 3: build a copy you can price. T-bills: β = 0, return Rf. The market: β = 1, return E(Rm). Put half your money in each: beta 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 1 = 0.5, expected return 0.5Rf + 0.5E(Rm) = Rf + 0.5[E(Rm) − Rf]. Swap 0.5 for any βi: Rf + βi[E(Rm) − Rf]. (Above β = 1, borrow at Rf; same algebra.)

Step 4: same priced risk, same return. A stock with that beta carries the same market risk as the mix; its extra noise ei diversifies away. If it offered less, investors would hold the mix. If more (positive alpha), they would buy it, pushing its price up and its return down to the line. That line is the CAPM.

Step 5: SML, not CML. Day 34’s CML prices by σ, but only efficient portfolios (T-bills plus the market) sit on it: their risk is all systematic, so ρ = 1, β = σp / σm, and the SML becomes the CML. A single stock’s σ includes diversifiable risk, so pricing it by σ overcharges.

Several priced risks. The arbitrage pricing theory (APT) allows several systematic factors, each with a sensitivity βk and a premium λk: E(R) = RF + λ1β1 + … + λKβK. The CAPM is the one-factor case.

In plain words: you are paid only for the risk you cannot get rid of, and beta counts how much of it you hold.

Memorize
  • Beta: βi = Cov(Ri, Rm) / σm2 = ρim × σi / σm
  • Capital asset pricing model: E(Ri) = Rf + βi × [E(Rm) − Rf]
  • Alpha against the SML: α = forecast (or actual) return − CAPM required return; α > 0 → undervalued
  • Arbitrage pricing / multi-factor model: E(Rp) = RF + λ1 βp,1 + … + λK βp,K
  • CML vs SML x-axis: CML: total risk σ (efficient portfolios); SML: beta (any asset or portfolio)

Worked example

Problem. A stock’s returns have a covariance of 0.0216 with the market’s, and the market’s standard deviation is 15%. Rf = 3% and E(Rm) = 9%. Find beta and the CAPM required return. An analyst forecasts 8%: undervalued or overvalued?

By hand
  1. β = Cov / σm2 = 0.0216 / 0.152 = 0.0216 / 0.0225 = 0.96. Keep both in decimals.
  2. Same answer in correlation form: if σi = 24%, ρ = 0.0216 / (0.24 × 0.15) = 0.0216 / 0.036 = 0.60, and β = 0.60 × 24 / 15 = 0.96.
  3. Market risk premium = 9% − 3% = 6%.
  4. E(Ri) = 3% + 0.96 × 6% = 3% + 5.76% = 8.76%.
  5. α = 8% − 8.76% = −0.76%. The forecast plots below the SML, so the stock is overvalued: its price is too high for the return it offers.
  6. On the SML the x-axis is beta (0.96), not σ. The CML uses σ and applies only to efficient portfolios.

Back to the story

The CAPM does not price gold’s σ. It prices how gold moves with the market, and on 8 August 2011 it moved the other way: stocks down 6.66%, gold up 3.7%.

Suppose, for illustration only, that gold’s correlation with stocks is 0.1 (an assumption, not a measurement). With State Street’s volatilities, β = 0.1 × 15.44 / 14.32 = 0.108. With Rf = 4% and a 5.5% premium, the CAPM asks only 4.59% a year: little more than cash.

A stock with the same 15.44% volatility but a correlation of 0.9 has β = 0.970 and must offer 9.34%. Same σ, required returns 4.74 points apart.

At ρ = 0.1 only ρ2 = 1% of gold’s variance is market risk; the other 99% diversifies away. The CAPM cannot prove Siegel’s numbers, but it explains their direction: the market pays for beta, not for volatility.

Exam traps
  • Multiplying beta by the market return instead of the market risk premium. In the worked example, 3% + 0.96 × 9% = 11.64%, not 8.76%. Check whether a question gives E(Rm) or the premium.
  • Using σ (the CML) to price a single stock. For the walkthrough stock, 4% + (5.5 / 18) × 36 = 15.00%: 4.40 points too much, charged for risk that diversifies away, and it would wrongly call the 14% forecast overvalued.
  • Calling a stock above the SML overvalued. Above the line = more return than its beta requires = price too low = undervalued.
  • Mixing decimals and percents. A covariance of 0.0216 is in decimals, so σm must be 0.15: dividing by 152 gives a beta of 0.000096.

2. Why. Why does the CAPM reward beta rather than total risk σ?

  • Right. Whatever anyone can shed for free by diversifying cannot command a reward; what is left in a diversified portfolio is each stock’s covariance with the market, and beta measures exactly that.

  • Estimation is not the reason; beta is itself built from σ’s and a correlation (or a regression). The reason is what diversification removes.

  • They need not: β = ρσi / σm, so a low correlation gives a low beta even with a high σ. Gold, about as volatile as stocks, is the example.

Explanation

In a portfolio of many stocks the own-variance term (1/N × average variance) melts away and only covariance stays (Step 1). So the risk a stock adds is its covariance with the market, which beta measures in market units. The stock’s own noise ei can be removed for free, so competition leaves no premium for it.

3. Twist: solve for beta. A stock’s CAPM required return is 12.4%. The risk-free rate is 4% and the expected return on the market is 10%. What beta does that imply? To two decimals.

β
Hint Rearrange the CAPM: subtract Rf from both sides, then divide by the market risk premium E(Rm) − Rf.
Solution

12.4 = 4 + β(10 − 4), so 8.4 = 6β and β = 8.4 / 6 = 1.40. Check: 4 + 1.4 × 6 = 12.4.

BA II Plus: ( 12.4 − 4 ) ÷ ( 10 − 4 ) = → 1.4000.

TI-84 Plus: ( 12.4 − 4 ) ÷ ( 10 − 4 ) ENTER → 1.4000.

4. Exam style. A stock has a beta of 1.3. The risk-free rate is 3% and the expected return on the market is 9%. According to the CAPM, the stock’s required return is closest to:

  • That is 3% + 1.3 × 9%: beta multiplied by the market return instead of the market risk premium.

  • That is 1.3 × (9% − 3%): the premium is right, but Rf was left out.

  • Right: 3% + 1.3 × (9% − 3%) = 3% + 7.8% = 10.80%.

Explanation

E(Ri) = Rf + β[E(Rm) − Rf]. A quick sense check: a beta above 1 must ask more than the market’s 9%, and 10.80% does; 7.80% asks less, so it cannot be right.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: how can gold be about as volatile as stocks yet be expected to earn much less?

Model answer

Only systematic risk is rewarded, because the rest diversifies away. Gold’s beta, ρσgold / σm, is small when its correlation with stocks is low, however large its σ, so its CAPM required return, Rf + β[E(Rm) − Rf], sits close to the risk-free rate.

A complete answer says: (1) only systematic (market) risk, measured by beta, is priced, because unsystematic risk diversifies away; (2) β = ρσi / σm, so a low correlation gives a low beta even with a high σ; (3) the CAPM required return is then close to Rf.
