Silicon Valley Bank’s $15 billion hole

Thursday, 9 March 2023. Depositors tried to pull $42 billion out of Silicon Valley Bank in a single day. The next morning, 10 March, regulators closed it.

The hole had been dug earlier. In the deposit boom of 2020–21, SVB put much of its cash into long-dated bonds: mostly mortgage-backed securities from government agencies, many maturing in 10 years or more.

Then rates rose. At the end of 2022 the bank carried $91.3 billion of these bonds as “held to maturity”, at amortized cost: roughly what it had paid. Their fair value, what they would actually fetch, was $76.2 billion.

That is an unrealized loss of about $15 billion. The bank’s equity was roughly $16 billion.

The Federal Reserve’s review of the failure later put that portfolio’s weighted-average duration at 6.2 years.

No issuer had defaulted on those bonds. Yet rising rates had nearly wiped out the bank’s equity. How do you turn “rates went up” into dollars lost?

The idea

Yesterday you built duration from the cash flows: each payment’s time, weighted by its PV. Today, a shortcut that needs no cash-flow table: reprice the bond.

Lower the yield a hair and price it: call that PV−. Raise the yield by the same hair: PV+. The price gap, per unit of yield, is how steeply the price falls as the yield rises. Divide by today’s price, PV0, and the steepness becomes a percent. That is the approximate modified duration.

Picture a hillside. To know how steep it is where you stand, take one step down and one step up and compare the heights. Height gap ÷ the distance between your feet = the slope.

Then turn the percent into money. Money duration = modified duration × what the position is worth. Multiply it by a yield change, as a decimal, and you get dollars. For one basis point (0.01%) that dollar figure has its own name: the price value of a basis point, PVBP.

Why it works

Start from yesterday’s rule and turn it around.

  1. Duration is a slope. Day 39: %ΔPV ≈ −ModDur × ΔYield. So ModDur ≈ −%ΔPV/ΔYield: the percent the price moves per unit of yield. Measure that and you have it.
  2. Measure it with a big step, so you can see it. The walkthrough’s bond (10 years, 3% annual coupon) is worth 91.8891 at 4%. At 3% it is 100.0000, up 8.1109; at 5%, 84.5565, down 7.3326. As a percent of 91.8891, per unit of yield: 8.1109/(0.01 × 91.8891) = 8.8268 going down, 7.3326/(0.01 × 91.8891) = 7.9798 going up.
  3. Two answers, because the price–yield line is a curve (Day 41’s convexity): a fall in yield helps more than a rise hurts. So average them: [(PV− − PV0) + (PV0 − PV+)] / (2 × ΔYield × PV0). The PV0s on top cancel, leaving (PV− − PV+)/(2 × ΔYield × PV0). That is the formula. The 2 is there because the two prices sit two steps apart. Here: (8.8268 + 7.9798)/2 = 8.4033.
  4. Shrink the step. At ±1 bp the two sides nearly agree (8.3917 and 8.3832), and their average, 8.3874, is the exact modified duration to four decimals. Times (1 + r) it is the Macaulay duration (Day 39): 8.3874 × 1.04 = 8.7229.
  5. Percent to dollars. A percent change times the value is a dollar change: ΔPV ≈ −ModDur × ΔYield × PV. Call ModDur × PV the money duration: ΔPV ≈ −MoneyDur × ΔYield. With ΔYield = 0.0001 that is the PVBP, which you can also read straight off the prices: (PV− − PV+)/2, the average move for 1 bp.
  6. A portfolio. If every yield moves by the same ΔYield, the dollar changes add: −Σ Di × MVi × ΔYield. Divide by the total value, MV, and the portfolio’s duration is Σ (MVi/MV) × Di. Market-value weights, because the dollars at risk depend on what each bond is worth, not its face value.

In plain words: wiggle the yield a tiny bit each way and see how far the price swings. That is how touchy the bond is. Times what you own, it is dollars.

ApproxModDur = (PV− − PV+) / (2 × ΔYield × PV0)

ApproxMacDur = ApproxModDur × (1 + r)

MoneyDur = AnnModDur × PVfull;   ΔPV ≈ −MoneyDur × ΔYield

PVBP = (PV− − PV+) / 2, with yields ±1 bp

Dp ≈ Σ wi × Di

PV0
the bond’s price at today’s yield
PV−
the price with the yield lowered by ΔYield (the higher price)
PV+
the price with the yield raised by ΔYield (the lower price)
ΔYield
the yield step or change, as a decimal: 1 bp = 0.0001, 25 bp = 0.0025
r
the yield per period, as a decimal
AnnModDur
annual modified duration; ApproxModDur estimates it
PVfull
the position’s market value, including accrued interest (on a coupon date, just price × size)
MoneyDur
money duration, in currency: times ΔYield it gives the estimated money change
PVBP
price value of a basis point: the money change for a 1 bp move
wi, Di
bond i’s share of the portfolio’s market value, and its duration
Memorize
  • Approximate modified duration: ApproxModDur = (PV− − PV+) / (2 × ΔYield × PV0)
  • Money duration: MoneyDur = AnnModDur × PVfull of the position; ΔPV ≈ −MoneyDur × ΔYield
  • Price value of a basis point: PVBP = (PV− − PV+) / 2, with yields ±1 bp
  • Portfolio duration (approximation): Dp ≈ Σ wi × Di, wi = market-value weights
  • Approximate Macaulay duration: ApproxMacDur = ApproxModDur × (1 + r)

Worked example

Problem. A bond is priced at 98.50 per 100. If its yield fell 25 bp the price would be 100.80; if it rose 25 bp, 96.25. Find its approximate modified duration, the money duration of a $5 million holding (market value), and the estimated change in value for a 10 bp rise.

By hand
  1. PV− − PV+ = 100.80 − 96.25 = 4.55.
  2. 2 × ΔYield × PV0 = 2 × 0.0025 × 98.50 = 0.4925.
  3. ApproxModDur = 4.55/0.4925 = 9.2386.
  4. Money duration = 9.2386 × $5,000,000 = $46,192,893.
  5. +10 bp: ΔPV ≈ −$46,192,893 × 0.0010 = −$46,193.
  6. Each basis point: $46,192,893 × 0.0001 = $4,619, the holding’s PVBP.

Answer: ApproxModDur 8.3874 (ApproxMacDur 8.7229); PVBP 0.0771 per 100, about $7,707 on the position; money duration $77,071,256; a 200 bp rise costs about $1,541,425. Repricing at 6% gives a loss of $1,396,937: on a move this big the straight line overstates the loss. That gap is convexity, Day 41.

Back to the story

Money duration answers it. Run it backwards first: treat the 6.2 years as a modified duration (a simplification) and the $91.3 billion cost as the starting value. The $15.1 billion loss is 16.54% of that, so yields must have risen about 16.54 ÷ 6.2 = 2.67 percentage points.

Compare the 5-year Treasury yield: about 1.37% to 3.99% during 2022, up 2.62 points. Money duration turns that into 6.2 × 0.0262 × $91.3 billion = about $14.8 billion. Close to the real hole.

Now forwards. At its $76.2 billion market value, the portfolio’s money duration was 6.2 × $76.2 billion = $472.44 billion: about $4.72 billion for every further 1-point rise, $47.2 million per basis point (its PVBP).

A rough check: the bonds were bought at different times, and mortgage bonds’ duration lengthens as rates rise. But no borrower defaulted. Duration alone turned a rate chart into a hole in the balance sheet.

Exam traps
  • The 2 in the denominator. PV− and PV+ are two steps apart. Drop the 2 and the walkthrough’s 8.3874 doubles to 16.7748.
  • ΔYield as a decimal. 1 bp = 0.0001, 25 bp = 0.0025, 50 bp = 0.005. Typing 0.01 for one basis point gives 0.0839, a duration 100 times too small.
  • Money duration uses market value, not par. $10 million par of the walkthrough’s bond is worth $9,188,910. Using par gives a money duration of $83,874,206 and a 200 bp loss of $1,677,484 instead of $1,541,425.
  • Portfolio weights are market-value weights. Dp ≈ Σ wi × Di with wi = MVi/MV. Weighting by par value, or taking a plain average, gives the wrong duration.

2. Why. Why does the approximate modified duration formula divide by 2 × ΔYield?

  • No: the answer comes out per unit of annual yield. The 2 has nothing to do with how often the coupon is paid.

  • Averaging the two sides shrinks the curvature error but does not remove it: with ±1-point steps the walkthrough’s bond gives 8.4033, against 8.3874 with ±1 bp.

  • Right: the price gap spans a yield change of 2 × ΔYield, so that is what you divide by to get the change per unit of yield.

Explanation

A slope is rise over run. The run from 3.99% to 4.01% is 0.0002, two basis points, not one. Equivalently, the formula is the average of the two one-sided slopes, (PV− − PV0)/(ΔYield × PV0) and (PV0 − PV+)/(ΔYield × PV0): add them and halve, and the 2 appears.

3. Twist: a portfolio. You hold two bonds. Bond X: par $6.5 million, market value $6.0 million, modified duration 4.0. Bond Y: par $3.5 million, market value $4.0 million, modified duration 9.0. If all yields rise by 25 basis points, about how much does the portfolio lose? In dollars, to the nearest dollar.

dollars
Hint Weight each duration by its share of the market value, not the par. Then money duration × ΔYield (25 bp = 0.0025).
Solution

Total market value $10 million; weights 6/10 = 0.60 and 4/10 = 0.40. Dp = 0.60 × 4.0 + 0.40 × 9.0 = 6.0. Money duration = 6.0 × $10,000,000 = $60,000,000. Loss ≈ $60,000,000 × 0.0025 = $150,000.

Check bond by bond: 4.0 × $6,000,000 × 0.0025 = $60,000 and 9.0 × $4,000,000 × 0.0025 = $90,000; together $150,000.

BA II Plus: ( .6 × 4 + .4 × 9 ) × 10000000 × .0025 = → 150,000.0000. TI-84 Plus: the same keys with ENTER → 150000.0000.

The trap: par weights (0.65 and 0.35) give a duration of 5.75 and a loss of $143,750.

5. Say it back. A bank treasurer holds $50 billion (market value) of bonds with a modified duration of 5. In one or two sentences, explain money duration and PVBP to the treasurer, with the numbers.

Model answer

Your money duration is modified duration times market value, 5 × $50 billion = $250 billion; multiply it by a yield change in decimals to get dollars, so a 1-point rise (0.01) costs about $2.5 billion. The PVBP is the dollar change for one basis point, $250 billion × 0.0001 = $25 million. Both are straight-line estimates, good for small moves; for big ones convexity matters.

A complete answer says: (1) money duration = annual modified duration × market value, and money duration × ΔYield (as a decimal) is the estimated dollar change; (2) PVBP = the dollar change for a 1 bp move, money duration × 0.0001; (3) it is a straight-line approximation that holds for small moves; large moves need convexity.
