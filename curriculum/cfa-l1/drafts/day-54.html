When a bond’s cash flows change with interest rates, no formula can read its risk off a schedule: you shift the benchmark curve down and up, let a model replay what borrowers would do, and measure the price swing, which for mortgages is lopsided.

From Day 53. A portfolio returned 9.8% with a beta of 0.8. The market returned 8.5% and the risk-free rate was 2.6%. What is the portfolio’s Jensen’s alpha? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint First the CAPM return the portfolio owed: Rf + β × (Rm − Rf). Alpha is what it actually earned above that.
Solution

Required = 2.6 + 0.8 × (8.5 − 2.6) = 2.6 + 0.8 × 5.9 = 2.6 + 4.72 = 7.32%. Alpha = 9.8 − 7.32 = 2.48%.

BA II Plus: 9.8 − ( 2.6 + .8 × ( 8.5 − 2.6 ) ) = → 2.4800.

TI-84 Plus: 9.8 − ( 2.6 + .8 × ( 8.5 − 2.6 ) ) ENTER → 2.4800.

The trap: beta times the market return, 9.8 − (2.6 + 0.8 × 8.5), gives 0.40%.

From Day 39. An annual-pay bond yields 5% and has a Macaulay duration of 8.40. If its yield falls by 30 basis points, its price changes by approximately:

  • Right: ModDur = 8.40 / 1.05 = 8.00, and −8.00 × (−0.30) = +2.40%.

  • Macaulay duration used as the sensitivity: 8.40 × 0.30. Divide by 1 + r first.

  • The right size with the wrong sign. Yield down, price up.

Why

%ΔPV ≈ −ModDur × ΔYield = −(8.40/1.05) × (−0.30) = +2.40%. That formula assumes the bond’s cash flows stay fixed when its yield moves. Today is about bonds where they don’t.

The market-neutral funds that were not

Friday, 4 February 1994. The Federal Reserve raised its target for the federal funds rate from 3% to 3.25%, a quarter of a point. Bond markets around the world sold off.

In New York, David Askin’s firm, Askin Capital Management, ran three funds: Granite Partners, Granite Corporation and the Quartz Hedge Fund. They held mortgage-backed securities, bonds paid out of the monthly payments on pools of home loans.

The funds had been presented as “market-neutral”. In the words of later court opinions, they held “a balanced mix of ‘bullish’ and ‘bearish’ securities”: some should gain when rates rose, others when rates fell, so the gains and losses would offset whichever way rates moved.

They were also highly leveraged, built largely with borrowed money. As prices fell, the lenders made margin calls. The forced sales showed the portfolio was worth far less than the “theoretical” values the firm had been reporting.

In April 1994 the funds, which had held about $600 million of investors’ money, went into bankruptcy. By later accounts, nearly all of it was lost.

If part of the book gained when rates rose and part when they fell, how could rising rates wipe it out? And why does ordinary duration, which treats a bond’s cash flows as fixed, miss the danger?

The idea

Days 39 to 41 assumed a bond’s cash flows are fixed: the coupons and principal arrive on schedule whatever rates do. Only the discounting changes.

Many bonds break that rule. A callable bond (Day 16) can be repaid early by the issuer, and it will be when rates fall. A mortgage-backed security (MBS) passes on homeowners’ payments, and homeowners can prepay at any time. They refinance when rates fall and stay put when rates rise. In both cases the investor has sold the borrower an option.

Picture a landlord. When market rents fall, tenants paying the old high rent move somewhere cheaper. When rents rise, tenants on cheap leases never leave. Good news is cut short; bad news drags on. That is negative convexity.

With cash flows that move, there is no fixed schedule to compute a Macaulay duration from. So measure the sensitivity directly: shift the benchmark yield curve down and up a little, let a model price each case, and see how far the price swings. That is effective duration; the same three prices give effective convexity.

Why it works

  1. Start from Day 40. Reprice the bond one step down and one step up in yield. The price moves PV− − PV+ over a span of 2 × ΔYield; divide by PV0 and you have the % change per unit of rates: duration.
  2. What Day 40 held fixed: the cash flows. Only the discounting changed. A callable’s or an MBS’s cash flows change with rates, so there is not even one yield to move.
  3. So shift the benchmark curve. Move the government curve down by ΔCurve. A model (a rate tree for a callable, a prepayment model for an MBS) re-decides the cash flows and values them at the same OAS (Day 38): PV−. Up: PV+. Day 40’s arithmetic, unchanged: EffDur = (PV− − PV+) / (2 × ΔCurve × PV0). For an option-free bond nothing moves, so EffDur ≈ ApproxModDur.
  4. Convexity from the same prices (Day 41). PV− + PV+ − 2PV0 = (PV− − PV0) − (PV0 − PV+): the gain when the curve falls minus the loss when it rises. Bigger gain, positive; bigger loss, negative.
  5. Why mortgages go negative. Rates down: refinancing hands back principal at par just when the bond is worth more, capping the gain (contraction risk); a callable is capped near its call price. Rates up: prepayments slow and the MBS lasts longer just when that hurts most (extension risk). Gain < loss, so EffCon < 0.
  6. It fits exactly. For the walkthrough’s bond, −EffDur × ΔCurve + ½ × EffCon × (ΔCurve)² at ±25 bp, times PV0, gives back +0.84 and −0.86: slope and bend of a curve through the three prices.
  7. Key rate durations. Shift one maturity’s par rate only and reprice. All key rates together make the parallel shift, so they add up to EffDur: 0.35 + 1.60 + 1.45 = 3.40 (2-, 5- and 10-year). They show where on the curve the risk sits.
  8. Prepayment speed. If 0.8% of the balance prepays each month (the SMM), 99.2% survives each month and 0.99212 = 90.81% survives the year. So 9.19% prepays: the CPR. Not 12 × 0.8% = 9.60%: each month’s 0.8% comes out of a smaller balance (Day 4’s compounding).

In plain words: if you can’t know a bond’s cash flows in advance, nudge rates down and up, let the model replay what borrowers would do, and measure how far the price really moves.

EffDur = (PV− − PV+) / (2 × ΔCurve × PV0)

EffCon = (PV− + PV+ − 2 × PV0) / [(ΔCurve)² × PV0]

%ΔPV ≈ −EffDur × ΔCurve + ½ × EffCon × (ΔCurve)²

CPR = 1 − (1 − SMM)12

PV0
the bond’s price today, at the current benchmark curve
PV−
the model price after the benchmark curve shifts down by ΔCurve (the higher price)
PV+
the model price after the curve shifts up by ΔCurve (the lower price)
ΔCurve
the size of the parallel shift in the benchmark curve, as a decimal (25 bp = 0.0025)
EffDur
effective duration: about the % price change for a 1-point (100 bp) shift in the curve
EffCon
effective convexity: the bend; negative when the gain from a fall is smaller than the loss from a rise
SMM
single monthly mortality: the share of the remaining balance (after scheduled principal) prepaid in a month
CPR
conditional prepayment rate: the SMM expressed as an annual rate

The other mortgage words (describe, no math):

  • PSA benchmark: 100 PSA is a CPR of 0.2% in month 1, rising 0.2% a month to 6% in month 30, then 6%. 150 PSA is 1.5 times those speeds.
  • WAC (weighted average coupon): the pool’s mortgage rates, weighted by balance.
  • WAM (weighted average maturity): the loans’ remaining terms, weighted by balance.
  • WAL (weighted average life): the average time until principal comes back. Faster prepayment shortens it.
  • LTV (loan-to-value): loan ÷ property value. Lower LTV, more owner equity, less default risk.
Memorize
  • Effective duration: EffDur = (PV− − PV+) / (2 × ΔCurve × PV0)
  • Effective convexity: EffCon = [PV− + PV+ − 2 × PV0] / [(ΔCurve)² × PV0]
  • Conditional prepayment rate from the single monthly mortality: CPR = 1 − (1 − SMM)12
  • Negative convexity: Price rises less when rates fall than it falls when rates rise (callables, MBS)
  • Key rate durations: Sum of key rate durations = effective duration

Worked example

Problem. A mortgage pass-through is priced at 98.50. A prepayment model reprices it at 100.10 if the benchmark curve falls 50 bp and at 96.60 if it rises 50 bp. Find its effective duration and effective convexity, estimate the price change for a 100 bp rise and a 100 bp fall, and convert the model’s 6% CPR into a monthly SMM.

By hand
  1. Look first: a 50 bp fall gains 1.60; a 50 bp rise loses 1.90. Lopsided already.
  2. EffDur = (100.10 − 96.60) / (2 × 0.005 × 98.50) = 3.50 / 0.985 = 3.5533.
  3. EffCon = (100.10 + 96.60 − 2 × 98.50) / (0.005² × 98.50) = −0.30 / 0.0024625 = −121.83.
  4. Convexity term for a 100 bp move either way: ½ × (−121.83) × 0.01² = −0.0061, or −0.61%.
  5. +100 bp: −3.5533% − 0.61% = −4.16%. −100 bp: +3.5533% − 0.61% = +2.94%. The convexity term hurts both ways.
  6. SMM from CPR: run the survival backwards. SMM = 1 − (1 − 0.06)1/12 = 1 − 0.994857 = 0.5143% a month, not 6%/12 = 0.50%.

Back to the story

Mortgage cash flows are not fixed. When rates rise, homeowners stop refinancing, prepayments slow and the securities’ lives extend just when a longer life hurts most. When rates fall, prepayments speed up and cap the gain.

So the risk can only be measured with a model: reprice the securities with a prepayment model under a shifted benchmark curve. Like Askin’s “theoretical” values, the answer is only as good as that model.

Today’s callable bond shows the lopsidedness: a 25 bp fall adds 0.84, a 25 bp rise takes away 0.86. Effective duration 3.3966 and effective convexity −31.97 say a 100 bp rise costs about 3.56%, a 100 bp fall earns only about 3.24%.

Borrowed money multiplies it. At an illustrative five-to-one leverage (not Askin’s actual figure), that 3.56% fall is a 17.8% loss of the investors’ own money. An asymmetry like that turns a “neutral” book into a one-way bet.

Exam traps
  • Modified duration for a callable or an MBS. It assumes fixed cash flows and a change in the bond’s own yield. Theirs move with rates: use effective duration, from model prices under curve shifts.
  • CPR = 12 × SMM. 12 × 0.8% = 9.60%; the right CPR is 1 − 0.99212 = 9.19%. Backwards too: a 6% CPR is an SMM of 0.5143%, not 0.50%.
  • “Negative convexity helps when rates fall.” It caps the gain. The term ½ × EffCon × (ΔCurve)² is negative for falls and rises.
  • Units. ΔCurve is a decimal: 25 bp = 0.0025, and 0.0025² = 0.00000625. Typed as 25 or 0.25, the answer is nonsense.

2. Why. Why is effective duration, not modified duration, the right measure of interest rate risk for a callable bond or a mortgage-backed security?

  • Modified duration works for any option-free bond, coupons and all (Day 39). The problem is not coupons; it is cash flows that change with rates.

  • Both are in the same units: % price change per 1-point move. The difference is how the prices are produced, not the units.

  • Right. Modified duration moves the bond’s own yield with the cash flows held still. Effective duration shifts the benchmark curve and lets the model re-decide the calls and prepayments.

Explanation

A callable’s or an MBS’s cash flows depend on rates: falling rates bring calls and refinancing, rising rates slow prepayments. With no fixed schedule there is no single yield to move. So you shift the benchmark curve ±ΔCurve, reprice with a model at a constant OAS, and plug the prices into (PV− − PV+) / (2 × ΔCurve × PV0).

3. Twist: chain the two measures. An MBS has an effective duration of 5.20 and an effective convexity of −80. Estimate its percentage price change if the benchmark curve rises 100 bp. As a percent, to two decimals, with its sign.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what is negative convexity, and why did it hurt funds like Askin’s?

Model answer

Negative convexity means the price rises less when rates fall than it falls when rates rise (EffCon < 0). It comes from the borrower’s option: when rates fall, homeowners prepay (or the issuer calls) and cap the gain; when rates rise, prepayments slow and the security’s life extends just when that hurts. Bought with borrowed money, those bigger losses were multiplied into margin calls and forced sales.

A complete answer says: (1) the price gains less from a fall in rates than it loses from an equal rise, EffCon < 0; (2) the cause is the prepayment (or call) option: faster prepayment caps the gain, slower prepayment extends the life when rates rise; (3) leverage magnifies the lopsided losses.
