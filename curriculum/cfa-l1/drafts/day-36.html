Cutting a Treasury bond into zeros

In 1982 Merrill Lynch began buying long-term US Treasury bonds, placing them in a trust, and selling separate receipts: one for each coupon, one for the principal.

It called them Treasury Investment Growth Receipts, or TIGRs. Each receipt paid one sum on one date and nothing before it: a zero-coupon bond, backed by Treasuries. Salomon Brothers answered with its own version, CATS.

In January 1985 the Treasury announced a program of its own, STRIPS: Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal of Securities. From February 1985 each coupon and the principal of eligible Treasury bonds could trade on its own, as a zero-coupon security.

That month a Christian Science Monitor headline called the private receipts “the Treasury’s feline menagerie”: CATS and TIGRS, it said, were fine for retirement accounts (IRAs) but not for short-term investing.

So one bond became a pile of single payments, each with its own market price.

Here is the puzzle. Once a bond is cut into separate payments, what rate should each payment be discounted at? And what does that say about the price of the whole bond?

The idea

A coupon bond is a bundle of IOUs. A 3-year bond with a 6% coupon is three promises: 6 in one year, 6 in two years, 106 in three. Cut apart, each promise is a zero-coupon bond: one payment, nothing before it.

A zero’s yield is the spot rate for its date, written Zt (Z for zero). Money due in 1 year and money due in 3 years are different goods, so their rates can differ. Spot rates plotted against maturity form the spot curve.

So instead of one rate for the whole bond (Day 15), discount each payment at the spot rate for its own date.

What $1 due at date t costs today is the discount factor, DFt. A 2-year strip paying 100 that sells for 90.70 has DF2 = 0.9070 and a spot rate of (100/90.70)1/2 − 1 = 5.00%.

Curves are often quoted as par yields instead: the coupon that prices a bond at exactly 100. Bootstrapping pulls spot rates out of them one maturity at a time. The 1-year bond gives Z1; with Z1 known, the 2-year bond gives Z2; and so on.

Picture a fruit basket priced by its pieces: if it cost less than its fruit sold one by one, you would buy baskets and sell fruit.

Why it works

Start from Day 2: PV = FV/(1 + r)N, and backwards, r = (FV/PV)1/N − 1.

  1. One payment, one rate. A zero paying 1 at date t costs DFt. Day 2 with FV = 1 and PV = DFt gives DFt = 1/(1 + Zt)t, and backwards Zt = (1/DFt)1/t − 1 (the same as DFt−1/t − 1). A payment of 6 at date t costs 6 × DFt.
  2. A bond is a bundle. Price = CF1 × DF1 + … + CFN × DFN. If the bond cost less than its pieces, dealers would buy it, strip it and sell the pieces; if more, buy the pieces and rebuild it. So the gap closes. Day 15 was the special case of one rate for every date.
  3. Par bonds give equations. A par bond costs 100 and its coupon c (per 100) equals its yield. The 1-year one pays only 100 + c1: 100 = (100 + c1) × DF1, so Z1 is the 1-year par yield.
  4. One new unknown at a time. 2-year: 100 = c2 × DF1 + (100 + c2) × DF2. DF1 is known, so DF2 = (100 − c2 × DF1)/(100 + c2). 3-year: DF3 = [100 − c3 × (DF1 + DF2)]/(100 + c3). Each step stands on the ones below it: the bootstrap.
  5. Prices add; rates don’t. That is why you solve for the DFs, each a plain sum with one unknown, and turn them into rates only at the end (step 1).
  6. Why Z2 beats the par yield on a rising curve. The first coupon is discounted at the lower Z1, so it takes a bit more of the 100. The final payment must be worth a bit less: a higher rate.
  7. Where the YTM fits. The YTM is the one rate that gives the step-2 price: a blend of the spot rates, pulled toward the date with the biggest payment, usually the last.

In plain words: a bond is a stack of IOUs with different dates. Each date has its own price, so its own rate; bootstrapping reads those rates off par bonds, shortest first.

PV = CF1/(1 + Z1)1 + CF2/(1 + Z2)2 + … + CFN/(1 + ZN)N

DFt = 1/(1 + Zt)t   ·   Zt = (1/DFt)1/t − 1

N-year par bond, coupon c:   DFN = [100 − c × (DF1 + … + DFN−1)] / (100 + c)

PV
the bond’s price, per 100 of par
CFt
the cash flow at date t: the coupon, plus 100 on the last date
Zt
the spot rate for date t: the annual yield on a zero-coupon bond paying then, as a decimal
DFt
the discount factor: what 1 due at date t costs today
t, N
the date in years, and the last date (the maturity)
c
the par bond’s coupon per 100, equal to its par yield (2.6 for 2.6%)
Memorize
  • Bond price using spot rates: PV = Σ CFt / (1 + Zt)t (each cash flow at its own spot rate)
  • Discount factor from a spot rate: DFt = 1 / (1 + Zt)t
  • Bootstrapping step for an N-year par bond with coupon c (per 100): 100 = c × DF1 + … + c × DFN−1 + (100 + c) × DFN → solve DFN, then ZN = DFN−1/N − 1
  • How the YTM relates to spot rates: the YTM is the single discount rate that gives the same price as discounting each cash flow at its own spot rate — a weighted average of the spot rates

Worked example

Problem. The 1-year spot rate is 4% and the 2-year par yield (annual coupons) is 5%. Bootstrap the 2-year spot rate.

By hand
  1. DF1 = 1/1.04 = 0.961538.
  2. A 2-year par bond with a 5% coupon pays 5, then 105, and is priced at 100: 100 = 5 × DF1 + 105 × DF2.
  3. 5 × DF1 = 4.807692, so DF2 = (100 − 4.807692)/105 = 95.192308/105 = 0.906593.
  4. Z2 = (1/0.906593)1/2 − 1 = 1.1030301/2 − 1 = 1.050252 − 1 = 5.0252%.
  5. Check: 5/1.04 + 105/1.0502522 = 100.00. The 2-year spot rate sits above the 5% par yield because the first coupon was discounted at the lower 1-year rate.

Back to the story

Each strip is a zero-coupon bond, so its price reveals the spot rate for its own date. That answers the first half: discount each payment at its own date’s spot rate.

From par yields of 2.0%, 2.6% and 3.0% you bootstrapped spot rates of 2.0000%, 2.6078% and 3.0183%. A 3-year 6% bond is just three strips: 5.8824 + 5.6989 + 96.9533 = 108.5346 per 100.

The second half is arbitrage. If the bond sold for less than its strips, dealers would buy it, strip it and sell the pieces. If it sold for more, they would buy the strips and put the bond back together. So the bond must equal the sum of its parts.

And its YTM, 2.9837%, is just the one rate that reproduces that price: a blend of the three spot rates, not the rate for any one date.

Exam traps
  • One rate for every cash flow when spot rates are given. Discount the walkthrough’s 6% bond at Z3 = 3.0183% throughout and you get 108.4311, not 108.5346. The early coupons deserve the lower early rates.
  • Par yield = spot rate. True only at 1 year. The 2-year par yield was 2.6% but Z2 = 2.6078%; using the par yields as spot rates prices the bond at 108.5871. On a rising curve, spot rates sit above par yields.
  • Rounding too early. Retype the displayed 0.9147 instead of recalling DF3 and Z3 comes out 3.0166%, not 3.0183%. Store each discount factor and recall it.
  • Forgetting the root. 1/DF3 − 1 = 9.3309% is the growth over all three years. The annual spot rate needs the 1/3 power: 3.0183%.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why should each cash flow of a bond be discounted at its own spot rate, and what is the YTM then?

Model answer

Each payment is a separate zero-coupon claim with its own market price, so its own rate; because dealers can strip a bond into its payments or rebuild it from them, the bond must cost exactly the sum of those pieces, each discounted at its own spot rate. The YTM is then just the single rate that gives that same price: a blend of the spot rates, not the rate for any one date.

A complete answer says: (1) each payment is a separate zero-coupon claim with its own price and rate; (2) no-arbitrage: stripping and reconstitution force the bond’s price to equal the sum of its strips’ values; (3) the YTM is the single rate that gives that same price, a blend of the spot rates, not a rate for any one date.
