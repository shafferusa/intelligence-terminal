Why

Sort, locate with (n + 1)y/100, and interpolate only when the location has a fractional part. Notice that the 18% month does not move the answer: percentiles ignore how extreme the extremes are. A standard deviation does not, and today’s story is about a month like that.

LTCM’s worst day

Long-Term Capital Management was a hedge fund in Greenwich, Connecticut, run by John Meriwether. Among its partners were two Nobel laureates, Robert Merton and Myron Scholes.

It spread leveraged bets across markets around the world, bets that looked unrelated to one another. By one later account, the correlations between its positions had historically been 10% or less, and its risk models allowed for up to 30%.

On 17 August 1998 Russia devalued the rouble and defaulted on its domestic debt. Investors everywhere fled to safety. By the same account, the correlations rose to about 70%.

On Friday 21 August, LTCM lost $553 million in a single day, about 15% of its capital. By Roger Lowenstein’s account in When Genius Failed, its models had judged a one-day loss above $35 million unlikely.

On 23 September, 14 banks and securities firms, convened by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, agreed to put in about $3.6 billion. In under four months the fund had lost about $4.6 billion.

Here is the puzzle. LTCM’s bets looked unrelated, so their risks should have largely cancelled. Why did diversification fail just when it was needed? And how much does a portfolio’s risk depend on the correlation?

The idea

Day 32 showed that a mix can be less risky than its parts. Today: which mix is the least risky, and how low can it go?

Take two strategies: strategy 1 with σ = 10%, strategy 2 with σ = 12%, correlation 0.1. Slide the weight on strategy 1, w, from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.2. The portfolio’s σ goes 12.00%, 10.00%, 8.58%, 8.05%, 8.58%, 10.00%.

Down, a bottom, back up: a U. The bottom, 8.05% at 60/40, is the minimum-variance portfolio. It is less risky than either strategy alone.

Now raise ρ to 0.7 and slide again: 12.00%, 11.09%, 10.40%, 9.97%, 9.83%, 10.00%. The U is nearly flat. The best mix reaches 9.83%, barely under 10%.

Correlation sets the depth. The lowest σp for this pair is 10% at ρ = +1 (just hold the safer one), 9.33% at 0.5, 7.68% at 0, 5.45% at −0.5, and 0% at −1.

With many assets, plot the least risky mix for every expected return (risk across, return up). The curve, shaped like a bullet on its side, is the minimum-variance frontier; its leftmost point is the global minimum-variance portfolio.

The part above that point is the efficient frontier. Every portfolio on the lower part has one straight above it with the same risk and more return, so nobody should hold it.

Why it works

Start from Day 32. Call the weight on asset 1 simply w, so asset 2 gets 1 − w:

σp2 = w2σ12 + (1 − w)2σ22 + 2w(1 − w)Cov

Step 1: multiply out. (1 − w)2 = 1 − 2w + w2 and 2w(1 − w) = 2w − 2w2. Collect the w2 pieces, the w pieces and the rest:

σp2 = (σ12 + σ22 − 2Cov)w2 − 2(σ22 − Cov)w + σ22

Name the brackets a = σ12 + σ22 − 2Cov and b = σ22 − Cov. Then σp2 = aw2 − 2bw + σ22.

Step 2: it is a U. a is the variance of the gap R1 − R2 (Day 32’s formula with weights +1 and −1). A variance is never negative, so the curve opens upward.

Step 3: two equal heights. At w = 0 the curve is σ22. Since aw2 − 2bw = w(aw − 2b), it is σ22 again at w = 2b/a.

Step 4: the bottom is halfway. This U is a parabola, the shape of y = x2, and a parabola is mirror-symmetric. So its lowest point sits midway between two equal heights: w1* = b/a = (σ22 − Cov)/(σ12 + σ22 − 2Cov).

Check it on the idea’s pair (Cov = 0.1 × 10 × 12 = 12): σp2 = 220w2 − 264w + 144. That is 144 at w = 0 and at w = 1.2, so the bottom is at 0.6. You saw the symmetry: 8.58% at both 0.4 and 0.8.

Step 5: read it. The top, σ22 − Cov, is asset 2’s risk that asset 1 does not share. The more of it, the more goes into asset 1.

Step 6: ρ = −1. Cov = −σ1σ2, so the top becomes σ2(σ1 + σ2) and a becomes (σ1 + σ2)2. Cancel: w1 = σ2/(σ1 + σ2) and w2 = σ1/(σ1 + σ2). Then w1σ1 = w2σ2, and Day 32’s σp = |w1σ1 − w2σ2| is 0.

Step 7: ρ = +1. σp is the weighted average of the SDs, a straight line, so the safest choice is all in the safer asset: mixing removes nothing. (The formula then asks for a weight above 1, a short sale, which comes later.)

In plain words: risk plotted against the mix is a U, the formula finds its bottom, and the lower the correlation, the deeper the U.

w1* = (σ22 − Cov12) / (σ12 + σ22 − 2Cov12),   w2* = 1 − w1*

Cov12 = ρ12σ1σ2

ρ12 = −1:   w1 = σ2 / (σ1 + σ2) and σp = 0

w1*, w2*
the weights of the minimum-variance portfolio: the share of the money in each asset
σ1, σ2
the two assets’ standard deviations (σ12, σ22 are their variances)
Cov12
the covariance of their returns, in the same units as the variances (%2 with percent, decimals with decimals)
ρ12
the correlation of their returns, from −1 to +1
σp
the portfolio’s standard deviation
Memorize
  • Two-asset minimum-variance weight on asset 1: w1* = (σ22 − Cov12) / (σ12 + σ22 − 2 Cov12)
  • Zero-risk weights when ρ = −1: w1 = σ2 / (σ1 + σ2)
  • Efficient frontier: the part of the minimum-variance frontier above (higher return than) the global minimum-variance portfolio
  • When does diversification reduce risk? Whenever ρ < +1; the lower ρ, the larger the reduction

Worked example

Problem. Asset 1 has σ = 20%, asset 2 has σ = 30%. Find the minimum-variance weight on asset 1 and the lowest σp when (a) ρ = 0 and (b) ρ = −1. Work in decimals.

By hand
  1. (a) ρ = 0, so Cov = 0 and the Cov terms drop out: w1* = σ22/(σ12 + σ22) = 0.09/(0.04 + 0.09) = 0.09/0.13 = 0.6923. So w2 = 0.3077: more in the safer asset, but not all of it.
  2. σp2 = 0.69232 × 0.04 + 0.30772 × 0.09 + 0 = 0.01917 + 0.00852 = 0.02769.
  3. σp = √0.02769 = 0.1664 = 16.64%, below both 20% and 30%.
  4. (b) ρ = −1: w1 = σ2/(σ1 + σ2) = 0.30/0.50 = 0.60, so w2 = 0.40.
  5. σp = |0.60 × 20% − 0.40 × 30%| = |12% − 12%| = 0%. Each asset’s swing is exactly offset by the other’s.

Back to the story

Diversification is a bet on correlations. At ρ = 0.1 the best mix of our two strategies cuts risk to 8.05%. At ρ = 0.7 it manages 9.83%, barely under the safer strategy’s 10%.

LTCM held far more than two bets. Take 100 equal bets, each with σ = 10%. Day 32’s recipe gives 100 own terms, each (1/100)2 × 102, plus 9,900 cross terms (each of the 4,950 pairs counted twice, like Day 32’s 2), each (1/100)2 × ρ × 102. Add them: σp = 10% × √(0.01 + 0.99ρ).

At ρ = 0.1 that is 3.30%. At 0.3, the models’ ceiling, 5.54%. At 0.7, 8.38%: two and a half times what a 0.1 correlation promised.

No number of bets gets below 10% × √ρ (8.37% at ρ = 0.7): the shared part of the risk never cancels. When correlations jump toward +1, as by that account they did after Russia’s default, the cushion shrinks just when it is needed.

Exam traps
  • Treating the global minimum-variance portfolio as the best portfolio for everyone. It is only the least risky one. Efficient portfolios above it offer more return for more risk, and which one suits an investor depends on risk aversion (Day 34).
  • Using ρ where the formula needs Cov. In the walkthrough, typing 0.1 for the covariance gives w1* = 0.5902, not 0.6000. Turn ρ into Cov = ρσ1σ2 first, in the same units as the variances: 12 with percent, 0.0012 with decimals, never mixed.
  • Assuming diversification still works when correlations jump toward +1. Moving ρ from 0.1 to 0.7 raised the lowest σp from 8.05% to 9.83%; at ρ = +1 mixing removes nothing.
  • Turning the ρ = −1 shortcut upside down. In w1 = σ2/(σ1 + σ2) the other asset’s σ is on top: the riskier asset gets the smaller weight.

2. Why. As the correlation between two risky assets falls from +1 to −1, what happens to the lowest portfolio standard deviation you can reach by mixing them?

  • The weights do adjust, but they cannot undo the correlation. For the 10%/12% pair the lowest σp goes from 10% at ρ = +1 to 7.68% at 0 and 0% at −1.

  • At ρ = −1 it reaches exactly zero. With w1 = σ2/(σ1 + σ2) the two swings cancel: the worked example’s 0.60 × 20% − 0.40 × 30% = 0.

  • Right. Only the cross term depends on ρ; the lower it is, the deeper the U.

Explanation

The own terms, w12σ12 + w22σ22, do not care about ρ. The cross term does: lower ρ, smaller (then negative) cross term, deeper U. For σ = 10% and 12% the bottom of the U is 10% at ρ = +1, 9.33% at 0.5, 7.68% at 0, 5.45% at −0.5 and 0% at −1.

3. Twist: the riskless mix. Asset 1 has σ = 12% and an expected return of 6%. Asset 2 has σ = 18% and an expected return of 10%. Their correlation is −1. Find the weights that make the portfolio riskless. What is that portfolio’s expected return? As a percent, to two decimals.

4. Exam style. Asset 1 has a standard deviation of 20% and asset 2 a standard deviation of 15%. The correlation between their returns is 0.25. The weight in asset 1 that minimizes the portfolio’s variance is closest to:

  • Right. Cov = 0.25 × 0.20 × 0.15 = 0.0075; w1* = (0.0225 − 0.0075)/(0.04 + 0.0225 − 0.015) = 0.0150/0.0475 = 0.3158.

  • That is the ρ = −1 shortcut, σ2/(σ1 + σ2) = 15/35. It holds only for perfectly negative correlation.

  • That ignores the covariance: σ22/(σ12 + σ22) = 0.0225/0.0625, right only if ρ = 0.

Explanation

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what is the efficient frontier, and why isn’t the global minimum-variance portfolio the best choice for everyone?

Model answer

The minimum-variance frontier holds the least risky portfolio for each expected return; the efficient frontier is its upper part, above the global minimum-variance portfolio, because every portfolio below has one straight above it with the same risk and more return. The global minimum-variance portfolio is only the least risky efficient portfolio: an investor willing to bear more risk earns more expected return further up, so the best point depends on how risk-averse the investor is.

A complete answer says: (1) the minimum-variance frontier gives the lowest σ for each expected return; (2) the efficient frontier is its upper part, above the global minimum-variance portfolio, and points below it are dominated (same risk, less return); (3) which efficient portfolio is best depends on the investor’s risk aversion (Day 34).
