From Day 27. 20% of the stocks a fund screens turn out to be winners. An analyst rates 60% of the winners “buy”, but also 25% of the rest. A stock gets a buy rating. The probability that it is a winner is closest to:

  • That is P(buy | winner), the conditional turned the wrong way round: the prosecutor’s fallacy.

  • Right. P(buy) = 0.60 × 0.20 + 0.25 × 0.80 = 0.12 + 0.20 = 0.32, so P(winner | buy) = 0.12 / 0.32 = 37.50%.

  • That is the joint probability P(buy and winner) = 0.60 × 0.20. It still has to be divided by P(buy) = 0.32.

Why

Posterior = P(information | event) × prior / P(information), and P(information) adds up every way a buy rating can happen. The rating lifts the chance from 20% to 37.5%, yet most buy-rated stocks still are not winners.

The game worth infinity that no one will buy

On 9 September 1713 Nicolaus Bernoulli sent a puzzle to the French mathematician Pierre Rémond de Montmort. His version used a die.

In 1738 his cousin, the Swiss mathematician Daniel Bernoulli, published the best-known version, with a coin, in a paper written in St Petersburg and printed there. The puzzle still carries the city’s name.

The game: Peter tosses a coin until it lands heads. He pays Paul 1 ducat if heads comes on the first toss, 2 if on the second, 4 if on the third, doubling each time.

What is Paul’s chance worth? Use Day 26’s expected value. Heads on toss 1: probability ½, pays 1, worth ½ ducat. Toss 2: ¼ × 2, another ½. Toss 3: ⅛ × 4, another ½. Every possible toss adds ½ ducat, and the possible tosses never run out. The expected payout is infinite.

Yet half the time Paul gets 1 ducat, and his chance of 1,024 ducats or more is 1 in 1,024. Daniel wrote that “any fairly reasonable man would sell his chance, with great pleasure, for twenty ducats.”

So the puzzle. If a gamble’s expected payoff is infinite, why won’t a sensible person pay much to play it? And how can you put a number on how much risk someone will accept?

The idea

Expected value is not the whole score. Offer most people a sure $50 or a coin flip for $0 or $100, and they take the $50. Both average $50; the flip adds risk, and risk costs something.

So the CFA curriculum scores a portfolio by its expected return minus a penalty for risk. That score is its utility: U = E(r) − ½Aσ2. Read U as a sure return: the T-bill rate that would leave you exactly as happy as the risky portfolio. A is your personal dial for how much you mind risk.

Second move: you are not stuck choosing between T-bills and the risky portfolio. You can split your money. Every split lands on one straight line, from the T-bill rate through the risky portfolio and beyond it if you borrow to buy more. That is the capital allocation line (CAL).

Each investor slides along the line to the point with the highest U. A cautious investor (high A) stops near T-bills; a bold one (low A) goes further out, even borrowing. When the risky portfolio is the whole market, the line is the capital market line (CML).

Why it works

Step 1: Bernoulli’s investor uses Day 12’s geometric mean. His rule scores an outcome by the log of what you end up with, and averaging logs is the same as taking a geometric mean (the log of a product is the sum of the logs). So a 50/50 bet of +20% or −20%, which averages 0%, is worth no more to him than a sure return of √(1.20 × 0.80) − 1 = −2.02%: a loss.

Step 2: where ½σ2 comes from. Day 12’s rough rule: geometric ≈ arithmetic − σ2/2. The bet’s σ is 0.20, so it is worth about 0 − ½ × 0.202 = −2.00%, close to the exact −2.02%. Risk costs ½σ2: double the swing, four times the cost.

Step 3: put a dial on it. U = E(r) − ½Aσ2. A = 1 is roughly Bernoulli’s investor; A = 4 minds risk four times as much; A = 0 ignores it (risk-neutral: the expected-value rule that broke in St Petersburg); A < 0 enjoys it (risk-seeking). T-bills have σ = 0, so their U is just Rf: U is a sure return. Points of equal U form an indifference curve: more σ needs more E, so it slopes up, more steeply for a larger A.

Step 4: the line is Day 32 with a riskless asset. Put w in the risky portfolio and 1 − w in T-bills. Return: E(Rp) = Rf + w[E(Ri) − Rf]. Risk: the T-bill’s σ of 0 kills its own term and the cross term, leaving σp = wσi. Both grow in step with w, so the mixes lie on a straight line; swap w for σp/σi and you have the CAL. Past w = 1 you are borrowing at Rf. With the market as the risky portfolio it is the CML: everyone holds the same market portfolio, in different amounts.

Step 5: the best point. On the line σp = wσi, so U = Rf + w[E(Ri) − Rf] − ½Aw2σi2. The gain grows with w, the penalty with w2, so the gain over T-bills rises like a thrown ball and falls back to zero at w = 2[E(Ri) − Rf]/(Aσi2). The arc is symmetric, so the top is halfway: w* = [E(Ri) − Rf]/(Aσi2). Today: zero at 0 and 0.875, peak at 0.4375.

Roy’s safety-first is Day 30’s z: the threshold RL lies SFRatio standard deviations below the mean, so the highest ratio gives the smallest chance of falling short (if returns are normal).

In plain words: risk carries a fee that grows with the square of the wobble, so you buy the risky portfolio only until one more slice no longer earns its fee.

U = E(r) − ½ × A × σ2

E(Rp) = Rf + [(E(Ri) − Rf) / σi] × σp, with σp = w × σi

w* = [E(Ri) − Rf] / (A × σi2)

the risk-aversion coefficient: above 0 risk-averse (larger = more averse), 0 risk-neutral, below 0 risk-seeking

E(Ri), σi

Memorize
  • Mean–variance utility: U = E(r) − ½ × A × σ2 (decimals)
  • Capital allocation line (and CML with the market portfolio): E(Rp) = Rf + [(E(Ri) − Rf) / σi] × σp
  • Optimal weight in the risky portfolio: w* = [E(Ri) − Rf] / (A × σi2)
  • Roy’s safety-first ratio: SFRatio = [E(Rp) − RL] / σp; choose the portfolio with the highest ratio
  • Risk attitude and the coefficient A: A > 0 risk-averse (larger A = more averse); A = 0 risk-neutral; A < 0 risk-seeking
  • What the utility number U means: the certainty-equivalent return, the sure (risk-free) return the investor would accept instead of the risky portfolio

Worked example

Problem. An investor with A = 3 chooses between portfolio X (E = 8%, σ = 10%) and portfolio Y (E = 12%, σ = 20%). Which does she prefer? Which would a risk-neutral investor prefer? Which wins on Roy’s safety-first criterion with a 3% threshold?

By hand
  1. UX = 0.08 − ½ × 3 × 0.102 = 0.08 − 0.015 = 0.065 (6.5%).
  2. UY = 0.12 − ½ × 3 × 0.202 = 0.12 − 0.06 = 0.060 (6.0%). She prefers X: Y’s extra 4 points of return cost her 4.5 points of penalty.
  3. Risk-neutral (A = 0): U = E(r), so Y (12%) wins; risk costs nothing. The two tie at A = 2.67; anyone less averse picks Y.
  4. Safety-first, RL = 3%: SFX = (8 − 3)/10 = 0.50; SFY = (12 − 3)/20 = 0.45. X has the higher ratio.
  5. If returns are normal, the chance of earning less than 3% is N(−0.50) = 30.85% for X and N(−0.45) = 32.64% for Y (Day 30’s z-table). X is the safer bet.

Answer. A = 3 → X (U = 6.5% vs 6.0%); A = 0 → Y; safety-first → X (0.50 vs 0.45).

Back to the story

Bernoulli’s answer: money’s usefulness grows more slowly than the amount. He used the logarithm, under which each doubling of what you have adds the same one step of usefulness.

That tames the infinite tail. Each extra toss doubles the prize but halves its chance, and a doubled prize adds at most one step, so the far tosses add almost nothing.

By our computation with his rule, the chance is worth a sure 2 ducats to a player with nothing else, about 3.04 to one who owns 10 ducats, 4.39 to one with 100 and 5.97 to one with 1,000. Nowhere near twenty.

Those ducat figures are sure-value equivalents, and so is U = E(r) − ½Aσ2: the same idea for portfolios. At A = 4 the risky portfolio alone scores 2.00%, worse than a 3% T-bill. But the choice is not all or nothing: 43.75% in it scores 4.53%, better than either. Take risk, but only as much as it pays for.

Exam traps
  • Percentages in the utility formula. 10 − ½ × 4 × 202 = −790 is nonsense; in decimals, 0.10 − ½ × 4 × 0.202 = 0.02 = 2%. Convert E and σ to decimals, then square σ.
  • Reading A backwards. A larger A means more risk-averse: a smaller w*, more T-bills, and the lower-σ portfolio wins (A = 3 chose X; A = 0 chose Y).
  • Thinking every investor on the CML holds a different risky portfolio. They all hold the same market portfolio and differ only in the mix with the risk-free asset: lending (w < 1) or borrowing (w > 1).
  • Safety-first with Rf in place of RL. (0.10 − 0.03) / 0.20 = 0.35 is a reward-to-risk ratio (the Sharpe ratio, Day 53); safety-first subtracts the threshold: (0.10 − 0.02) / 0.20 = 0.40. Then pick the highest ratio.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why do investors with very different risk aversion all hold the same risky portfolio on the CML, yet end up with different portfolios overall?

Model answer

The CML is the straight line from the risk-free rate through the market portfolio, the best risk–return trade-off on offer, so no one gains by holding a different risky portfolio. Each investor then picks a point on that line by mixing the market with the risk-free asset in the proportion w* = (E − Rf)/(Aσ2): a high-A investor lends (w < 1, partly in T-bills), a low-A investor borrows (w > 1).

A complete answer says: (1) the CAL/CML is a straight line from Rf through the optimal risky (market) portfolio, the best trade-off available; (2) each investor picks a point on it by mixing that portfolio with the risk-free asset, w* = (E − Rf)/(Aσ2); (3) high A means more lending (w < 1), low A means borrowing (w > 1).
