When the ETF said one thing and its bonds another

Thursday, 12 March 2020. The pandemic was tearing through markets, and the bond market was caught in a liquidity crunch.

That day, shares of the iShares iBoxx $ Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF, ticker LQD, closed 5.0% below the fund’s net asset value (NAV): what the fund said the bonds inside it were worth. In 2018 and 2019 it had never closed more than 1% away.

So a seller got about 95 cents for each dollar the fund said its bonds were worth.

Where did the NAV come from? Many of the fund’s bonds had not traded. Pricing services estimated “evaluated” prices for them, largely from the yields of comparable bonds that had.

The gap shrank to 0.19% the next day. By 19 March it was back near 5%. On 23 March the Federal Reserve said it would buy corporate bonds and bond ETFs, and by Bloomberg’s account, on 25 March LQD stood at a premium of about 5%: above its NAV.

Two prices for the same bonds, 5% apart. If a bond hasn’t traded for days, what is it worth? And how do you price it from bonds that did trade?

The idea

A yield spread is the extra yield a bond pays over a benchmark: a safe yield for the same length of time. It pays for what the benchmark doesn’t carry: default risk, being hard to sell.

The spreads differ by benchmark:

  • G-spread: over a government bond of the same maturity (interpolated when none matches).
  • I-spread: over the swap rate of the same maturity and currency (the fixed rate paid in an interest rate swap for a floating one).
  • Z-spread: one constant added to every spot rate on the government curve (Day 36) so that the discounted cash flows equal the price.
  • Option-adjusted spread (OAS): the Z-spread minus the value of an embedded option, such as the issuer’s right to call.

Matrix pricing values a bond that rarely trades. Think of a house appraiser: no one has bought your house this year, so she looks at recent sales of similar houses nearby. For a bond: take the yields of comparable bonds (same credit rating, nearby maturities, similar coupons), interpolate to your bond’s maturity, and discount its cash flows at that yield.

Floating-rate notes (FRNs) have spreads too. The coupon resets each period to a market reference rate (MRR, such as SOFR) plus a fixed quoted margin. The market’s required return is MRR plus the discount margin.

Why it works

You know both directions already: Day 15 turns a yield into a price, Day 16 turns a price into a yield (the YTM).

  1. Split the yield. Bond yield = benchmark yield + spread. The benchmark (a government bond of the same maturity) pays for waiting; the spread pays for the rest.
  2. Match the maturity. Yields differ by maturity (Days 36–37), so a 7-year bond is set against a 7-year government yield. None trades? Draw a straight line between the neighbours. From 5 years (3.90%) to 10 years (4.30%) the yield climbs 0.40 points in 5 years: 0.08 a year. Seven years is 2 years along: 3.90 + 2 × 0.08 = 4.06%. That is the formula below: start at the shorter yield, add the share of the gap you have walked.
  3. Subtract. Bond YTM − that government yield = the G-spread. Subtract the swap rate instead: the I-spread.
  4. One spread, every date. A YTM is one rate for all dates; Day 36 discounts each cash flow at its own spot rate, zt. The Z-spread adds one constant, Z, to every spot rate: the Z that makes the PV equal the price. Spot rates 3% and 4%, Z = 1%: a 2-year 5% bond is worth 5/1.04 + 105/1.052 = 100.05 (at the spot rates alone, 101.93).
  5. Take the option out. A callable bond pays extra because the issuer can repay early when rates fall. That extra sits inside the Z-spread. OAS = Z-spread − option value: what is left pays for credit and liquidity.
  6. Matrix pricing runs step 1 backwards. An untraded bond has no price, so no yield. Borrow one: interpolate comparable bonds’ yields to its maturity, then discount its cash flows at that yield (Day 15).
  7. FRNs. Same math, per period, assuming the MRR stays put: coupon = (MRR + QM)/m × 100, discount rate = (MRR + DM)/m. If DM = QM, coupon rate = discount rate, so the note is worth par (Day 15). If DM > QM, it is worth less.

In plain words: a spread is the extra a bond pays over a safe bond of the same length. Matrix pricing guesses a quiet bond’s yield from its busier neighbours, then turns that yield into a price.

Memorize
  • G-spread = YTM of the bond − YTM of the (interpolated) government bond of the same maturity
  • Z-spread: the constant Z that makes PV = Σ CFt / (1 + zt + Z)t equal the bond’s price (zt = the benchmark spot rate for date t — Day 36’s Zt)
  • Option-adjusted spread: OAS = Z-spread − option value (in bps): callable OAS < Z-spread
  • Linear interpolation of a yield: y = yshort + (T − Tshort)/(Tlong − Tshort) × (ylong − yshort)
  • FRN inputs per period (P/Y = 1): Coupon = (MRR + quoted margin)/m × 100; discount rate = (MRR + discount margin)/m
  • I-spread = YTM of the bond − swap rate of the same maturity (in the same currency)

Worked example

Problem. A 7-year corporate bond yields 5.35%. Government bonds yield 3.90% at 5 years and 4.30% at 10 years; the 7-year swap rate is 4.25%. Find the G-spread and the I-spread.

By hand
  1. No 7-year government bond, so interpolate. Seven years is (7 − 5)/(10 − 5) = 0.4 of the way from 5 to 10.
  2. The gap: 4.30 − 3.90 = 0.40 points. Walk 0.4 of it: 0.4 × 0.40 = 0.16.
  3. 7-year government yield = 3.90 + 0.16 = 4.06%.
  4. G-spread = 5.35 − 4.06 = 1.29 points = 129 bp (1 bp = 0.01%).
  5. I-spread = 5.35 − 4.25 = 1.10 points = 110 bp.
  6. The I-spread is smaller here because the swap rate sits 19 bp above the government yield. That is the usual pattern, not a law: long-dated US swap rates have sat below Treasury yields in recent years.

Back to the story

A pricing service valuing LQD’s untraded bonds did, in essence, what you just did: borrow the yields of comparable bonds, line them up by maturity, and discount.

That works when the comparables are fresh. In March 2020 credit spreads were widening fast. LQD’s shares traded live on the exchange, while evaluated prices lagged.

Size the gap with step 6. A 10-year 4% bond valued at par yields 4%. At 95 it yields 4.6361%. A 5% discount meant buyers wanted about 64 bp more spread than the evaluated price assumed.

Which price was right? Neither exactly: the NAV was an estimate, the ETF price a live trade. Matrix pricing is only as fresh as its comparables. When the Fed stepped in, the gap flipped to a premium.

Exam traps
  • An unmatched benchmark. A 7-year bond needs a 7-year government yield. In the worked example, subtracting the 5-year 3.90% gives 145 bp instead of 129 bp. Interpolate first.
  • Z-spread vs G-spread. The G-spread is one subtraction of two YTMs. The Z-spread is the constant added to every spot rate that reproduces the price. Unless the curve is flat they differ, and the OAS starts from the Z-spread.
  • An FRN at the wrong margin. The quoted margin sets the coupon; the discount margin sets the rate you discount at. If your FRN comes out at exactly 100.00 when the margins differ, you discounted at the quoted margin.
  • Points vs basis points. 4.40% − 3.20% = 1.20 percentage points = 120 bp, because 1 bp = 0.01%.

2. Why. A callable corporate bond has a Z-spread of 180 bp and an option-adjusted spread of 130 bp. What is the 50 bp difference?

  • Right: OAS = Z-spread − option value, so 180 − 130 = 50 bp is what the investor is paid for having sold the issuer the call.

  • No: credit and liquidity risk stay inside the OAS. Removing the option is what leaves the credit spread behind.

  • No: that is the G-spread, a different measure. It compares two YTMs and says nothing about the option.

Explanation

The Z-spread pays for everything over the government spot curve, including the call the investor has handed the issuer: the issuer will repay early when rates fall, just when the bond is worth most. The OAS strips that option out. For a callable bond OAS < Z-spread; for a putable bond, where the option belongs to the investor, OAS > Z-spread.

4. Exam style. A 6-year corporate bond yields 5.10%. Government bonds yield 3.60% at 5 years and 4.10% at 10 years. The bond’s G-spread is closest to:

  • Used the 5-year 3.60% without interpolating: 5.10 − 3.60. The benchmark’s maturity does not match the bond’s.

  • Used the simple average of the two yields, 3.85%. That is the 7.5-year point, not the 6-year one.

  • Right: the interpolated 6-year government yield is 3.70%, and 5.10 − 3.70 = 1.40 points = 140 bp.

Explanation

6 years is (6 − 5)/(10 − 5) = 0.2 of the way from 5 to 10. The gap is 4.10 − 3.60 = 0.50, and 0.2 × 0.50 = 0.10, so the 6-year government yield is 3.60 + 0.10 = 3.70%. G-spread = 5.10 − 3.70 = 140 bp.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: explain matrix pricing to someone who has never heard of it.

Model answer

Matrix pricing values a bond that trades rarely or not at all: you take the yields of comparable bonds that do trade (same credit quality, similar maturity and coupon), interpolate them to your bond’s maturity (or add a comparable spread to a benchmark yield), and discount your bond’s cash flows at that yield to get its price.

A complete answer says: (1) it is for a bond that trades rarely or not at all; (2) the yield comes from comparable bonds (same credit quality, similar maturity and coupon), interpolated to the bond’s maturity or built as benchmark plus spread; (3) the bond’s cash flows are discounted at that yield to give its price.
