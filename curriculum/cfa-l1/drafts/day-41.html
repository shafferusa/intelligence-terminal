When gilts broke in 2022, duration’s straight line overstated the loss. Convexity bends it back

__DATELINE__ · 20 min

Duration lays a straight line against a curve; add ½ × convexity × (ΔYield)2 and the estimate bends back toward the truth: smaller losses, bigger gains, and the bigger the move, the bigger the fix.

Solution

(99.95 − 94.93)/(2 × 0.003 × 97.40) = 5.02/0.5844 = 8.59.

From Day 34. An investor with a risk-aversion coefficient A = 3 looks at a portfolio with an expected return of 10% and a standard deviation of 18%. Its mean–variance utility is closest to:

  • Right: U = 0.10 − ½ × 3 × 0.182 = 0.10 − 0.0486 = 0.0514.

  • The ½ was dropped: 0.10 − 3 × 0.0324 = 0.0028.

  • σ was not squared: 0.10 − ½ × 3 × 0.18 = −0.17. Utility uses the variance.

U = E(r) − ½ × A × σ2, in decimals. BA II Plus: .1 − .5 × 3 × .18 x² = → 0.0514. TI-84 Plus: the same keys with ENTER → .0514.

Keep an eye on that ½. Today’s convexity term has one too, and you will see exactly why.

The week that broke the gilt market

Friday 23 September 2022. The UK government’s “mini-budget” of tax cuts set off a sell-off in long-dated government bonds, known as gilts.

On the eve of the statement the 30-year gilt yielded between about 3.5% and 3.7% (sources differ). In three trading days the yield rose about 1.2 percentage points. Early on Wednesday 28 September it touched about 5.1%, its highest since 2002.

Pension funds using liability-driven investment (LDI) strategies faced collateral calls. They had to sell gilts into the falling market, which pushed yields higher still.

That day the Bank of England announced emergency purchases of long-dated gilts. The 30-year yield fell more than a full percentage point, closing near 3.9% by Reuters’ figures.

Now take a stylized long gilt, an illustration rather than a particular bond: 30 years to maturity, a 0.5% annual coupon. Start it at 3.7%, the top of that eve-of-statement range. Its modified duration is about 25.4, so duration says the 1.4-point jump to 5.1% costs about 25.4 × 1.4 = 35.6% of its value.

Is that right? And if not, does the straight line overstate the damage or understate it?

The idea

Duration is a ruler laid against a curve. It reads the price’s slope at today’s yield and assumes the slope never changes. For small moves that is fine. For big ones it is not.

Picture a playground slide: steep at the top, flattening out toward the bottom. Price against yield has that shape. As yields rise, the price falls more and more slowly. As yields fall, it climbs faster and faster. That bend is convexity.

The ruler touches the curve at one point and lies below it everywhere else. So duration alone overstates losses and understates gains.

The fix grows with the square of the move. For the gilt it is 1.79 percentage points for a 70 bp move, 7.17 for 140 bp, 28.67 for 280 bp. Double the move, four times the fix. This is the piece Day 39 was missing, when duration alone had Austria’s century bond losing an impossible 171%.

More convexity comes from the same things that raise duration: longer maturity, a lower coupon, a lower yield. The gilt’s convexity, 731, is more than nine times the worked example’s 10-year 4% bond (78).

Why it works

Take Day 40’s three prices for the gilt (3.7%, steps of 10 bp) and look at each half:

  1. Gain versus loss. A 10 bp fall adds PV− − PV0 = 1.0994. A 10 bp rise takes away PV0 − PV+ = 1.0683. On a straight line they would match. The gain is bigger by 0.0311: the curve is steeper on the left.
  2. Duration uses the sum. Day 40’s numerator, PV− − PV+, is gain + loss: the average slope, the ruler.
  3. Convexity uses the difference. Gain − loss = (PV− − PV0) − (PV0 − PV+) = PV− + PV+ − 2PV0. It is how much the slope changed from the left half to the right half.
  4. Make it a rate. A slope is a price change ÷ ΔYield, so the change in slope is (PV− + PV+ − 2PV0)/ΔYield. That happened over one step of ΔYield: divide again, and by PV0 for a share of the price. That is ApproxCon.
  5. A car first. A car at speed v, speeding up steadily by a, goes from v to v + a·t. Its average speed is v + ½a·t, so the distance is v·t + ½a·t2. The ½: the extra speed is all there only at the end, so on average it is half.
  6. Now the bond. Measured as a share of PV0, its slope starts at −AnnModDur and changes steadily by AnnConvexity per unit of yield. Over the move the average slope is −AnnModDur + ½ × AnnConvexity × ΔYield. Times the move: %ΔPV ≈ −AnnModDur × ΔYield + ½ × AnnConvexity × (ΔYield)2.
  7. Always plus. (ΔYield)2 is positive whether yields rise or fall, and an option-free bond’s convexity is positive. So the fix always adds: a smaller loss, a bigger gain.
  8. In money. Multiply by the position’s value: MoneyCon = AnnConvexity × PVfull.

Why long bonds? Duration weighted each payment by its time t (Day 39); convexity weights it by roughly t2. Far payments count hugely, and spreading payments out raises convexity even at the same duration.

In plain words: duration guesses with a ruler, convexity adds back the bend, and the bend matters more the further you go.

ApproxCon = (PV− + PV+ − 2 × PV0) / [(ΔYield)2 × PV0]

%ΔPV ≈ −AnnModDur × ΔYield + ½ × AnnConvexity × (ΔYield)2

ΔPV ≈ −MoneyDur × ΔYield + ½ × MoneyCon × (ΔYield)2

PV0
the bond’s (full) price at today’s yield
PV−, PV+
its price with the yield a small step lower, and a small step higher
ΔYield
in ApproxCon, that small step; in the estimate, the actual change in the annual yield. Always a decimal: 10 bp = 0.001
AnnModDur
annual modified duration (Day 39); ApproxModDur estimates it (Day 40)
AnnConvexity
annual convexity; ApproxCon estimates it, already annual when the steps are in annual yield
MoneyDur
AnnModDur × PVfull of the position
MoneyCon
money convexity, AnnConvexity × PVfull of the position
Memorize
  • Approximate convexity: ApproxCon = (PV− + PV+ − 2 × PV0) / [(ΔYield)2 × PV0]
  • Price change with duration and convexity: %ΔPV ≈ −AnnModDur × ΔYield + ½ × AnnConvexity × (ΔYield)2
  • Money convexity and the currency price change: MoneyCon = AnnConvexity × PVfull; ΔPV ≈ −MoneyDur × ΔYield + ½ × MoneyCon × (ΔYield)2
  • Sign of the convexity adjustment for an option-free bond: Always positive (adds to the estimate for yield rises and falls)
  • What gives a bond more convexity? Longer maturity, lower coupon, lower yield, and (for the same duration) more dispersed cash flows

Worked example

Problem. A 10-year bond with a 4% annual coupon yields 5%; its price is 92.2783 per 100. Repriced at 4.75% it is 94.1377; at 5.25%, 90.4639. Estimate the % price change for a 150 bp rise with duration alone and with convexity, and compare with the exact change (the price at 6.5% is 82.0279).

By hand
  1. The repricing step is 25 bp: ΔYield = 0.0025.
  2. ApproxModDur = (94.1377 − 90.4639)/(2 × 0.0025 × 92.2783) = 3.6738/0.4613915 = 7.9624.
  3. ApproxCon = (94.1377 + 90.4639 − 184.5566)/(0.00000625 × 92.2783) = 0.0450/0.00057674 = 78.0248.
  4. Duration term = −7.9624 × 0.015 = −11.9437%.
  5. Convexity term = ½ × 78.0248 × 0.0152 = ½ × 78.0248 × 0.000225 = +0.8778%.
  6. Estimate = −11.9437% + 0.8778% = −11.0659%.
  7. Exact = 82.0279/92.2783 − 1 = −11.1081%. Duration alone missed by 0.84 of a point; with convexity, by 0.04.
  8. One warning: unrounded prices give ApproxCon = 78.30, not 78.02. The numerator is a tiny difference of big numbers, so rounding moves it. That is why the walkthrough stores every price.

Back to the story

Repriced at 5.1%, the stylized gilt is worth 30.0857 per 100, against 42.5929 at 3.7%. The true fall is 29.36%, not 35.63%. Duration overstated the loss by about 6.3 percentage points, because the price curve bends up and away from its straight line.

Add ½ × 731.34 × 0.0142 = +7.17% and the estimate becomes −28.46%, within about 0.9 of a point of the truth.

Run the move in reverse, yields down 1.4 points, and the bond gains 43.92%, more than duration’s +35.63% (with convexity, +42.79%). Smaller losses, bigger gains: that is positive convexity.

It softened the blow; it did not stop it. Even the true figure is a loss of almost 30% on a government bond.

Exam traps
  • Forgetting the ½. Without it the walkthrough adds 14.33 instead of 7.17 and lands on −21.29%, not −28.46%.
  • A minus sign on the convexity term because yields rose. (ΔYield)2 is positive either way, so for an option-free bond the term always adds. Subtracting it gives −42.79%, wrong.
  • ΔYield in percent. Use 0.014, not 1.4. Squaring 1.4 makes the term 10,000 times too big: ½ × 731.34 × 1.42 = 716.71, nonsense.
  • Rounding the prices before ApproxCon. Its numerator is tiny (0.0311 here). Retyped 4-decimal prices give 732.5 instead of 731.3; store them.

2. Why. For an option-free bond, why does the convexity term add to the estimate whether yields rise or fall?

  • The sign trap. ΔYield is squared, so its sign disappears: +140 bp and −140 bp both give the gilt +7.17 points.

  • Right: the truth sits above the ruler for a rise and for a fall, so the fix is upward both times.

  • That is reinvestment risk (Day 39), and it does not even help both ways: lower yields hurt reinvestment. Convexity is about the shape of the price curve today.

Explanation

The gilt, ±140 bp. Yields up: duration says −35.63%, the truth is −29.36%, higher. Yields down: duration says +35.63%, the truth is +43.92%, higher again. Both times the curve is above the line, and the convexity term, +7.17 points, pushes the estimate up toward it.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences, in your own words: after a big yield move, why does duration alone overstate the loss and understate the gain for an option-free bond?

Model answer

Duration is a straight line, the tangent to the price–yield curve at today’s yield, but the curve is convex: it bends upward and sits above that line on both sides. So after any move the true price is higher than duration says, a smaller loss if yields rose and a bigger gain if they fell, and the gap grows with the square of the move, which the convexity term ½ × Convexity × (ΔYield)2 adds back.

A complete answer says: (1) the price–yield curve is convex; (2) duration is the straight tangent line, so the curve lies above it whether yields rise or fall; (3) the error grows with (ΔYield)2, and the convexity term adds it back.
