“Beta is dead”? Fama and French found a flat slope in 1992; Fischer Black said not so fast

__DATELINE__ · 20 min

A fitted slope is never exactly 0 or exactly 1. Count how many of its own standard errors it sits from the value you are testing, and the t-table tells you whether the gap is real or noise.

Solution

b̂1 = 0.6 × 5.0 / 4.0 = 0.75. b̂0 = Ȳ − b̂1X̄ = 1.4 − 0.75 × 0.8 = 1.4 − 0.60 = 0.80%.

The usual slips: sX / sY instead of sY / sX (slope 0.48, intercept 1.02%), or leaving out r (slope 1.25, intercept 0.40%). Keep this line in mind: today the slope is the stock’s beta and the intercept its alpha.

From Day 43. 36 monthly returns have a mean of 1.0% and a sample standard deviation of 4.2%. With t = 2.030 (35 df, 2.5% in each tail), the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval for the mean monthly return is closest to:

  • That divides s by n (4.2 / 36 = 0.117) instead of by √n. The standard error shrinks with the square root of the sample size.

  • Right: SE = 4.2 / √36 = 0.70, and 1.0 − 2.030 × 0.70 = 1.0 − 1.421 = −0.421%.

Why

Interval = X̄ ± t × s / √n = 1.0 ± 2.030 × 0.70 = 1.0 ± 1.421: from −0.42% to 2.42%. Today does the same with a slope: estimate ± t × its standard error.

“Beta is dead” – or just hard to measure?

In June 1992 Eugene Fama and Kenneth French published “The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns” in the Journal of Finance. They studied US stocks from 1963 to 1990.

The theory under test was simple. A stock that swings more with the market has a higher beta, and it should earn more on average to pay for that risk.

Their abstract delivered the blow. Once beta was allowed to vary for reasons unrelated to size, “the relation between market beta and average return is flat, even when beta is the only explanatory variable.” Size and book-to-market, meanwhile, explained a great deal.

Fama’s view had reached the press months earlier. As Fischer Black later cited it: “Eugene Fama says (according to Eric Berg of The New York Times, February 18, 1992) beta as the sole variable explaining returns on stocks is dead.”

Black, co-creator of the Black-Scholes option model, answered in 1993 in the Journal of Portfolio Management. His paper, “Beta and Return”, opens: “Announcements of the death of beta seem premature.”

A fitted slope is never exactly 0, or exactly 1. So how do you decide whether beta really explains a stock’s returns? And if a test cannot find the relation, does that prove it is not there?

The idea

Day 47 fitted the line. Day 48 measured how well it fits. Today asks whether its slope is real.

Fit a line through six months of noisy returns and the slope comes out at, say, 1.27. Take six other months and you get a different number. The slope is an estimate, and like a sample mean (Day 43) it wobbles from sample to sample. Its typical wobble is its standard error, sb̂1.

So test it the Day 44 way: how many standard errors does b̂1 sit from the value you are testing? Beyond the t-table’s critical value (n − 2 df), noise is a poor explanation: reject.

For a stock, two values matter:

  • B1 = 0: does the market move this stock at all?
  • B1 = 1: does it move one-for-one with the market, or is it more (or less) risky than average?

Beta by regression. Put the stock’s returns as Y and the market’s as X. The slope is the stock’s beta: Cov(Ri, Rm) / Var(Rm), Day 47’s slope. With excess returns (above the risk-free rate), the intercept is its alpha: the return the market does not explain. Alpha is tested the same way.

Why it works

The recipe is Day 44’s. Test statistic = (estimate − hypothesized value) / standard error. Here: t = (b̂1 − B1) / sb̂1. Only sb̂1 is new.

Where the wobble comes from. Day 47: b̂1 = Σxy / Σx2 (x and y measured from the means). Write each Y as the true line plus an error ε, and the true slope b1 drops out: b̂1 = b1 + Σxε / Σx2. The miss is a weighted sum of the errors, weights x / Σx2.

Size of the wobble. The errors are independent, each with variance σ2 (Day 47’s assumptions), so Day 32’s portfolio variance with zero covariances applies: σ2 × Σ(weight2) = σ2 × Σx2 / (Σx2)2 = σ2 / Σx2. Take the root and put Day 48’s SEE in for the unknown σ: sb̂1 = SEE / √Σ(X − X̄)2.

Noisier returns (bigger SEE), shakier slope. More months, or a market that moves more (bigger Σx2), steadier slope.

Why t, with n − 2. Estimating σ turns z into Student’s t (Day 43), and the SEE has n − 2 df (Day 48).

The correlation shortcut. Put in b̂1 = r sY / sX (Day 47), SSE = (1 − r2)SST (Day 48), SST = (n − 1)sY2 and Σx2 = (n − 1)sX2: sb̂1 = (sY / sX)√(1 − r2) / √(n − 2). Divide, and sY / sX cancels: b̂1 / sb̂1 = r√(n − 2) / √(1 − r2). Testing slope = 0 is testing correlation = 0.

F = t2. Day 48’s SSR = b̂12Σx2, so F = SSR / MSE = b̂12Σx2 / SEE2 = (b̂1 / sb̂1)2.

In plain words: measure the slope’s distance from the value you are testing in units of its own wobble. Far enough, and luck is a poor explanation.

Memorize
  • t-statistic for a regression slope: t = (b̂1 − B1) / sb̂1, df = n − 2
  • t-statistic for a correlation (simple regression): t = r √(n − 2) / √(1 − r2), df = n − 2
  • Beta from data: β = Cov(Ri, Rm) / Var(Rm) = slope of Ri regressed on Rm
  • Link between F and t in simple regression: F = t2 (slope test of H0: b1 = 0)
  • Test of the intercept (alpha): t = (b̂0 − B0) / sb̂0, df = n − 2
  • Standard error of the slope: sb̂1 = SEE / √Σ(Xi − X̄)2

Worked example

Problem. A stock’s 60 monthly excess returns are regressed on the market’s. Slope 1.25, standard error 0.10; intercept 0.20%, standard error 0.15%. At 5% (two-tailed, t0.025, 58 = 2.002): is beta different from 1? From 0? Is alpha different from 0? What are F and the correlation?

By hand
  1. df = 60 − 2 = 58. Reject when |t| > 2.002.
  2. β = 1? t = (1.25 − 1) / 0.10 = 2.50 > 2.002: reject. Beta is above 1; the stock amplifies the market.
  3. β = 0? t = 1.25 / 0.10 = 12.5: reject, by a mile. The market clearly moves this stock.
  4. α = 0? t = 0.20 / 0.15 = 1.33 < 2.002: fail to reject. No evidence of an abnormal return, which is not proof that alpha is zero.
  5. F = t2 for H0: b1 = 0: 12.52 = 156.25.
  6. Correlation: square t = r√58 / √(1 − r2) and solve for r: r = t / √(t2 + n − 2) = 12.5 / √(156.25 + 58) = 12.5 / 14.6373 = 0.8540. Check: 0.8540 × √58 / √(1 − 0.85402) = 12.50.

Back to the story

Today’s six months give a beta of 1.27 with a standard error of 0.22. Against β = 0, t = 5.80, well past the critical 2.776: the market clearly moves this stock. Against β = 1, t = 1.22: six months cannot tell this stock’s beta from 1. The 95% interval runs from 0.66 to 1.87.

Fama and French’s slope was a different one: average return across stocks, set against beta. “Flat” is a verdict about a slope. But the question is today’s: is a fitted slope far enough from zero, in standard errors, to be more than noise?

The same logic sat under the quarrel. Returns are so noisy that slopes come with wide standard errors, and “fail to reject” is not “proved zero”. Black went further, arguing that Fama and French had misinterpreted their own data.

Exam traps
  • Testing β = 1 with 0 in the numerator. b / sb tests β = 0. In the walkthrough that gives 5.80 (reject) where the right t, (1.2661 − 1) / 0.2185 = 1.22, fails to reject: opposite verdicts.
  • n − 1 degrees of freedom. A regression estimates two coefficients, so df = n − 2. With six months the critical t is 2.776 (4 df), not 2.571 (5 df): a t of 2.65 would reject with the wrong df and not with the right one.
  • The stock as X, the market as Y. Beta is the stock regressed on the market. Swap them and the walkthrough’s “beta” becomes 0.7057 (r2 / b), and its test of β = 1 gives t = −2.42 instead of 1.22. The test of 0 survives the swap (5.7953 both ways, because r is symmetric), which is why the slip is easy to miss.
  • “Not significant” read as “equal”. t = 1.22 does not show beta is 1. It shows six months cannot rule 1 out, nor 0.7, nor 1.8.

2. Why. Why does the t-test of a regression slope use n − 2 degrees of freedom?

  • Right. sb̂1 = SEE / √Σ(X − X̄)2, and SEE2 = SSE / (n − 2). The t inherits the SEE’s degrees of freedom.

  • The n − 1 habit. A sample mean estimates one number, X̄, so it loses one df. A regression line estimates two, intercept and slope, so the residuals lose two: n − 2 from the start, not n − 1 with an add-on.

  • The hypothesized value changes only the numerator, b̂1 − B1. The standard error, and with it the df, is the same whatever value you test.

Explanation

Estimating σ with the SEE is what makes the statistic a t rather than a z (Day 43), and the t has as many degrees of freedom as that estimate: the residuals must add to zero and be uncorrelated with X, two conditions, so only n − 2 of them are free (Day 48). In the walkthrough, 6 − 2 = 4 df and a critical value of 2.776.

4. Exam style. An analyst regresses 36 monthly excess returns of a stock on the market’s excess returns. The estimated beta is 1.18 with a standard error of 0.08. The t-statistic for testing whether beta differs from 1 is closest to:

  • That is 1.18 / 0.08: 0 in the numerator, which tests β = 0. To test β = 1, subtract 1 first.

  • That multiplies the standard error by √36 = 6, as if 0.08 were a standard deviation. The 0.08 already is the slope’s standard error: use it as it is.

  • Right: (1.18 − 1) / 0.08 = 0.18 / 0.08 = 2.25.

Explanation

t = (b̂1 − B1) / sb̂1 = (1.18 − 1) / 0.08 = 2.25, with 36 − 2 = 34 df. The 5% two-tailed critical value is about 2.032, so beta is significantly above 1 (p = 0.031). The 95% interval, 1.18 ± 2.032 × 0.08 = 1.02 to 1.34, excludes 1: the same verdict.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what does a slope t-test tell you about a stock’s beta, and why is “not significant” different from “beta is zero”?

Model answer

The test counts how many standard errors the estimated beta sits from the value you are testing, t = (b̂1 − B1) / sb̂1 with n − 2 df; if |t| beats the critical value, that value is rejected. Failing to reject only means the data cannot rule the value out: with short or noisy samples the standard error is wide and the test has little power, so a real beta can hide in the noise (a Type II error), and the confidence interval shows how wide the range still plausible is.

A complete answer says: (1) t = (estimate − hypothesized value) / standard error, with n − 2 df; (2) fail to reject means “cannot rule out”, not “proved”: possible Type II error, low power with noisy or short samples; (3) a confidence interval shows the range of betas still plausible.
