Warm-up · from earlier lessons

Two quick ones, from Day 13 and Day 5, so they stick. First try counts.

From Day 13. An account is worth $50,000 on 1 January. On 30 June it is worth $56,000, and the client then deposits $24,000. On 31 December it is worth $76,000. The account’s time-weighted return for the year is closest to:

  • That adds the two half-year returns, +12% and −5%. Returns compound: multiply the growth factors instead.

  • That is the dollar gain, (76,000 − 24,000 − 50,000) = 2,000, over the starting 50,000. It never cuts the year at the deposit, so the client’s timing leaks into the result.

  • Right. Cut at the deposit: 56,000 / 50,000 = 1.12, then 76,000 / 80,000 = 0.95, and 1.12 × 0.95 − 1 = 6.40%.

Why

A time-weighted return breaks the year at every external cash flow and chain-links the pieces: TWR = (1 + HPR1)(1 + HPR2) − 1. The second half starts at 56,000 + 24,000 = 80,000.

BA II Plus: 56000 ÷ 50000 × 76000 ÷ 80000 = → 1.0640. TI-84 Plus: the same keys, with ENTER for = → 1.0640.

The money-weighted return is lower, about 3.23% a year: the client’s deposit landed just before the losing half.

From Day 5. You save $250 at the end of every month for 20 years. The account earns 6% a year, stated, compounded monthly. How much is in it at the end? In dollars, to the nearest cent.

dollars
Hint Monthly payments, so N = 20 × 12 = 240 and I/Y = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5. PV = 0, PMT = 250 (paid in, so negative), END mode; compute FV.
Solution

BA II Plus (no BGN on the display): 2ND CLR TVM 240 N 6 ÷ 12 = I/Y 0 PV 250 +|− PMT CPT FV → FV = 115,510.2238.

TI-84 Plus: APPS 1:Finance 1:TVM Solver: N=240, I%=6/12, PV=0, PMT=−250, FV=0, P/Y=1, C/Y=1, PMT: END; cursor on FV=, then ALPHA ENTER → FV=115510.2238.

So $115,510.22. You paid in 240 × 250 = $60,000; the other $55,510.22 is interest.

What this week was about

Every formula this week comes from two moves. Move one: discount each cash flow from its own date, then add. Level payments forever add up to A/r, the perpetuity (Day 8). Level payments for N periods repay a loan, and what you still owe is the PV of the payments left (Day 9). Payments of any size give the NPV, and the rate that makes it zero is the IRR (Day 10). Move two: returns multiply. Chain the growth factors (1 + R) to link periods, and raise to a power to annualize (Day 11). The geometric mean is the one steady rate with the same product; the harmonic mean is what equal money really paid per share (Day 12). Day 13 put the moves side by side: the money-weighted return is move one, an IRR on your own cash; the time-weighted return is move two, the manager’s periods chained.

Memorize · the week on one card
  • Day 8 · Perpetuities.
    PV = A / r (first payment one period from now)
    r = A / PV (implied rate)
    First payment at time t: PV0 = (A / r) / (1 + r)t − 1
    Growing (preview): PV = A1 / (r − g), valid only if r > g
    Cap rate = NOI / property value, so value = NOI / cap rate (low priority)
  • Day 9 · Amortizing loans.
    A = PV × r / [1 − (1 + r)−N]
    Interestt = r × Balancet−1; Principalt = A − Interestt
    Balancek = PV of the remaining N − k payments at the loan rate
    BA II Plus: solve the loan in TVM first, then 2ND AMORT: P1, P2, ↓ BAL, ↓ PRN, ↓ INT
  • Day 10 · NPV and IRR.
    NPV = Σt=0N CFt / (1 + r)t
    IRR is the r that makes Σ CFt / (1 + IRR)t = 0
    Cash-flow additivity: PVs of cash flows measured at the same point in time can be added
    BA II Plus: CF 2ND CLR WORK; CFo ENTER ↓ C01 ENTER ↓ F01 ENTER ↓ …; NPV I ENTER ↓ CPT; IRR CPT
    TI-84: npv(rate in %, CF0, {CF1, CF2, …}[, {freq1, freq2, …}]); irr(CF0, {CF1, CF2, …}[, {freqs}]) returns a percent
  • Day 11 · Holding-period returns.
    HPR = (P1 − P0 + D1) / P0
    Rtotal = (1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + Rn) − 1
    Rannual = (1 + Rperiod)c − 1, c = periods per year (for a return over several years, c = 1/years)
    Gain needed to recover a loss L = L / (1 − L) (a 50% loss needs +100%)
  • Day 12 · The three means.
    R̄ = (R1 + R2 + … + Rn) / n
    RG = [(1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + Rn)]1/n − 1
    X̄H = n / Σ(1 / Xi) (average cost per share when equal money is invested each period)
    Positive values, not all equal: harmonic < geometric < arithmetic
    RG ≈ R̄ − σ2/2 (rough; poor when swings are large)
  • Day 13 · Money- vs time-weighted returns.
    MWR = the IRR of the investor’s cash flows: Σ CFt / (1 + MWR)t = 0
    TWR = (1 + HPR1)(1 + HPR2)…(1 + HPRn) − 1, annualized geometrically
    TWR is the right measure when the manager does not control the timing of external cash flows (manager evaluation)
    MWR > TWR if more money is invested just before strong returns; MWR < TWR if before weak returns
Exam traps
  • A deferred perpetuity discounted one period too many. A/r is the value one period before the first payment. First payment at t = 6? Then A/r sits at t = 5, and you discount 5 periods, not 6.
  • Old values left in the CF worksheet. The BA II Plus keeps every C0x and F0x until you clear them, and a leftover C05 from an earlier problem quietly joins today’s NPV. Press CF 2ND CLR WORK before every new stream. On the TI-84, type the whole list fresh inside npv( and irr(.
  • The arithmetic mean used for compound growth. +50% then −50% averages 0%, yet $100 → $150 → $75. Growth over several periods: multiply the growth factors and take the n-th root. The arithmetic mean is only the typical single period.

Ten questions, graded here, all with new numbers. Each names the day it comes from, so a miss tells you what to reread. Work them before peeking; the first try is what counts.

1. Day 12 · What did you really pay? You invest $600 in a fund at the start of each month for three months. The share prices on the three dates are $16, $20 and $25. What is your average cost per share? In dollars, to the nearest cent.

dollars per share
Hint The same money each time, so this is the harmonic mean of the prices: 3 ÷ (1/16 + 1/20 + 1/25). Or count the shares each $600 buys and divide the $1,800 by them.
Solution

Shares: 600 ÷ 16 = 37.5; 600 ÷ 20 = 30; 600 ÷ 25 = 24. That is 91.5 shares for $1,800, and 1,800 ÷ 91.5 = $19.67.

BA II Plus: 16 1/x + 20 1/x + 25 1/x = → 0.1525; 1/x → 6.5574; × 3 = → 19.6721.

TI-84 Plus: 3 ÷ ( 16 x⁻¹ + 20 x⁻¹ + 25 x⁻¹ ) ENTER → 19.6721.

The plain average of the prices, $20.33, is the trap: $600 bought 37.5 shares at $16 but only 24 at $25.

2. Day 8 · Payments that start later. A perpetuity pays $10 a year forever, and the first payment arrives at the end of year 4. At a required return of 8%, its value today is closest to:

  • Right. 10 / 0.08 = 125.00 is the value at t = 3, one year before the first payment; 125.00 / 1.083 = 99.23.

  • That discounts 125.00 for 4 years. A/r already sits one period before the first payment, at t = 3, so only 3 years of discounting are left.

  • That is A/r with no discounting: the value of a perpetuity whose first payment is one year away, not four.

Explanation

Payments come at t = 4, 5, 6, … and A/r values them all at t = 3. From there it is a single sum: PV0 = 125.00 / 1.083 = 125.00 / 1.259712 = 99.23.

BA II Plus: 10 ÷ .08 = → 125.0000; then 2ND CLR TVM 3 N 8 I/Y 0 PMT 125 FV CPT PV → PV = −99.2290.

TI-84 Plus: 10 ÷ .08 ENTER → 125.0000; then the TVM Solver with N=3, I%=8, PMT=0, FV=125, P/Y=1, C/Y=1; cursor on PV=, ALPHA ENTER → PV=−99.2290.

3. Day 9 · The mortgage payment. A $250,000 mortgage runs 30 years at 6.6% a year, stated, with a payment at the end of each month. What is the monthly payment? In dollars, to the nearest cent.

dollars a month
Hint Monthly, so N = 30 × 12 = 360 and I/Y = 6.6 ÷ 12 = 0.55. PV = 250,000, FV = 0, END mode; compute PMT.
Solution

BA II Plus: 2ND CLR TVM 360 N 6.6 ÷ 12 = I/Y 250000 PV 0 FV CPT PMT → PMT = −1,596.6470.

TI-84 Plus: APPS 1:Finance 1:TVM Solver: N=360, I%=6.6/12, PV=250000, PMT=0, FV=0, P/Y=1, C/Y=1, PMT: END; cursor on PMT=, then ALPHA ENTER → PMT=−1596.6470.

The payment is $1,596.65, negative because it leaves your pocket. Typing 6.6 for I/Y with N = 360 would treat 6.6% as a monthly rate and give $16,500.00: an alarm, not an answer. Leave the loan in the calculator for question 4.

4. Day 9 · Where year 1 went. For the same loan, how much interest do the first 12 payments pay in total? In dollars, to the nearest cent.

dollars
Hint Keep question 3’s loan in the calculator. BA II Plus: 2ND AMORT with P1 = 1 and P2 = 12, then ↓ down to INT. TI-84: ΣInt(1,12) from the Finance menu.
Solution

BA II Plus: 2ND AMORT; at P1= type 1 ENTER ↓; at P2= type 12 ENTER ↓ → BAL ≈ 247,258.28; ↓ → PRN ≈ −2,741.72; ↓ → INT = −16,418.0486.

TI-84 Plus: 2nd MODE (QUIT) to the home screen; APPS 1:Finance, ▼ down the list until A:ΣInt( is highlighted, ENTER, then 1 , 12 ) ENTER → −16418.0486. From the same menu, 9:bal( 12 ) ENTER → about 247258.28.

Check by hand: 12 payments are 12 × 1,596.647045 = 19,159.7645. The balance fell by 250,000 − 247,258.2841 = 2,741.7159. The rest, 16,418.05, was interest: about 86% of everything paid in year 1. Payment 1 alone was 0.0055 × 250,000 = 1,375.00 of interest and only 221.65 of principal, because interest is r × the balance, and the balance is biggest at the start.

The BA II Plus rounds each month to its display decimals, so its BAL and PRN can differ from the TI-84’s in the last digit or two. Anything within 10 cents is marked right.

5. Day 10 · Is the project worth it? A project costs $5,000 today and pays $1,500, $2,000, $2,500 and $1,000 at the ends of years 1 to 4. At a 10% required return, what is its NPV? In dollars, to the nearest cent.

dollars
Hint Clear the CF worksheet first. CFo = −5,000, then C01 to C04, each with F = 1; NPV with I = 10. TI-84: npv(10,−5000,{1500,2000,2500,1000}).
Solution

BA II Plus: CF 2ND CLR WORK 5000 +|− ENTER ↓ 1500 ENTER ↓ ↓ 2000 ENTER ↓ ↓ 2500 ENTER ↓ ↓ 1000 ENTER, then NPV 10 ENTER ↓ CPT → NPV = 577.8294. (Each ↓ ↓ steps past F0x, the frequency, which stays 1.)

TI-84 Plus: APPS 1:Finance 7:npv( 10 , (−) 5000 , 2nd ( 1500 , 2000 , 2500 , 1000 2nd ) ) ENTER → 577.8294.

By hand: 1,500/1.1 + 2,000/1.12 + 2,500/1.13 + 1,000/1.14 = 1,363.64 + 1,652.89 + 1,878.29 + 683.01 = 5,577.83, and 5,577.83 − 5,000 = 577.83. Positive, so the project earns more than 10%.

6. Day 10 · The project’s own rate. What is the IRR of the same project? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint The cash flows are still in the worksheet: just IRR CPT. TI-84: irr(−5000,{1500,2000,2500,1000}).
Solution

BA II Plus: IRR CPT → IRR = 15.3221.

TI-84 Plus: APPS 1:Finance 8:irr( (−) 5000 , 2nd ( 1500 , 2000 , 2500 , 1000 2nd ) ) ENTER → 15.3221.

15.32%. It fits question 5: the NPV at 10% was positive, and NPV falls as the rate rises, so it hits zero somewhere above 10%.

7. Day 11 · Nine months, made yearly. You buy a share at $80.00, receive a $2.00 dividend, and sell it at $86.00 nine months after you bought it. What is your annualized holding-period return? As a percent, to two decimals.

% a year
Hint First the HPR: (86 − 80 + 2) ÷ 80. A year holds 12/9 nine-month periods, so raise (1 + HPR) to the power 12/9, then subtract 1.
Solution

HPR = (86.00 − 80.00 + 2.00) / 80.00 = 8.00 / 80.00 = 10.00%. Annualized: 1.1012/9 − 1 = 1.1355 − 1 = 13.55%.

BA II Plus: 86 − 80 + 2 = → 8.0000; ÷ 80 = → 0.1000; + 1 = → 1.1000; yˣ ( 12 ÷ 9 ) = → 1.1355; − 1 = → 0.1355; × 100 = → 13.5508.

TI-84 Plus: ( 86 − 80 + 2 ) ÷ 80 ENTER → .1000; + 1 ENTER → 1.1000; ^ ( 12 ÷ 9 ) ENTER → 1.1355; − 1 ENTER → .1355; × 100 ENTER → 13.5508.

Scaling, 10% × 12/9 = 13.33%, is the trap: it forgets that each period’s gain would itself earn gains.

8. Day 11 · Down 30, up 30. A fund loses 30% in year 1 and gains 30% in year 2. Its total return over the two years is:

  • That adds the returns. The 30% gain is earned on the smaller base the loss left: $1,000 → $700 → $910.

  • Right. Growth factors multiply: 0.70 × 1.30 = 0.91, and 0.91 − 1 = −9.00%.

  • That is √0.91 − 1, the geometric mean: the loss per year. The question asks for the total over both years.

Explanation

Chain-link: Rtotal = (1 + R1)(1 + R2) − 1 = 0.70 × 1.30 − 1 = −9.00%. To climb back from $700 to $1,000 the fund needed 0.30 / 0.70 = 42.86%, not 30%.

9. Day 12 · The honest average. A fund returns +20%, −10% and +15% in three straight years. Get the arithmetic mean from one-variable statistics, then compute the geometric mean return and type it. As a percent, to two decimals.

% a year
Hint Growth factors 1.20, 0.90 and 1.15: multiply them, take the cube root (power 1/3), subtract 1.
Solution

BA II Plus: 2ND DATA 2ND CLR WORK 20 ENTER ↓ ↓ 10 +|− ENTER ↓ ↓ 15 ENTER; 2ND STAT (2ND SET until 1-V shows) ↓ → n = 3.0000 ↓ → x̄ = 8.3333. Then 2ND QUIT 1.2 × .9 × 1.15 = → 1.2420; yˣ ( 1 ÷ 3 ) = → 1.0749; − 1 = → 0.0749; × 100 = → 7.4914.

TI-84 Plus: STAT 1:Edit… (if L1 holds old numbers, ▲ to the L1 title, CLEAR ENTER), then 20 ENTER (−) 10 ENTER 15 ENTER; STAT ► (CALC) 1:1-Var Stats 2nd 1 ENTER → x̄=8.3333, with n (3) further down. Then CLEAR 1.2 × .9 × 1.15 ENTER → 1.2420; ^ ( 1 ÷ 3 ) ENTER → 1.0749; − 1 ENTER → .0749; × 100 ENTER → 7.4914.

Arithmetic 8.33%, geometric 7.49%. Three years at the geometric mean turn $10,000 into $12,420.00, exactly what 10,000 × 1.2 × 0.9 × 1.15 gives. The 0.84-point gap is what the swings cost.

10. Day 13 · Whose return? An investor buys 100 shares at $20. A year later she buys 100 more at $22. At the end of year 2 she sells all 200 shares at $21.50. There are no dividends. Which pair of annual returns is right?

  • The two measures swapped. 3.68% chain-links the share’s two yearly returns: that is the time-weighted return.

  • That halves the two-year TWR of 7.50% (arithmetic, not geometric, annualizing) and uses it for both. The MWR comes from her own cash flows, which this ignores.

  • Right. MWR = IRR of −2,000, −2,200, +4,300 = 1.60%. TWR: +10% then −2.27%, so √(1.10 × 21.50/22) − 1 = 3.68%. Twice as much of her money was in during the weaker year.

Explanation

Her cash flows: −2,000 today, −2,200 at t = 1, +4,300 at t = 2.

BA II Plus: CF 2ND CLR WORK 2000 +|− ENTER ↓ 2200 +|− ENTER ↓ ↓ 4300 ENTER IRR CPT → IRR = 1.6046. For the TWR: 2ND QUIT 1.1 × 21.5 ÷ 22 = → 1.0750; √x → 1.0368; − 1 = → 0.0368.

TI-84 Plus: APPS 1:Finance 8:irr( (−) 2000 , 2nd ( (−) 2200 , 4300 2nd ) ) ENTER → 1.6046. Then 2nd x² (√) 1.1 × 21.5 ÷ 22 ) − 1 ENTER → .0368.

Year 1 (+10%) had $2,000 of her money at work; year 2 (−2.27%) had $4,400. Her timing pulled her own return below the share’s.
