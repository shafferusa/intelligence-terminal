Apple’s split and the Dow’s reshuffle

Apple’s shares closed at $499.23 on Friday 28 August 2020. Over the weekend its 4-for-1 stock split took effect: each old share became four new ones. Friday’s close was the same as about $124.81 a share.

On Monday 31 August, before the open, the Dow Jones Industrial Average changed too. Out went ExxonMobil, Pfizer and Raytheon Technologies. In came Salesforce, Amgen and Honeywell. S&P Dow Jones Indices, which runs the Dow, said the changes were prompted by Apple’s split.

Apple had been the Dow’s heaviest member: about 12% of the index by one estimate (others put it nearer 10%). After the split it was about 3%.

In the S&P 500 the split left Apple’s weight exactly where it was.

The split itself changed nothing about Apple’s business, profits or total market value. So how can one index decide Apple suddenly matters a quarter as much, while another says nothing changed?

The idea

An index turns many prices into one number: how did the market do? It is also the yardstick. Day 13’s time-weighted return judges a manager; an index is what the manager is judged against.

To build one, you must decide how much each stock counts: its weight. The main answers:

  • Price weighting. Each stock counts by its share price. The Dow works this way.
  • Market-cap (value) weighting. Each stock counts by the company’s value, price × shares.
  • Float-adjusted. The same, counting only the free float: shares outstanding minus closely held shares (insiders, controlling owners, governments), the shares the public can actually buy. The S&P 500 works this way.
  • Equal weighting. Every stock counts the same.
  • Fundamental weighting. By a size measure that is not the share price, such as sales, earnings, book value or dividends.

An everyday picture. A price-weighted index is a shopping list that says “one of each”. If the shop starts selling cheese by the quarter-wheel, “one cheese” now means a quarter-wheel. Cheese shrinks on your bill, though there is just as much cheese in the shop.

One more term: liquidity, how easily shares trade. A common measure is average daily volume, the number of shares that change hands on a typical day.

Why it works

Start from Day 11: a return is end value ÷ start value − 1. An index is a basket of shares on paper, so its return is the basket’s; each weighting method is a recipe for what goes in.

  1. Price-weighted: one share of each. The basket is worth P1 + P2 + … + PN. Divide by a divisor D to get a handy level: Index = ΣPi / D.
  2. A weight is a share of the money: Pi / ΣPj. A $400 share counts five times an $80 one, whatever the companies’ size.
  3. A k-for-1 split turns P into P/k, so ΣP drops though nothing real happened. The fix: Dnew = (sum of prices after the split) / (index just before). Same level, but the stock’s weight now follows its smaller price.
  4. Market-cap: the whole company. Stock i is worth Pi × Qi, so wi = PiQi / ΣPjQj. A split makes the price P/k and the shares kQ: the product does not move, so neither does the weight. Float-adjusted: Q counts float shares only.
  5. Equal: the same money in each, wi = 1/N. Prices move and the weights drift (with the worked example’s returns, thirds become 34.78%, 30.60%, 34.62% in a quarter), so the index is rebalanced: sell some winners, buy losers.
  6. The return, for any recipe. Each stock’s money ends at its start × (1 + Ri). So the basket’s end ÷ start = Σwi(1 + Ri) = 1 + ΣwiRi, since the weights add to 1. Hence Rindex = ΣwiRi, with start-of-period weights. Price-weighted, the divisor cancels: ΣPend / ΣPstart − 1.
  7. Total return adds Day 11’s income: each Ri becomes (Pend − Pstart + Inci)/Pstart. So total return = price return + Σwi × Inci/Pi, the income return.

In plain words: an index is a pretend basket of shares. The recipe says how much of each goes in, and the index’s return is how much the basket gained.

After a split: Dnew = Σ(prices after the split) / index just before

Market-cap: wi = PiQi / ΣPjQj   Equal: wi = 1/N

Rindex = ΣwiRi;   total return = price return + Σwi × Inci/Pi

Pi
share price of stock i (at the start of the period, in the weights)
D
the divisor of a price-weighted index
Qi
shares of stock i counted: shares outstanding, or the free float in a float-adjusted index
wi
weight of stock i: its share of the basket’s money at the start; the weights add to 1
Ri
return of stock i over the period, as a decimal
N
number of stocks in the index
Inci
income (dividends) paid by stock i during the period
Memorize
  • Price-weighted index value: Index = Σ Pi / divisor
  • New divisor after a split: choose Dnew so that Σ (post-split prices) / Dnew = index value just before the split
  • Market-cap (value) weight: wi = (Pi × Qi) / Σ (Pj × Qj); float-adjusted uses float shares for Q
  • Equal weight with N stocks: wi = 1/N (must be rebalanced as prices move)
  • Index total return vs price return: total return = price return + income (dividends) return
  • Free float: shares outstanding − closely held shares (insiders, controlling owners, governments); float-adjusted weights use float shares

Worked example

Problem. Three stocks over one quarter. X: 50 → 54; 200m shares, 40m closely held. Y: 20 → 19; 500m shares, none closely held; pays a 0.40 dividend. Z: 80 → 86; 100m shares, 50m closely held. Find (a) the float-adjusted weights and price return, (b) the full-cap and equal-weighted price returns, (c) the float-adjusted total return.

By hand
  1. Returns: X 54/50 − 1 = +8.00%; Y 19/20 − 1 = −5.00%; Z 86/80 − 1 = +7.50%.
  2. Free float = outstanding − closely held: 160m, 500m, 50m.
  3. Float caps ($m): 50 × 160 = 8,000; 20 × 500 = 10,000; 80 × 50 = 4,000. Total 22,000.
  4. (a) Weights 36.36%, 45.45%, 18.18%. Return = 0.3636 × 8 + 0.4545 × (−5) + 0.1818 × 7.5 = 2.91 − 2.27 + 1.36 = 2.00%.
  5. (b) Full caps 10,000, 10,000, 8,000 (of 28,000): weights 35.71%, 35.71%, 28.57%, return 3.21%. Equal: (8 − 5 + 7.5)/3 = 3.50%.
  6. (c) Y with its dividend: (19 + 0.40)/20 − 1 = −3.00%. Index: 0.3636 × 8 + 0.4545 × (−3) + 0.1818 × 7.5 = 2.91% = 2.00% price + 0.91% income (0.4545 × 2%).

Back to the story

The Dow is price-weighted: one share of each of its 30 stocks, summed and divided by a divisor of about 0.15. Apple counted as $499.23 in that sum on Friday and as $124.81 after the split. Its price fell by three-quarters, and its weight nearly as much: about 12% became about 3%. (Apple was a small slice of the sum; A, most of the walkthrough’s basket, only halved.)

Each $1 move in any Dow stock moved the index about 6.7 points (1 ÷ 0.15). So a 1% move in Apple, about $5, had been worth roughly 33 points; after the split, about 8.

That morning the divisor was reset, for the split and the three swaps at once, so the Dow’s level did not jump: the walkthrough’s step 2, for real.

The S&P 500 weights by price × shares. Apple’s price went to a quarter, its share count rose fourfold, and the product, its market value, did not move. Neither did its weight.

Exam traps
  • Not resetting the divisor after a split. In the walkthrough, keeping D = 3 shows 300 ÷ 3 = 100 the moment A splits: a 50% “fall” for no economic reason.
  • Thinking a split changes a cap-weighted index’s weights. A’s market cap is 400 × 10m = 4,000 before and 100 × 40m = 4,000 after: 34.48% of the index both times.
  • Reporting the price return when the total return is asked. In the walkthrough that is 4.00% instead of 5.00%: the dividends are the gap.
  • Using shares outstanding in a float-adjusted index. In the worked example that turns the 2.00% return into 3.21%.

2. Why. A company in a market-cap-weighted index splits its stock 2-for-1. Nothing else changes. What happens to its weight in the index?

  • That is price-weighted thinking, where a stock’s weight follows its share price. A cap-weighted index looks at price × shares.

  • The share count doubles, but each share is now worth half as much. Counting the shares and forgetting the price is half the picture.

  • Right. The weight is the company’s market value over the total, and a split changes neither.

Explanation

wi = PiQi / ΣPjQj. After a 2-for-1 split, (P/2) × (2Q) = PQ, and every other company is unchanged, so the top and the bottom of the fraction stay put. That is why Apple’s weight in the S&P 500 stayed put through the split, while the Dow had to reset its divisor.

3. Twist: a swap, like the Dow’s in 2020. A price-weighted index of four stocks has prices that add up to 260 and a divisor of 2.0. A stock priced 30 is replaced by one priced 110. What must the new divisor be? To four decimals.

4. Exam style. A price-weighted index holds three stocks. Over the year, A rises from 25 to 30, B falls from 50 to 45 and C rises from 100 to 110; C also pays a dividend of 2.00. The index’s price return for the year is closest to:

  • Right: (30 + 45 + 110)/(25 + 50 + 100) − 1 = 185/175 − 1. Within the year the divisor cancels.

  • That averages the three returns (20%, −10%, 10%): equal weighting. A price-weighted index gives C, the dearest stock, the most weight.

  • That adds C’s dividend: (185 + 2)/175 − 1 is the total return, not the price return.

Explanation

The weights are the starting prices over their sum: 25/175 = 14.29%, 50/175 = 28.57%, 100/175 = 57.14%. Weighted return = (25 × 20% + 50 × (−10%) + 100 × 10%)/175 = (5 − 5 + 10)/175 = 5.71%, the same as 185/175 − 1.

BA II Plus: ( 30 + 45 + 110 ) ÷ ( 25 + 50 + 100 ) − 1 = → 0.0571, × 100 = → 5.7143. TI-84 Plus: the same keys with ENTER → .0571, then × 100 ENTER → 5.7143.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why must a price-weighted index change its divisor after a stock split, and why does a cap-weighted index not need to?

Model answer

A price-weighted index is the sum of the share prices divided by a divisor, and a split cuts a share’s price without changing the company’s value, so without a new divisor the index would fall for no economic reason; the divisor is reset so the level is the same just after the split as just before. In a cap-weighted index the price falls by the split factor while the share count rises by the same factor, so price × shares, and with it the weight, does not change.

A complete answer says: (1) index = Σ prices / divisor, so weight follows share price; (2) a split cuts the price but not the value, so the divisor is reset to keep the index level unchanged; (3) in a cap-weighted index P falls and Q rises by the same factor, so value and weight are unchanged.
