From Day 24. Fund 1 has a mean annual return of 8% and a standard deviation of 12%. Fund 2 has a mean of 5% and a standard deviation of 9%. Judged by the coefficient of variation, which fund takes less risk per unit of return?

  • The SD alone ignores how much return you get for the risk. The CV divides it by the mean.

  • Those are mean ÷ SD (5/9 and 8/12), the ratio upside down. CV = s / X̄, risk on top.

  • Right. CV = s / X̄: 12/8 = 1.50 for Fund 1, 9/5 = 1.80 for Fund 2. Lower CV, less risk per point of return.

Why

The coefficient of variation scales risk by return: Fund 1 carries 1.50 points of SD per point of mean return, Fund 2 carries 1.80. Fund 2 is less volatile in absolute terms, but it pays less for each unit of volatility.

Ulam’s solitaire problem

In 1946 the Polish-American mathematician Stanisław Ulam was recovering from an illness and passing the time with Canfield solitaire. A question caught him: what is the chance that a game laid out with 52 cards comes out?

He went at it the classic way, with combinatorics: count the winning deals, divide by all the deals. But a deck can be ordered in 52! ways, about 8 × 1067. He spent a long time on it and got nowhere.

Then he wondered whether it would be more practical to lay the game out, say, one hundred times and simply count the successes.

He took the idea to John von Neumann. At Los Alamos it became a way to model neutron diffusion in nuclear weapons. Their colleague Nicholas Metropolis named it after the Monte Carlo casino, and in 1949 Metropolis and Ulam published “The Monte Carlo Method”.

A stock’s price a year from now is the same kind of problem: about 252 trading days, each a random step, and the price built from all of them. When an outcome depends on so many random steps that no formula can handle it, how can you still find its probability?

The idea

Prices move by multiplying: a +2% day multiplies the price by 1.02. The continuously compounded return (the log return), r = ln(P1/P0), turns that multiplying into adding. A week’s log return is simply the sum of its days’.

If each day’s log return is a draw from a normal curve, the price is lognormal: its log is normal. A lognormal price can never fall below zero, and it is skewed right: gains can multiply it without limit, losses only shrink it toward zero.

When a question is too tangled for a formula, Monte Carlo simulation does what Ulam proposed. Specify the model and how each input is random; draw random values; compute the outcome; repeat thousands of times; summarize the results (average, percentiles, the share below a line).

Two cousins use the past instead of an assumed curve. Historical simulation replays the returns that actually happened. Bootstrap resampling draws from those past returns at random, with replacement, to build as many new paths as you like. Neither assumes a distribution, but neither can produce a return the sample never saw. Monte Carlo can model anything, but only as well as the assumed distribution fits.

Why it works

Start from Day 4. Continuous compounding at rate r turns P0 into P1 = P0 × er. LN undoes eˣ, so r = ln(P1/P0) = ln(1 + HPR).

Step 1: chain the days (Day 11). Growth factors multiply, and the in-between prices cancel: PT/P0 = (P1/P0) × (P2/P1) × … × (PT/PT−1).

Step 2: take ln. The log of a product is the sum of the logs, so ln(PT/P0) = r1 + r2 + … + rT. Log returns add, exactly.

Step 3: undo the ln. PT = P0 × er1 + … + rT.

Step 4: make it random (Day 30). Let each day’s log return be r = μ + σz, z a standard normal draw (Day 30’s X = μ + zσ). A sum of independent normals is normal, so ln PT is normal: PT is lognormal.

Step 5: never below zero. e to any power is positive: even a −100% log return leaves 100 × e−1 = 36.79 of a $100 stock.

Step 6: skewed right. From 100, a log move of +0.20 gives 122.14 (up 22.14); −0.20 gives 81.87 (down 18.13). Gains stretch, losses squash, so the mean sits above the median: median P0eμ, mean P0eμ + σ²/2. For μ = 5%, σ = 20%: median 105.13, mean 107.25.

Step 7: where z comes from. A computer draws u, uniform between 0 and 1: a random percentile. invNorm(u) returns the z at that percentile (the inverse transform): u = 0.90 gives 1.2816. Equally likely percentiles give normally distributed z’s.

In plain words: a price is its start times e to the sum of its log returns. Make the returns random, deal the year out thousands of times, and count.

Memorize
  • Continuously compounded return over a period: rcc = ln(P1 / P0) = ln(1 + HPR)
  • Additivity of log returns: r0,T = r1 + r2 + … + rT
  • Price from a continuously compounded return: PT = P0 × er0,T

  • Monte Carlo simulation, step by step: Specify the model and the distribution of each random input → draw random values → compute the outcome → repeat many times → summarize the distribution of outcomes

Worked example

Problem. A stock closes at 50.00, then 55.00, then 49.50. Find each day’s simple and log return and the 2-day log return, and show which kind of return adds up correctly.

By hand
  1. Simple returns: 55/50 − 1 = +10.00%; 49.50/55 − 1 = −10.00%. Added: 0%. But the price fell: 49.50/50 − 1 = −1.00%.
  2. Log returns: r1 = ln(55/50) = ln 1.1 = 0.0953; r2 = ln(49.50/55) = ln 0.9 = −0.1054.
  3. Add them: 0.0953 + (−0.1054) = −0.0101.
  4. Check in one step: ln(49.50/50) = ln 0.99 = −0.0101. The same, because ln(1.1 × 0.9) = ln 1.1 + ln 0.9.
  5. Back to a price: 50 × e−0.010050 = 49.50.

2-day continuously compounded return: −0.0101, or −1.01%. Log returns add; simple returns do not.

Back to the story

Ulam’s way out was to stop solving and start dealing. Try it on a stock at 100 whose one-year log return is normal with mean 5% and SD 20%. What is the chance it ends the year below 80?

Here a formula does exist, so we can grade the simulation. Below 80 means a log return below ln 0.8 = −0.22314, so z = (−0.22314 − 0.05) / 0.20 = −1.3657, and the chance is 8.60%.

A seeded computer run of 100 simulated years, Ulam’s hundred deals, gave 15%: rough. 10,000 runs gave 8.92%: close. The typical error shrinks with the square root of the runs, about 2.8 points at 100 runs and 0.28 at 10,000: a hundred times the work for ten times the precision. Monte Carlo buys precision with repetitions, which is why it waited for computers.

And not one of the 10,100 simulated prices fell below zero. The lowest was 46.83. Lognormal, as promised.

Exam traps
  • Adding simple returns across periods. +10% then −10% adds to 0%, but the price fell 1%. Add log returns (they sum exactly), or multiply growth factors (Day 11).
  • Believing a lognormal price can go negative. er is positive for every r, so the price is bounded below by 0: even a −100% log return leaves 36.79% of it. It is the log return that is normal and can be any size.
  • Mixing up the simulations. Monte Carlo draws from an assumed distribution (e.g. normal log returns). Historical simulation and bootstrap resampling both use the actual past returns; the bootstrap draws from them at random, with replacement.
  • Typing a rate in percent into eˣ. TVM wants 5 for 5%; eˣ and LN want 0.05. In the walkthrough, e2.6132 instead of e0.026132 turns 102.65 into 1,364.26.

2. Why. Why can you add daily log returns to get the week’s log return?

  • Right. Growth factors multiply (Day 11), and ln turns multiplying into adding.

  • The identity is exact, not an approximation. It holds even for huge moves: a doubling then a halving gives ln 2 + ln 0.5 = 0.693147 − 0.693147 = 0 = ln 1.

  • Additivity holds for any prices, whatever their distribution. Normality is a modelling choice made afterwards, and it is the log returns, not the prices, that the model makes normal.

Explanation

The in-between prices cancel in the product of growth factors, and the log of a product is the sum of the logs. In the worked example, ln 1.1 + ln 0.9 is exactly ln 0.99 = −0.0101, while the simple returns +10% and −10% wrongly add to 0%.

4. Exam style. A stock’s price rises from 50 to 60 in period 1 and falls from 60 to 45 in period 2. Its continuously compounded return over the two periods is closest to:

  • That adds the simple returns, +20% and −25%. Simple returns do not add.

  • Right: ln(45/50) = ln 0.9 = −0.1054, the same as ln(60/50) + ln(45/60) = 0.1823 − 0.2877.

  • That is the holding-period return, 45/50 − 1, not the continuously compounded one.

Explanation

The continuously compounded return over any span is the ln of the ending price over the starting price: ln(45/50) = −10.54%. It is more negative than the −10.00% simple return because ln(1 + R) is always below R. Exponentiating it gets you back: 50 × eln 0.9 = 50 × 0.9 = 45.

5. Say it back. In two or three sentences: how would you use Monte Carlo simulation to estimate the chance a stock ends the year below 80, and how does bootstrap resampling differ?

Model answer

Assume a model for the random input, say a normal annual log return with a chosen mean and SD, draw a random return from it, compute the year-end price P0 × er, and repeat thousands of times; the share of runs ending below 80 estimates the probability, more precisely the more runs you do. Bootstrap resampling skips the assumed distribution: it draws returns at random, with replacement, from the actual past returns, so it keeps their real shape but can only produce returns that appear in the sample.

A complete answer says: (1) assume a distribution for the random input (e.g. normal log returns) and draw from it; (2) compute the ending price for each draw, repeat thousands of times and count the share below 80; (3) bootstrap resampling instead draws at random, with replacement, from the actual past returns: no distribution is assumed, but only returns seen in the sample can appear (historical simulation also relies on the actual past record).
