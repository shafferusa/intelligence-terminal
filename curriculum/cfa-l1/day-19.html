From Day 3. Investors require a 9% nominal return on a share and expect 3% inflation. The exact real required return is closest to:

  • Right: 1.09 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 0.058252, so 5.83%.

  • That is the shortcut 9% − 3%. Close, but not exact: it drops the cross term, real × inflation.

  • That is 1.09 × 1.03 − 1: multiplying builds a nominal rate from a real one. Going from nominal to real, divide.

Why

(1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation), so real = 1.09 / 1.03 − 1 = 5.83%. Keep it in mind today: the r in today’s formula is a nominal required return like this 9%, so the growth rate g that goes with it must be nominal too.

Shell’s first dividend cut since the war

On 30 April 2020, with the pandemic crushing demand for oil, Royal Dutch Shell cut its quarterly dividend by two-thirds: from 47 US cents a share to 16 cents.

It was Shell’s first dividend cut since the Second World War. The company had held its dividend even through the deep oil slumps of the 1980s.

Over a year, 47 cents a quarter is $1.88 a share. At 16 cents a quarter, it would be $0.64.

The shares fell. Within the first hour of trading in London they were about 7% lower. In New York, by one report, its US-listed shares fell as much as 14% in the first half hour.

Now set those falls next to the cut. If a share is worth the dividends it will pay, cutting the dividend by two-thirds should cut the share’s value by two-thirds.

The price fell by only a fraction of that. Why? And what was the price saying before the news?

The idea

A share is a claim on dividends: cash paid to its owners year after year, with no end date. Its value today is the present value of all of them.

Day 8 valued a level payment forever: A / r. Dividends usually grow. If they grow at a constant rate g, forever, the share is a growing perpetuity, worth

V0 = D1 / (r − g)

next year’s dividend divided by the gap between the return investors require, r, and the growth rate, g. This is the Gordon growth model, or constant-growth dividend discount model.

The intuition. Investors want 8% a year. If the dividend grows 3% a year, the share’s value grows 3% with it, so growth supplies 3 of the 8 points and the dividend the other 5. Shell’s next dividend, $1.88 × 1.03 = $1.9364, must be 5% of the price: $1.9364 ÷ 0.05 = $38.73.

Read backwards, a market price tells you what investors expect: required return = dividend yield + growth.

Who gets a dividend? Four dates, in order:

  • Declaration date: the board announces it.
  • Ex-dividend date: buy on or after it and you don’t get this dividend. The share now carries one dividend fewer, so its price drops by about the dividend.
  • Holder-of-record date: the owners on the company’s list get paid.
  • Payment date: the cash arrives.

Why it works

Start from Day 2: money due in a year is worth that amount ÷ (1 + r) today.

  1. Hold the share a year: you get D1 plus the share, worth V1 by then. So V0 = (D1 + V1) / (1 + r).
  2. A year on, every dividend still to come is (1 + g) times bigger, so the share is worth (1 + g) times more: V1 = V0 × (1 + g).
  3. Substitute: V0 = [D1 + V0(1 + g)] / (1 + r).
  4. Multiply both sides by (1 + r): V0(1 + r) = D1 + V0(1 + g).
  5. Collect the V0 terms; the 1s cancel: V0 × (r − g) = D1.
  6. Divide by (r − g): V0 = D1 / (r − g).

Backwards. Call V0 the market price P0 and divide step 4 by it: 1 + r = D1/P0 + 1 + g. So r = D1/P0 + g (dividend yield plus the price growth of step 2), and g = r − D1/P0.

Only D0 given? Then D1 = D0(1 + g) hides g on the right. From P0(r − g) = D0(1 + g): rP0 − gP0 = D0 + gD0, so g = (rP0 − D0) / (P0 + D0). Test: P0 = 38.728, D0 = 1.88, r = 0.08 give back 3.0000%.

No growth. g = 0 gives Day 8’s A / r: the value of a non-callable, non-convertible preferred share, whose dividend is fixed. V0 = Dp / rp, at the preferred’s own required return.

Why r must beat g. Written out, V0 = D1/(1 + r) + D1(1 + g)/(1 + r)2 + … Each term is the one before times (1 + g)/(1 + r), here 1.03/1.08 = 0.9537, so the terms shrink and the sum settles at 38.7280. If g ≥ r they never shrink: no finite value, and D1/(r − g) gives nonsense.

In plain words: a share is worth next year’s dividend divided by how far the return you want outruns the dividend’s growth.

V0 = D1 / (r − g), with D1 = D0 × (1 + g)

r = D1 / P0 + g

g = r − D1 / P0

V0 = Dp / rp (preferred share)

Memorize
  • Gordon growth model: V0 = D1 / (r − g), where D1 = D0 × (1 + g)
  • Implied required return on equity: r = D1 / P0 + g
  • Implied dividend growth rate: g = r − D1 / P0
  • Value of a non-callable, non-convertible preferred share: V0 = Dp / rp
  • Dividend chronology: Declaration date → ex-dividend date (shares bought on or after it do not get the dividend; price falls by about D) → holder-of-record date → payment date
  • Implied growth when only D0 and P0 are known: g = (r × P0 − D0) / (P0 + D0), from P0 = D0(1 + g)/(r − g)

Worked example

Problem. (a) A stock just paid a $2.00 dividend; dividends grow 4% a year forever and investors require 10%. What is it worth? (b) If it trades at $40, with D1 = $2.08 and g = 4%, what return does the market require? (c) What is a preferred share paying $3.00 a year worth at a 7.5% required return?

By hand
  1. (a) “Just paid” is D0. Grow it: D1 = 2.00 × 1.04 = 2.08.
  2. V0 = 2.08 / (0.10 − 0.04) = 2.08 / 0.06 = 34.6667, so $34.67.
  3. (b) r = D1/P0 + g = 2.08/40 + 0.04 = 0.052 + 0.04 = 9.20%: a 5.20% dividend yield plus 4% growth.
  4. Sense check: $40 is above the $34.67 the share is worth at 10%, so the market must require less than 10%. It does: 9.20%.
  5. (c) V0 = Dp/rp = 3.00 / 0.075 = $40.00.

Answer: (a) $34.67; (b) 9.20%; (c) $40.00.

Back to the story

The model says value is proportional to next year’s dividend. With r = 8% and g = 3% (illustrative, not anyone’s estimate for Shell), the old $1.88 dividend supports $38.73 a share and the new $0.64 supports $13.18: 66% less, the size of the cut.

Yet the shares fell about 7% in early London trading and, by one report, as much as 14% early in New York. So before the news the price cannot have been near what the old dividend supported: it must already have been far lower.

Read the model backwards: at the same r, a lower price means lower expected growth, g = r − D1/P0. At a made-up $25, the old dividend implies growth of just 0.45% a year, not 3%.

The likeliest reading: investors had largely priced in a cut before Shell announced it. A price moves on the surprise, not on the news.

Exam traps
  • Putting D0 on top. The model needs next year’s dividend: if the question says “just paid”, multiply by (1 + g) first. Shell’s 1.88 / 0.05 = 37.60, not 38.73.
  • Using the model when g ≥ r. At g = 9% and r = 8%: 2.0492 / (−0.01) = −204.92, a negative price. The model doesn’t apply; a stretch of fast growth needs a multistage model (Day 20).
  • Valuing a preferred share at the common shares’ required return. Use rp: the $5 preferred is worth 83.33 at its own 6%, not 62.50 at a common-stock 8%.

2. Why. A share goes ex-dividend today; the dividend is $0.50. What happens to its price, and why?

  • The cash leaves on the payment date, but who gets it is settled by the ex-date. From the ex-date the share no longer carries that dividend, so that is when the price drops.

  • Nobody has to sell. Whoever owned the share before the ex-date gets the dividend whether they sell or not; the price drops because the share itself now carries one dividend fewer.

  • Right. Step 1 of the derivation: just before, the share is worth the coming dividend plus everything after it; from the ex-date a buyer gets only everything after it.

Explanation

Just before the ex-date: value ≈ the $0.50 about to be paid + the value of the dividends after it. On the ex-date the $0.50 stays with the seller, so the share is worth about $0.50 less. “About”, because the market moves that day for other reasons too.

3. Twist: the return the market requires. A share trades at $50. It just paid a $1.50 dividend, and dividends are expected to grow 6% a year forever. What return is the market requiring? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint First D1 = D0 × (1 + g). Then r = D1/P0 + g: dividend yield plus growth.
Solution

D1 = 1.50 × 1.06 = 1.59. Dividend yield = 1.59 / 50 = 0.0318 = 3.18%. r = 3.18% + 6% = 9.18%.

BA II Plus: ( 1.5 × 1.06 ÷ 50 + .06 ) × 100 = → 9.1800.

TI-84 Plus: ( 1.5 × 1.06 ÷ 50 + .06 ) × 100 ENTER → 9.1800.

Check it forwards: 1.59 / (0.0918 − 0.06) = 50.00. Using D0 instead gives 9.00%.

4. Exam style. A company has just paid an annual dividend of $1.20 per share. Dividends are expected to grow at 5% a year indefinitely, and the required return on its equity is 11%. Using the Gordon growth model, the value of one share is closest to:

  • Right: D1 = 1.20 × 1.05 = 1.26, and 1.26 / (0.11 − 0.05) = 21.00.

V0 = D0(1 + g) / (r − g) = 1.26 / 0.06 = 21.00.

BA II Plus: 1.2 × 1.05 ÷ ( .11 − .05 ) = → 21.0000. TI-84 Plus: the same keys, ending ENTER → 21.0000.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what does V0 = D1 / (r − g) say, and why does it work only when r is bigger than g?

Model answer

A share is worth the present value of all its future dividends; if they grow at a constant rate g forever, that growing perpetuity adds up to next year’s dividend divided by (r − g). Each discounted dividend is the one before times (1 + g)/(1 + r), so only when r is bigger than g do they shrink and add up to a finite value; if g ≥ r they never shrink and the sum has no end.

A complete answer says: (1) it is the present value of a growing perpetuity of dividends; (2) D1 is next year’s dividend, D0 × (1 + g); (3) if g ≥ r the discounted dividends never get smaller, so the sum has no finite value.
