Pascal, Fermat and the unfinished game

In the summer of 1654 Blaise Pascal, in Paris, was trading letters with Pierre de Fermat in Toulouse. Their subject was a gambling puzzle, probably put to Pascal by the Chevalier de Méré.

Two equally skilled players each stake 32 pistoles. The first to win three rounds takes all 64. But they have to stop early, before anyone has won. How should they split the pot?

There was an old answer. Luca Pacioli had printed it in 1494: split the money in proportion to the rounds already won.

In his letter of 29 July 1654, Pascal gave a different answer. If one player leads two rounds to one, the leader should take 48 pistoles and the other 16.

Pacioli’s rule gives the leader two-thirds of 64: about 42.67. Pascal said 48.

Where does 48 come from? How do you put a value on rounds that were never played?

The idea

You can’t know a random outcome in advance. You can still price it.

The expected value is the probability-weighted average of the outcomes: what you would average per try if the same situation could be replayed again and again.

A bet that pays $10 with probability 0.3, and nothing otherwise, is worth 0.3 × $10 = $3.00. No single play pays $3; the long-run average does.

The variance measures how far outcomes stray from that average, weighted the same way. Its square root is the standard deviation.

Often the odds depend on a scenario. A bank’s earnings depend on whether the economy is good or poor. Then work in two steps: find the average inside each scenario (a conditional expected value, E(X | S), read “given S”), then average those, weighted by how likely each scenario is. That is the total probability rule.

An everyday picture: a 30% chance of rain, when your commute takes 40 minutes; otherwise it takes 25. Expected commute: 0.3 × 40 + 0.7 × 25 = 12 + 17.5 = 29.5 minutes.

A probability tree draws it: a first fork for the scenario, then a fork for the outcome inside it.

Why it works

Start from a plain average (Day 12). Put 100 tickets in a hat: 45 say 2.60, 15 say 2.45, 10 say 2.20, 30 say 2.00. Their mean is (45 × 2.60 + 15 × 2.45 + 10 × 2.20 + 30 × 2.00) / 100 = 235.75 / 100 = 2.3575.

Split the division. Divide each count by 100 first: 0.45 × 2.60 + 0.15 × 2.45 + 0.10 × 2.20 + 0.30 × 2.00, the same 2.3575. Each count / 100 is the chance of drawing that ticket, so the mean is Σ P(Xi) Xi.

Variance, the same way. Day 24’s population variance averages squared deviations over every ticket: Σ P(Xi)[Xi − E(X)]2. The probabilities describe the whole hat; nothing is estimated from a sample, so there is no n − 1: on the calculator, σx, not Sx.

Where 0.45 comes from: multiply along the path. EPS is 2.60 only if the economy is good (0.60) and earnings then come in high (0.75 of good years): 0.75 × 0.60 = 0.45. That is P(AB) = P(A | B) × P(B); the bar reads “given”.

Add the paths. An event that can happen in several scenarios gets the sum of its paths: P(A) = Σ P(A | Si) P(Si). The scenarios must not overlap and must cover everything, or a path is counted twice or missed.

Group the paths by branch. Pull 0.60 out of the good-economy terms and 0.40 out of the poor ones:

E(X) = 0.60 × (0.75 × 2.60 + 0.25 × 2.45) + 0.40 × (0.25 × 2.20 + 0.75 × 2.00)

= 0.60 × 2.5625 + 0.40 × 2.05 = 2.3575.

The brackets are E(X | good) and E(X | poor). Nothing new: the same sum, factored. That is the total probability rule for expected value.

The overlap. P(A) + P(B) counts the outcomes where both happen twice, so take them off once: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(AB).

Independence. If knowing B tells you nothing about A, P(A | B) = P(A); only then is P(AB) = P(A) × P(B).

In plain words: an expected value is what something pays on average if you could play it over and over. When the future forks, average each branch, then average the branches by how likely each one is.

E(X) = Σ P(Xi) Xi

σ2(X) = Σ P(Xi) [Xi − E(X)]2

E(X) = Σ E(X | Si) P(Si)  ·  P(A) = Σ P(A | Si) P(Si)

P(AB) = P(A | B) P(B)  ·  P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(AB)

Xi
outcome i (an EPS, a payoff, a return)
P(Xi)
its probability; all of them add to 1
E(X)
expected value: the probability-weighted mean
σ2(X)
variance; its square root σ(X) is the standard deviation
Si
scenario i; the scenarios do not overlap and together cover everything (mutually exclusive and exhaustive)
E(X | Si)
conditional expected value: the mean of X if Si happens
P(A | B)
conditional probability: the chance of A, given that B happens
P(AB)

Memorize
  • Expected value of a discrete random variable: E(X) = Σ P(Xi) × Xi
  • Variance of a discrete random variable: σ2(X) = Σ P(Xi) × [Xi − E(X)]2
  • Total probability rule for expected value: E(X) = E(X | S1)P(S1) + E(X | S2)P(S2) + … for mutually exclusive, exhaustive scenarios
  • Joint probability (multiplication rule): P(AB) = P(A | B) × P(B)
  • Addition rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(AB)
  • Independent events: P(A | B) = P(A), so P(AB) = P(A) × P(B) — only then may probabilities simply be multiplied

Worked example

Problem. A one-year bond pays 100 unless the issuer defaults, in which case holders recover 40. There is a 30% chance of recession (R), when the default probability is 10%, and a 70% chance of expansion, when it is 2%. Find P(recession and default), P(default), P(recession or default) and the expected payoff.

By hand
  1. Multiplication rule: P(R and D) = P(D | R) × P(R) = 0.10 × 0.30 = 0.03.
  2. Total probability: P(D) = 0.10 × 0.30 + 0.02 × 0.70 = 0.030 + 0.014 = 0.044.
  3. Addition rule: P(R or D) = 0.30 + 0.044 − 0.03 = 0.314. The 0.03 sat inside both, so it comes off once.
  4. Conditional expected values: E(payoff | R) = 0.90 × 100 + 0.10 × 40 = 94.0. E(payoff | expansion) = 0.98 × 100 + 0.02 × 40 = 98.8.
  5. Total probability rule: E(payoff) = 0.30 × 94.0 + 0.70 × 98.8 = 28.20 + 69.16 = 97.36.
  6. Check by the short route: P(D) = 0.044, so E(payoff) = 0.956 × 100 + 0.044 × 40 = 95.60 + 1.76 = 97.36.
  7. Answer: P(R and D) = 3.0%; P(D) = 4.4%; P(R or D) = 31.4%; E(payoff) = 97.36.

Back to the story

Pascal priced the game by what could happen next, not by what had happened. That is the total probability rule for expected value, with the next round as the scenario.

At 2–1 the next round goes one of two equally likely ways, since the players are equally skilled.

The leader wins it (½): three wins, all 64. He loses it (½): 2–2, an even game worth 32 to each.

His share: ½ × 64 + ½ × 32 = 48. The other player gets 16. Pacioli’s 42.67 shortchanged the leader by 5.33.

The rule chains backwards. At 2–0: ½ × 64 + ½ × 48 = 56. At 1–0: ½ × 56 + ½ × 32 = 44, exactly the 44 pistoles Pascal gives in the same letter. Pacioli’s rule would hand that leader all 64 after a single round.

Pacioli looked back at rounds won; Pascal looked forward. A share of an unfinished game is the expected value of all the ways it could finish.

Exam traps
  • Reading Sx for a probability distribution. Sx divides by n − 1 as if the weights were a sample (0.2632 today); the probability-weighted SD is σx = 0.2619.
  • Probabilities that do not add to 1. Check every fork (0.60 + 0.40; 0.75 + 0.25) and the paths (0.45 + 0.15 + 0.10 + 0.30 = 1) before weighting anything.
  • Confusing a joint probability with a conditional one. P(default | recession) = 10%, but P(recession and default) = 3%: the second also needs the recession to happen.
  • Multiplying probabilities of events that are not independent. P(R) × P(D) = 0.30 × 0.044 = 1.32%, not the true 3%: default is likelier in a recession (10% against 4.4% overall).

2. Why. When the frequencies on the calculator stand for probabilities, why read σx rather than Sx?

  • σx is the smaller (0.2619 against 0.2632 today), and size is no reason to pick a formula.

  • It is still the wrong formula, and the gap depends on how you scale the weights: enter Pascal’s two outcomes, 64 and 32 (½ each), with weights 1 and 1, and Sx = 22.63 against the true 16.

  • Right. Weights / total = probabilities, so σx is exactly the probability-weighted standard deviation.

Explanation

σx divides the weighted squared deviations by the total weight (100), which turns each weight back into its probability: that is Σ P(Xi)[Xi − E(X)]2. Sx divides by 99, the n − 1 correction for estimating from a sample. Here there is no sample: the probabilities are the whole distribution.

4. Exam style. P(A) = 0.40, P(B) = 0.30 and P(A | B) = 0.50. The probability that A or B occurs is closest to:

  • Right: P(AB) = P(A | B) × P(B) = 0.50 × 0.30 = 0.15, and 0.40 + 0.30 − 0.15 = 0.55.

  • P(A) + P(B) with the joint probability not subtracted: the outcomes where both happen are counted twice.

  • Treats A and B as independent, P(AB) = 0.40 × 0.30 = 0.12. They are not: P(A | B) = 0.50 is not P(A) = 0.40.

Explanation

First the joint probability, by the multiplication rule: P(AB) = P(A | B) P(B) = 0.15. Then the addition rule takes the overlap off once: 0.40 + 0.30 − 0.15 = 0.55.

Multiplying P(A) × P(B) is allowed only when P(A | B) = P(A). Here learning that B happened raises A’s chance from 0.40 to 0.50, so they are dependent.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences, using Pascal’s game at 2–1: how does the total probability rule put a value on a future that has several possible paths?

Model answer

Split what can happen next into scenarios that cannot both happen and that cover every possibility, find the expected value inside each, then weight each by its probability and add. At 2–1 the next round is a win for the leader (½), worth all 64, or a loss (½), leaving an even game worth 32: ½ × 64 + ½ × 32 = 48.

A complete answer says: (1) split the future into mutually exclusive, exhaustive scenarios; (2) find the expected value within each scenario (the conditional expectation); (3) weight each by its probability and add.
