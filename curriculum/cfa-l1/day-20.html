__DATELINE__ · 20 min

When dividends grow fast for a few years and then settle down, value the fast years one by one and everything after them as a single Gordon lump at the end of the sprint.

From Day 13. A client puts most of her money into an account just before the manager’s best year. Compared with the time-weighted return, her money-weighted return will be:

  • Right. The money-weighted return is the IRR of her own cash flows, so it gives the most weight to the period when the most money was invested, here the strong year.

  • That is the case the other way round: a big deposit just before a bad year pulls the money-weighted return below the time-weighted one.

  • Only the time-weighted return strips out the client’s deposits and withdrawals. The money-weighted return keeps them, so their timing moves it.

Why

TWR chain-links the sub-period returns, so the size of the account in each period does not matter: it judges the manager. MWR is the IRR of the client’s deposits, withdrawals and ending value: it describes what she actually earned, timing included.

Microsoft’s 8-cent starter dividend

Microsoft went public in March 1986. For almost 17 years it paid no dividend at all. It kept its cash.

On 16 January 2003 it declared its first: 8 cents a share for the year, after a 2-for-1 stock split announced the same day (16 cents on the old shares). On about 10.8 billion shares that came to roughly $864 million. Reports at the time called it a “starter” dividend.

Then the cash started to flow. On 20 July 2004 Microsoft announced a one-time special dividend of $3 a share, about $32 billion, paid on 2 December 2004. And the regular dividend kept climbing. By September 2025 it was 91 cents a quarter: $3.64 a year.

From 8 cents to $3.64 is 45.5 times as much. That is growth of about 18% a year, compounded, for more than 22 years.

Day 19’s model needs one growth rate that lasts forever and stays below the required return. 18% a year is above any sensible required return, and it cannot last forever either. So how do you value a dividend that sprints for a while and then slows?

The idea

Split the future in two.

  • The sprint. The first n years, when dividends grow fast, at gS. There are only a few of them, so list each one and discount it on its own.
  • The cruise. From year n + 1 on, growth settles at a long-run rate gL, below r, forever. That is Day 19’s growing perpetuity, so one formula sums all of it.

The cruise is summed into a single lump dated at the end of the sprint, time n. It is called the terminal value, Vn.

An everyday picture: you buy the share, collect the sprint dividends, and sell at the end of year n. Your buyer is getting an ordinary constant-growth stock, so she pays the Gordon price. That price is Vn.

Today, then, the share is worth the dividends you collect plus the price you sell for, each discounted from its own date. The fast rate is used only for the years it lasts. The rate that runs forever is the slow one.

Why it works

Start from Day 19. A share is worth the present value of all its future dividends: V0 = D1/(1 + r) + D2/(1 + r)2 + D3/(1 + r)3 + …

Cut the list after year n. D1 to Dn are the sprint: only a few, so discount each one separately, as Day 10 did with uneven cash flows.

Look at what is left, standing at time n. The rest is Dn+1, Dn+2, …, each (1 + gL) times the one before, forever. The first of them is one period away. That is Day 19’s picture exactly, moved n years into the future.

So use Day 19’s formula there. Gordon always gives a value one period before the first dividend it counts. Here that dividend is Dn+1, so Vn = Dn+1/(r − gL) is a value dated n, not today. It needs gL < r: that is why the sprint rate cannot be used here.

The top is Dn+1, not Dn. Dn is the last sprint dividend, already counted. The first cruise dividend grows at the new, slower rate: Dn+1 = Dn × (1 + gL).

Bring Vn back to today. It is one lump sum at time n, so divide by (1 + r)n, as on Day 2. n periods, no more.

Add. Values at the same date can be added (cash-flow additivity, Day 10). That gives the formula below.

One shortcut. Dn and Vn sit on the same date, so they can travel together as one cash flow, Dn + Vn, at time n. That is what goes into the CF worksheet.

In plain words: count the fast dividends one by one, then pretend you sell the share when the sprint ends, for the price a Day 19 buyer would pay, and bring that price back to today too.

V0 = D1/(1 + r) + D2/(1 + r)2 + … + Dn/(1 + r)n + Vn/(1 + r)n

Vn = Dn+1 / (r − gL), with Dn+1 = Dn × (1 + gL)

V0
what the share is worth today
Dt
the dividend paid at the end of year t; in the sprint, Dt = D0 × (1 + gS)t
gS
the fast growth rate of the first n years
n
the number of fast-growth years: the end of the sprint
gL
the long-run growth rate from year n + 1 on, forever; must be below r
r
the required return on the equity, as a decimal
Vn
terminal value: what all dividends from year n + 1 on are worth at time n
Memorize
  • Two-stage DDM: V0 = Σt=1..n Dt/(1 + r)t + [Dn+1/(r − gL)]/(1 + r)n
  • Terminal value at the end of the high-growth stage: Vn = Dn+1 / (r − gL), with Dn+1 = Dn × (1 + gL)
  • How many periods to discount the terminal value Vn: n periods (it is a value at time n)

Worked example

Problem. D0 = 1.00. Dividends grow 10% a year for 2 years, then 4% a year forever. The required return is 8%. Value the share by hand.

By hand
  1. The sprint: D1 = 1.00 × 1.10 = 1.1000; D2 = 1.10 × 1.10 = 1.2100.
  2. The first cruise dividend, at the slow rate: D3 = 1.21 × 1.04 = 1.2584.
  3. Terminal value at t = 2: V2 = D3/(r − gL) = 1.2584/0.04 = 31.4600.
  4. PV of D1 = 1.10/1.08 = 1.0185.
  5. D2 and V2 share a date, so add them: 1.21 + 31.46 = 32.67. PV = 32.67/1.082 = 32.67/1.1664 = 28.0093.
  6. V0 = 1.0185 + 28.0093 = 29.0278.

Answer: V0 = 29.03 a share.

Back to the story

Microsoft’s 18% a year is the whole problem. Put it into Day 19’s formula with a 9% required return and r − g comes out at −9%: the formula hands back a negative price.

The two-stage model uses the fast rate only for the years it lasts. After that a slower rate, below r, takes over, and Gordon works again, from the end of the sprint.

The walkthrough shows where the value sits. Its three fast-growing dividends are worth $1.82 today; the terminal value is worth $17.51. Of the $19.34 total, about 91% comes from the years after the sprint.

That is the point for a company like Microsoft in 2003. An 8-cent starter dividend said little about what the shares were worth: in a two-stage view, most of the value lies in the long, steady stretch after the fast years.

Exam traps
  • Discounting the terminal value n + 1 periods. Vn is already a value at time n. In the walkthrough, dividing 22.68 by 1.094 instead of 1.093 gives V0 = 17.8907 instead of 19.3368.
  • Putting Dn instead of Dn+1 on top of the terminal value: .864 / .04 = 21.60, and V0 drops to 18.5028.
  • Growing Dn+1 at the sprint rate. D4 = .864 × 1.20 = 1.0368 makes V3 = 25.92 and V0 = 21.8386. The first cruise dividend grows at gL.
  • Forgetting to add Vn to the year-n cash flow in the CF worksheet: the NPV is then 1.8236, the sprint dividends alone.

2. Why. In a two-stage model the terminal value Vn = Dn+1/(r − gL) is discounted n periods, not n + 1. Why?

  • Dn+1 is paid at the end of year n + 1, one period after n. The timing of the dividend is not the reason; the timing of the formula’s answer is.

  • Right. D1/(r − g) is a value today, one period before D1. Moved forward, Dn+1/(r − gL) is a value at time n, one period before Dn+1.

  • It is an ordinary growing perpetuity: its first payment, Dn+1, comes one period after time n. A perpetuity due would also count Dn, which belongs to the sprint.

Explanation

Every Gordon value is dated one period before its first dividend. So Vn is a lump sum sitting at time n, and a lump sum at time n is brought to today by dividing by (1 + r)n. One more period would discount the cruise twice for the same year.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: how does a two-stage dividend discount model value a share, and why is the terminal value discounted only n periods?

Model answer

It discounts each high-growth dividend on its own, then adds the present value of a terminal value: Day 19’s Gordon value of all the later dividends, Dn+1/(r − gL), which needs gL below r. Gordon gives a value one period before its first dividend, so the terminal value is dated n and is discounted n periods.

A complete answer says: (1) the PV of each high-growth dividend, one by one; (2) plus the PV of a Gordon terminal value at the start of stable growth, with gL < r; (3) the terminal value is a value at time n, so it is discounted n periods.
