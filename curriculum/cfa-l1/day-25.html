From Day 24. A fund’s five annual returns: 7%, −4%, 12%, 1%, 9%. What is the sample standard deviation? As a percent, to two decimals.

Solution

From Day 18. A 180-day T-bill is quoted at a 4.00% discount rate (360-day year). Its bond-equivalent yield is closest to:

  • That is 4.00% × 365/360: the year is fixed, but the return is still figured on the face value instead of the price paid.

  • That is figured on the price paid (2/98), but annualized over a 360-day year: 360/180 × 2/98. The bond-equivalent yield uses 365.

  • Right. Price = 100 × (1 − 180/360 × 0.04) = 98.00; BEY = 365/180 × 2/98 = 4.1383%.

Why

The bond-equivalent yield is a 365-day add-on rate: the gain (100 − 98 = 2) divided by the money paid (98), scaled by 365/days. A discount rate is figured on face value over 360 days, so it always understates it.

The year the cushion failed

On the morning of Tuesday 13 September 2022, the US government reported that consumer prices were 8.3% higher than a year earlier, more than forecast.

By the close the S&P 500 had fallen 4.32%, 177.72 points to 3,932.69: its worst day since June 2020. Bonds offered no shelter. The two-year Treasury yield jumped from about 3.57% to 3.74%, and when yields rise, bond prices fall.

The whole year went the same way. The S&P 500 lost 18.11% including dividends. The Bloomberg US Aggregate bond index lost 13.01%. It was the only year in the Aggregate’s history in which both fell by more than 10%.

That stung, because the classic 60/40 portfolio (60% stocks, 40% bonds) rests on one idea: when stocks fall, bonds rise and soften the blow.

So how do you measure whether two assets really move against each other? And what does that number say about the years leading up to 2022?

The idea

Two questions about any pair of assets: do they move together, and how tightly?

Picture a scatter plot: one dot per year, the stock return across, the bond return up. Dots climbing left to right: they move together. Falling: opposite. A shapeless cloud: no pattern.

Covariance turns that picture into one number. Its sign is the direction: positive, together; negative, opposite.

Correlation rescales it to lie between −1 and +1, so it also says how tight the pattern is. +1: every dot on one rising straight line. −1: one falling line. 0: no straight-line pattern at all.

An everyday picture: a beach with an umbrella stand and an ice-cream stand. Rain sells umbrellas; sun sells ice cream. Each alone is bumpy; own both and the bumps cancel. That negative correlation is the cushion a 60/40 investor hopes bonds give stocks.

One limit from the start: r only sees straight lines. Take X = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 and Y = X2 = 4, 1, 0, 1, 4. Y is fixed exactly by X, yet r = 0.

Why it works

Start from Day 24. Sample variance multiplies each deviation by itself and divides by n − 1: sX2 = Σ(Xi − X̄)(Xi − X̄) / (n − 1).

Swap one copy for Y. Use Y’s deviation in the same year: (Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ). Each year now casts a vote: both above their means, (+)(+) = +; both below, (−)(−) = +; one of each, −. Big moves cast big votes.

Average the votes with n − 1, as on Day 24: that is the sample covariance sXY. Positive: mostly together. Negative: mostly opposite.

The snag. Covariance is in %2 and grows with volatility: double every X in the worked example and sXY doubles (−18.33 to −36.67), yet the link is no tighter.

The fix. Measure each deviation in its own standard deviations, zX = (Xi − X̄) / sX (zY likewise), then average the products: r = ΣzXzY / (n − 1) = sXY / (sX sY). Units cancel, and doubling X doubles sXY and sX alike, so r stays −0.93.

Why ±1 is the limit. Each set of z’s squared adds to n − 1 (z’s have variance 1). Squares can’t be negative, so Σ(zX − zY)2 = 2(n − 1)(1 − r) ≥ 0 forces r ≤ 1, with r = 1 only if every dot sits on one rising line. Σ(zX + zY)2 = 2(n − 1)(1 + r) ≥ 0 forces r ≥ −1.

The BA II Plus route. It has no covariance key, so turn the formula round: sXY = r × Sx × Sy. Use the sample Sx and Sy: the n − 1’s cancel inside r, but not in the covariance.

Spearman, as insurance. Rank each series (1 = highest) and take r of the ranks. Ranks 1 to n always have the same spread, so r collapses to a shortcut in d, each observation’s rank gap. Only order counts, so one wild year weighs less.

In plain words: covariance asks each year “were both better than usual, or both worse?” and adds up the answers. Correlation turns that tally into a grade from −1 (always opposite) to +1 (always together).

Memorize
  • Sample covariance: sXY = Σ (Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ) / (n − 1)
  • Correlation coefficient: rXY = sXY / (sX × sY)
  • Covariance from correlation (BA II Plus route): sXY = r × sX × sY
  • Range and meaning of correlation: −1 ≤ r ≤ +1; 0 means no LINEAR relation, not no relation
  • Spearman rank correlation (2027 coverage uncertain): rs = 1 − 6 Σ di2 / [n (n2 − 1)], di = difference between the two ranks of observation i

Worked example

Problem. Four years of returns (%): stock fund X = 10, −5, 15, 0; bond fund Y = 2, 6, 1, 3. Find the sample covariance, both sample standard deviations and the correlation; then the Spearman rank correlation.

By hand
  1. Means: X̄ = 20 / 4 = 5; Ȳ = 12 / 4 = 3.
  2. Deviations: X: +5, −10, +10, −5. Y: −1, +3, −2, 0.
  3. Cross-products: −5, −30, −20, 0. Sum = −55. sXY = −55 / (4 − 1) = −18.3333 (%2).
  4. sX2 = (25 + 100 + 100 + 25) / 3 = 83.3333, so sX = 9.1287. sY2 = (1 + 9 + 4 + 0) / 3 = 4.6667, so sY = 2.1602.
  5. r = −18.3333 / (9.1287 × 2.1602) = −0.9297: a strong negative linear relation.
  6. Spearman. Ranks (1 = highest): X 2, 4, 1, 3; Y 3, 1, 4, 2. d = −1, +3, −3, +1; Σd2 = 20.
  7. rs = 1 − 6 × 20 / [4 × (16 − 1)] = 1 − 120/60 = −1: the two rankings are exactly reversed.
  8. Read it: when the stock fund did badly, the bond fund did well: the cushion.

Back to the story

For 2018–2022: covariance +146.29 (%2), correlation +0.78. In those five years stocks and bonds mostly moved the same way.

Look at the votes: in 2018, 2019, 2020 and 2022 both sat on the same side of their averages. Only 2021 split them: stocks +28.71%, bonds −1.54%.

That is the cushion problem. Positive correlation means bonds tend to fall when stocks fall, so they soften a crash less. 2022 was the extreme: a 60/40 mix of the two lost about 0.6 × 18.11 + 0.4 × 13.01 = 16.07%.

Three cautions. One year carries the result: 2022’s vote is about two-thirds of the total. For 2018–2021 alone r is 0.38: still positive, but far weaker. Five annual returns are a small sample: the number describes those years, it is not a law. And r never says why: on 13 September it was one piece of news, an inflation report, that hit both.

Exam traps
  • Mixing population SDs with a sample covariance: r × σx × σy on today’s data gives 117.03, not 146.29. Use Sx and Sy.
  • Reading r = 0 as “no relationship”. It means no linear relationship: Y = X2 above is tied to X and has r = 0.
  • Treating correlation as causation. Two series can correlate by chance or because a third factor drives both; the curriculum calls both spurious correlation.
  • Comparing covariances across pairs. A bigger covariance may just mean more volatile assets; compare correlations, which are scale-free.

2. Why. Why do analysts compare how closely different pairs of assets move together using correlation rather than covariance?

  • Right. Double one asset’s returns and the covariance doubles though the link is the same; r does not move, so r can be compared across any pairs.

  • It can be negative: the worked example’s covariance is −18.33. Covariance and correlation always share the same sign.

  • Both can be sample statistics. Inside r the n − 1 cancels anyway; the reason is units and scale, not which divisor is used.

Explanation

r = sXY / (sX sY) measures each deviation in its own standard deviations before multiplying. The walkthrough’s covariance (146.29) is eight times the size of the worked example’s (−18.33), yet its correlation is the weaker one: 0.78 against −0.93. The big covariance mostly reflects bigger swings, not a tighter link.

3. Twist: rank correlation. Six funds’ returns (%) in two years, fund by fund: Year 1 = 12.5, 8.1, 15.2, 3.4, 9.9, 6.0; Year 2 = 10.1, 9.0, 11.8, 2.2, 6.5, 7.7. What is the Spearman rank correlation between the two years? To four decimals.

rs
Hint Rank each year on its own (1 = highest), take d = rank in Year 1 minus rank in Year 2 for each fund, add up the d2, then rs = 1 − 6Σd2 / [n(n2 − 1)] with n = 6.
Solution

Ranks, Year 1: 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 5. Year 2: 2, 3, 1, 6, 5, 4. d = 0, 1, 0, 0, −2, 1; d2 = 0, 1, 0, 0, 4, 1; Σd2 = 6.

rs = 1 − 6 × 6 / [6 × (36 − 1)] = 1 − 36/210 = 0.8286.

BA II Plus: 1 − 6 × 6 ÷ ( 6 × 35 ) = → 0.8286 (AOS mode). TI-84 Plus: 1 − 6 × 6 ÷ ( 6 × 35 ) ENTER → .8286.

Check that Spearman is just r on the ranks: enter the two rank lists as X and Y (BA II Plus LIN, or L1 and L2 with 8:LinReg(a+bx) on the TI-84) and r = 0.8286. On the raw returns r = 0.8659. Ranking from the lowest instead gives the same answer: every d just flips sign.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what does a correlation of +0.78 between stocks and bonds tell a 60/40 investor, and what would a correlation of 0 not tell them?

Model answer

At +0.78 the two tend to move the same way, so when stocks fall, bonds tend to fall too and cushion the loss less. A correlation of 0 would only mean no straight-line relation, not no relation at all, and no correlation, whatever its size, proves that one causes the other.

A complete answer says: (1) positive r = the two tend to move together, so bonds cushion stock losses less; (2) r measures the strength of a linear relation on a scale from −1 to +1; (3) zero means no linear relation, not no relation; (4) correlation is not causation.
