Paul the octopus, 8 for 8

The 2010 World Cup, in South Africa. At the Sea Life aquarium in Oberhausen, Germany, keepers lowered two boxes into the tank of an octopus named Paul before each of Germany’s matches. Each box held food and carried one team’s flag. The box he opened first was his “pick”.

News outlets reported his picks before the games. “Psychic octopus (named Paul) picks Germany over England in World Cup,” ran one headline in June.

He picked Germany to beat Australia, Ghana, England, Argentina and Uruguay, and Germany beat all five. He picked Serbia for the match Germany lost to Serbia, and Spain for the semi-final that Germany lost to Spain.

Then he picked Spain over the Netherlands for the final on 11 July 2010. Spain won.

Paul died in his tank on 26 October 2010, aged about two, with a perfect World Cup record: eight correct calls out of eight.

Eight right in a row. Was Paul psychic? And if he was only guessing, how surprised should we be?

The idea

Many finance questions are yes-or-no, asked again and again: does the stock rise, does the bond default, was the analyst right? Each go is a Bernoulli trial: success with probability p, failure with probability 1 − p.

Run n independent trials with the same p and count the successes. That count, X, is a binomial random variable: anything from 0 up to n.

Flip a coin 8 times. Every particular sequence, say HHTHHHTH, has the same chance: 0.58 = 1/256. But 8 heads happens in one sequence and 6 heads in 28 (the 2 tails can fall anywhere), so 6 heads is 28 times as likely.

That is the whole formula: (number of ways) × (chance of one way).

A binomial tree for a stock works the same way: each period the price goes up (probability p) or down, and the ending node depends only on how many up-moves there were. So each node’s probability is a binomial probability.

Also today: the uniform distribution, where every outcome is equally likely. Discrete: k outcomes, 1/k each (a fair die: 1/6). Continuous, from a to b: the chance of a slice is its share of the range, (x2 − x1) / (b − a); the mean is the midpoint, (a + b) / 2.

Why it works

Start with one order. A fund beats its benchmark with probability 0.7 each year, independently. Beat, beat, then miss: independent events multiply (Day 26), so 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.147.

Every order has the same chance. Beat-miss-beat and miss-beat-beat shuffle the same three factors: 0.147 each. Each success brings a p and each failure a (1 − p), so any one order of x successes in n trials has probability px(1 − p)n − x.

Add the orders. Only one order can happen, so they are mutually exclusive and add. Exactly 2 beats in 3 years: 3 × 0.147 = 0.441. In general, P(X = x) = (number of orders) × px(1 − p)n − x.

Count the orders. Choosing Paul’s 2 misses fixes his 6 hits. The first miss can be any of 8 matches, the second any of the other 7: 8 × 7 = 56 (the multiplication rule). That is a permutation, 8P2 = 8!/6!: it counts ordered choices.

Remove the double count. “Match 3, then match 5” and “match 5, then match 3” are the same two misses. Each pair was counted 2! = 2 times: 56 / 2 = 28 orders. In general r items can be listed in r! orders, so nCr = nPr / r! = n! / [(n − r)! r!]. Choosing 2 misses or 6 hits is the same choice: 8C2 = 8C6 = 28.

Mean and variance. One trial is a Bernoulli variable: 1 with probability p, else 0. By Day 26’s formulas its mean is 1 × p + 0 × (1 − p) = p, and its variance is p(1 − p)2 + (1 − p)(0 − p)2 = p(1 − p)[(1 − p) + p] = p(1 − p). X adds up n independent trials (no covariance terms), so the means add to np and the variances to np(1 − p).

In plain words: count the ways it can happen, then multiply by the chance of any one way.

P(X = x) = nCx × px × (1 − p)n − x

nCx = n! / [(n − x)! × x!]

E(X) = np; Var(X) = np(1 − p)

X
the number of successes in n trials: a binomial random variable
n
the number of trials, independent, each with the same p
x
the number of successes asked about, from 0 to n
p
the probability of success on one trial; 1 − p is the probability of failure
nCx
“n choose x”: how many orders hold exactly x successes (a combination)
n!
n factorial, n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1; by definition 0! = 1, so 8C8 = 1
Memorize
  • Binomial probability of exactly x successes in n trials: P(X = x) = nCx × px × (1 − p)(n − x)
  • Mean and variance of a binomial random variable: E(X) = n p; Var(X) = n p (1 − p)
  • Combinations (order does not matter): nCr = n! / [(n − r)! × r!]
  • Permutations (order matters): nPr = n! / (n − r)!
  • Continuous uniform distribution on [a, b]: P(x1 ≤ X ≤ x2) = (x2 − x1) / (b − a); mean = (a + b) / 2

Worked example

Problem. (a) A stock moves up with probability 0.6 each period, independently. Over 4 periods, find P(exactly 3 up-moves), the probability of the tree’s 3-up ending node, and the mean and variance of the number of up-moves. (b) How many ways can you choose 3 funds from 8, and how many ordered podiums (1st, 2nd, 3rd)? (c) A return is uniform between −4% and 10%: find P(0% ≤ R ≤ 5%) and the mean.

By hand
  1. (a) 4C3 = 4! / (1! × 3!) = 4 paths with three ups: UUUD, UUDU, UDUU, DUUU.
  2. Each path: 0.63 × 0.4 = 0.216 × 0.4 = 0.0864. P(X = 3) = 4 × 0.0864 = 0.3456.
  3. E(X) = np = 4 × 0.6 = 2.4; Var(X) = np(1 − p) = 4 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.96.
  4. (b) Order does not matter: 8C3 = 8! / (5! × 3!) = 56. Order matters: 8P3 = 8! / 5! = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336 = 56 × 3!, since each group of 3 fills the podium 6 ways.
  5. (c) P = (5 − 0) / (10 − (−4)) = 5/14 = 35.71%; mean = (−4 + 10) / 2 = 3%.

Back to the story

If Paul was guessing, each pick was a coin flip: n = 8, p = 0.5. Then P(8 of 8) = 8C8 × 0.58 = 1/256 = 0.39%. For one guesser, that is rare.

But Paul was far from the only animal, pundit or fan making picks, and nobody writes about the ones who failed. Take 100 coin-flip guessers calling the same eight matches. All 100 fall short with probability (255/256)100 = 0.6761, so at least one goes 8 for 8 with probability 1 − 0.6761 = 32.4%. With 256 guessers, expect one perfect record: np = 256 × 1/256 = 1.

A guesser averages np = 4 right, give or take √2 ≈ 1.41, and gets 6 or more right 14.45% of the time.

A streak picked out after the fact is weak evidence of skill, for an octopus or for a fund manager with one lucky run.

Exam traps
  • A permutation where order does not matter. 8P6 = 20,160 counts every set of 6 hits 6! = 720 times over; the binomial needs 8C6 = 20,160 / 720 = 28. Ask: would swapping two items give a different outcome? If not, use C.
  • Dropping (1 − p)n − x. Exactly 3 ups in 4 periods also means exactly 1 down. Leave it out and you get 4 × 0.63 = 0.864, not 0.3456. With p = 0.5 the two factors merge into 0.58 and the second one hides; with any other p, both must be there.
  • “Exactly” when the question says “at least”. Exactly 6 of 8 is 10.94%; at least 6 (6, 7 or 8) is 14.45%. On the TI-84, binomcdf(n,p,x) adds everything up to and including x, so at least 6 is 1 − binomcdf(8,.5,5). Subtracting binomcdf(8,.5,6) instead gives 3.52%, which is “at least 7”.

2. Why. In P(X = x) = nCx × px × (1 − p)n − x, what job does nCx do?

  • The formula’s answer is a probability between 0 and 1 (Paul’s 6 of 8: 0.1094). nCx is a count, 28 there, not a unit conversion.

  • The binomial assumes independent trials: that is why one order’s probability is a plain product of p’s and (1 − p)’s. With dependent trials the formula does not apply at all.

  • Right. The orders are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities add, and adding the same number nCx times is multiplying by nCx.

Explanation

Exactly 2 beats in 3 years can happen as BBM, BMB or MBB. Each has probability 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.147, and only one can happen, so P = 0.147 + 0.147 + 0.147 = 3 × 0.147 = 0.441. The 3 is 3C2.

3. Twist: at least one. A portfolio holds 10 bonds. Each has a 5% chance of defaulting this year, independently of the others. What is the probability that at least one defaults? As a percent, to two decimals.

%

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: was Paul psychic? Use the binomial formula, and say why his record is weak evidence of skill.

Model answer

Probably not. If every pick was a coin flip, P(8 of 8) = 8C8 × 0.58 = 1/256, rare for one guesser; but with many animals, pundits and fans guessing, someone was likely to go 8 for 8 (32.4% with just 100 guessers), and only the winner made the news. One streak picked out after the fact is weak evidence of skill.

A complete answer says: (1) under pure guessing, P(8 of 8) = 0.58 = 1/256; (2) with many guessers a perfect run somewhere is likely, and only the winner gets noticed; (3) a streak chosen after the fact is weak evidence of skill, for an octopus or a fund manager.
