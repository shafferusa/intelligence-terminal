Greece pays for the days, not the coupons

March 2012, deep in Greece’s debt crisis. In a deal known as the PSI, for “private-sector involvement”, private holders of almost €200 billion (face value) of Greek government bonds handed them in.

For every €100 of face value they received €31.50 of new Greek bonds and €15 of short-term notes from the eurozone’s rescue fund, the European Financial Stability Facility (EFSF), plus GDP-linked warrants. That is €46.50 of new debt for every €100 of old: a 53.5% cut in face value.

One item was settled on its own. Each old bond had been building up interest since its last coupon date. That interest, counted up to 24 February 2012, was paid separately, in six-month EFSF zero-coupon notes due on 12 September 2012.

Here is the odd part. No coupon was due on the day the old bonds were cancelled. So why did Greece owe bondholders any interest at all? And how much is owed for part of a coupon period?

The idea

A bond pays its coupon on fixed dates, but it trades on any day. Whoever holds it on the payment date gets the whole coupon, even if they bought it last week.

Think of a job paid once a month. Quit on the 10th and you are still owed for 10 days, even though payday is weeks away. A bond seller is in the same spot: they held the bond for part of the period, so they earned part of the coming coupon.

So a buyer between coupon dates pays the full price (also called the invoice or “dirty” price): what the bond’s remaining cash flows are worth on the settlement day, at the market yield. Inside it sits the seller’s share of the coming coupon, the accrued interest (AI). Take AI out and you have the flat price (the quoted or “clean” price), the one on screens.

Everything turns on one fraction, t/T: t is the days since the last coupon, T the days in the whole coupon period. Government bonds count actual calendar days (actual/actual). Corporate bonds pretend every month has 30 days and the year 360 (30/360), so a half-year period is always 180 days.

Why it works

Start on a coupon date (Day 15). Just after a coupon is paid, the bond is worth the PV of what is left: PV = PMT/(1 + r) + PMT/(1 + r)2 + … + (PMT + FV)/(1 + r)N, with r the yield per coupon period.

Let t days pass. Settlement is t/T of a period later, so a cash flow that was k periods away is now only k − t/T away.

Rediscount each one. Its discount factor becomes 1/(1 + r)k − t/T = [1/(1 + r)k] × (1 + r)t/T. Every term gains the same factor, (1 + r)t/T.

Pull it out of the sum. PVfull = PV × (1 + r)t/T. That is Day 1’s FV formula with N = t/T: the coupon-date value, grown at the yield for the part of the period that has gone.

Split off the seller’s share. The next coupon is earned evenly over the T days, and the seller held the bond for t of them: AI = (t/T) × PMT. By convention it is a straight-line share, not compounded.

What is left is quoted. PVflat = PVfull − AI.

Why quote flat? The full price climbs all period, then drops by the coupon on payment day. For the walkthrough bond below it is 106.5811 just before the 15 August coupon and 104.0811 just after. The flat price is 104.0811 on both sides. No saw-tooth, so moves in the quoted price show the market, not the calendar.

Fine print. AI grows in a straight line while the full price compounds. So a 4% semiannual bond priced at exactly 100 on its coupon dates (4% yield) shows a flat price of 99.9950 halfway between them, not 100.

In plain words: you pay what the bond was worth on its last payday, grown for the days since. Part of that is the seller’s share of the next payment, and the price on the screen leaves that part out.

PVfull = PV × (1 + r)t/T

AI = (t / T) × PMT

PVflat = PVfull − AI

Memorize
  • PVfull = PVlast coupon date × (1 + r)t/T
  • AI = (t / T) × PMT (t = days since last coupon, T = days in the coupon period)
  • PVflat = PVfull − AI
  • Day counts: government bonds actual/actual; corporate bonds 30/360
  • BA II Plus BOND worksheet: PRI = flat price per 100 of par; full price = PRI + AI

Worked example

Problem. A corporate bond pays a 6% coupon semiannually (15 March and 15 September) and matures on 15 March 2028. It settles on 2 June 2027 at a 5% yield. Day count 30/360. Find the full price, accrued interest and flat price per 100.

By hand
  1. Days since the last coupon (30/360, 15 Mar → 2 Jun): (6 − 3) × 30 + (2 − 15) = 77, out of 180.
  2. Per period: coupon 6/2 = 3, yield 5%/2 = 2.5%. Two coupons are left after 15 March 2027.
  3. PV on 15 March: 3/1.025 + 103/1.0252 = 2.9268 + 98.0369 = 100.9637.
  4. Full price: 100.9637 × 1.02577/180 = 100.9637 × 1.010619 = 102.0358.
  5. AI = (77/180) × 3 = 1.2833.
  6. Flat price = 102.0358 − 1.2833 = 100.7525.
  7. Actual days would be 79 of 184, giving AI = 1.2880: the day-count convention changes the answer.

Back to the story

A coupon is earned day by day, by whoever holds the bond. Greece ended the old bonds partway through their coupon periods, so their holders had already earned a slice of coupons that would now never be paid. Greece paid that slice, the accrued interest, on its own, in the six-month EFSF notes.

An illustration (not an actual Greek bond): €100,000 of a bond paying a 4.5% coupon once a year, last paid on 20 August 2011. By 24 February 2012 it had run 188 of the period’s 366 days (actual/actual, and 2012 had a 29 February). AI = 4,500 × 188/366 = €2,311.48.

That is today’s (t/T) × PMT, nothing more. The 53.5% cut fell on face value; the interest already earned was its own line, just as every bond trade splits the full price into AI and the flat price.

Exam traps
  • Quoting the full price as the flat price, or the reverse. Quotes are flat; the buyer pays full = flat + AI. The BA II Plus PRI is flat, so add AI for the full price.
  • Using 30/360 for a government bond (or actual days for a corporate one). Check the day count first: it changes both t and T.
  • Using the annual yield or coupon. t/T is a fraction of a coupon period, so r is the periodic yield (2% for a 4% semiannual yield) and PMT the periodic coupon.

2. Why. Why does the buyer of a bond between coupon dates pay the seller accrued interest?

  • No: AI goes to the seller, not a dealer, and it is fixed by a formula, (t/T) × coupon. Dealers earn the bid–ask spread.

  • No: default risk is priced through the yield. AI depends only on the days and the coupon, and is the same for a safe issuer or a risky one.

  • Right. The seller held the bond for t of the T days; AI hands them that earned share now, and the buyer gets it back inside the full coupon on payment day.

Explanation

The issuer pays the whole coupon to whoever holds the bond on the payment date. Without AI the buyer would collect interest for days the seller owned the bond. So the buyer pays (t/T) × PMT now and receives the full PMT later, keeping only the share for the days he holds it.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what is the difference between a bond’s full price and its flat price, and why are bonds quoted flat?

Model answer

The full price is what the buyer pays: the bond’s value on the last coupon date grown at the yield to settlement, and it includes accrued interest, the seller’s (t/T) share of the coming coupon; the flat price is the full price minus that AI. Bonds are quoted flat because the full price drops by a coupon on every payment date, while the flat price does not, so moves in the quote reflect yield changes rather than the calendar.

A complete answer says: (1) full = flat + AI, and the full price is what changes hands; (2) AI = (t/T) × PMT is the seller’s earned share of the next coupon; (3) the flat price avoids the jump on each coupon date, so its moves reflect yield changes.
