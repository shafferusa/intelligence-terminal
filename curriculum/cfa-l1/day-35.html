Warm-up · from earlier lessons

Two quick ones, from Days 34 and 24, so they stick. First try counts.

From Day 34. An investor with a risk-aversion coefficient A = 5 can hold T-bills at 4% or a risky portfolio with an expected return of 11% and a standard deviation of 16%. What share of her money should go in the risky portfolio? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint w* = [E(Ri) − Rf] / (A × σi2), with decimals in. Square the σ.
Solution

w* = (0.11 − 0.04) / (5 × 0.162) = 0.07 / (5 × 0.0256) = 0.07 / 0.128 = 0.5469 = 54.69%, with 45.31% in T-bills. That mix scores U = 5.91%, better than all T-bills (4.00%) or all risky (4.60%). (Forget to square σ and you get 8.75%.)

BA II Plus: ( .11 − .04 ) ÷ ( 5 × .16 x² ) = → 0.5469; × 100 = → 54.6875.

TI-84 Plus: ( .11 − .04 ) ÷ ( 5 × .16 x² ) ENTER → .5469; × 100 ENTER → 54.6875.

From Day 24. A fund’s five annual returns: 9%, −3%, 5%, 12% and −8%. Their mean absolute deviation is closest to:

  • That is the sample standard deviation, √(278 / 4): it squares the deviations. MAD averages their plain sizes.

  • That divides 34 by n − 1 = 4. The n − 1 belongs to the sample variance; MAD divides by n.

  • Right. Mean 15 / 5 = 3%. Distances from it: 6, 6, 2, 9, 11, adding to 34. 34 / 5 = 6.80%.

Why

MAD = Σ |Xi − X̄| / n: the average distance from the mean. The signs must go, because signed deviations always add to zero: +6 − 6 + 2 + 9 − 11 = 0. The standard deviation squares first, which weights the big misses more, so it comes out larger.

BA II Plus: 6 + 6 + 2 + 9 + 11 = ÷ 5 = → 6.8000. TI-84 Plus: ( 6 + 6 + 2 + 9 + 11 ) ÷ 5 ENTER → 6.8000.

What this week was about

This week went from single outcomes to whole distributions, then to portfolios. Day 29 counted: a binomial probability is the number of orders times the chance of one order, nCx px(1 − p)n − x. Day 30 measured distance in standard deviations, z = (X − μ) / σ, so one table serves every normal curve, and showed where real returns break the bell: skew pulls the mean away from the median, and excess kurtosis fattens the tails. Day 31 turned a price path into a sum of log returns, ln(P1 / P0), which makes prices lognormal and lets Monte Carlo simulation deal out thousands of possible years. Days 32 to 34 built portfolios: returns average but risks do not (the cross term), one mix has the least variance, and utility, U = E(r) − ½Aσ2, picks each investor’s point on the capital allocation line.

Learning Module 11, on financial data science, has no calculations, so this math course does not test it.

The week’s formulas

Memorize · Days 29–34
  • Day 29. P(X = x) = nCx × px × (1 − p)(n − x); E(X) = np; Var(X) = np(1 − p).
  • Day 29. Combinations (order does not matter): nCr = n! / [(n − r)! × r!]. Permutations (order matters): nPr = n! / (n − r)!. Continuous uniform on [a, b]: P(x1 ≤ X ≤ x2) = (x2 − x1) / (b − a); mean = (a + b) / 2.
  • Day 30. z = (X − μ) / σ. Bands: 68% ≈ μ ± 1σ; 90% = μ ± 1.645σ; 95% = μ ± 1.96σ; 99% = μ ± 2.58σ. One tail: 5% → 1.645; 2.5% → 1.96; 1% → 2.33.
  • Day 30. Positive skew (long right tail): mean > median > mode; negative skew (long left tail): mean < median < mode. Excess kurtosis = kurtosis − 3; above 0 (leptokurtic) means fatter tails than the normal. Value at risk: the minimum loss expected a given small % of the time, a left-tail percentile.
  • Day 31. rcc = ln(P1 / P0) = ln(1 + HPR); log returns add, r0,T = r1 + r2 + … + rT; PT = P0 × er0,T. If ln(PT) is normal, PT is lognormal: bounded below by 0 and skewed right.
  • Day 31. Monte Carlo: specify the model and the distribution of each random input → draw random values → compute the outcome → repeat many times → summarize. Historical simulation replays the actual past returns; bootstrap resampling draws from them at random, WITH replacement.
  • Day 32. E(Rp) = Σ wi × E(Ri); σp2 = w12σ12 + w22σ22 + 2w1w2ρ12σ1σ2, with Cov12 = ρ12 × σ1 × σ2.
  • Day 32. From a joint probability table: Cov(RA, RB) = Σ P × [RA − E(RA)] × [RB − E(RB)], with no n − 1.
  • Day 33. w1* = (σ22 − Cov12) / (σ12 + σ22 − 2Cov12); at ρ = −1, w1 = σ2 / (σ1 + σ2) and σp = 0. Diversification cuts risk whenever ρ < +1, more the lower ρ. Efficient frontier: the part of the minimum-variance frontier above the global minimum-variance portfolio.
  • Day 34. U = E(r) − ½ × A × σ2 (decimals): the certainty-equivalent return. A > 0 risk-averse (larger A = more averse); A = 0 risk-neutral; A < 0 risk-seeking.
  • Day 34. CAL (the CML with the market portfolio): E(Rp) = Rf + [(E(Ri) − Rf) / σi] × σp. Best weight: w* = [E(Ri) − Rf] / (A × σi2). Roy’s safety-first: SFRatio = [E(Rp) − RL] / σp; choose the highest.
Exam traps
  • One tail or two? 1.645 puts 5% in one tail: the z for a 5% value at risk or any one-sided 5% cutoff, and the edge of the 90% band (5% per tail). 1.96 puts 2.5% in each tail: the 95% band. Ask where the 5% sits before you choose the z.
  • The 2 in the cross term. Drop it and Day 32’s 60/40 mix comes out at 12.72% instead of 13.02%. Picture the (a + b)2 square: two rectangles, so 2w1w2Cov. The same 2 sits in the minimum-variance weight’s bottom line, σ12 + σ22 − 2Cov.
  • Percent versus decimal in utility. U needs decimals: 0.10 − ½ × 4 × 0.202 = 0.02 = 2%, while 10 − ½ × 4 × 202 = −790 is nonsense. Square σ and keep the ½. (The portfolio-σ formula works in either unit, as long as you never mix them.)

1. Binomial (Day 29) · calculator. A fund manager beats her benchmark in any one year with probability 0.4, independently of other years. What is the probability that she beats it in exactly 3 of the next 5 years? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint Number of orders (5C3) times the chance of one order: 0.4 for each beat, 0.6 for each miss.
Solution

5C3 = 10 orders. One order: 0.43 × 0.62 = 0.064 × 0.36 = 0.02304. P(X = 3) = 10 × 0.02304 = 0.2304 = 23.04%.

BA II Plus: 5 2ND nCr 3 = → 10.0000; × .4 yˣ 3 × .6 x² = → 0.2304 (AOS does the power before the ×); × 100 = → 23.0400.

TI-84 Plus: 5 MATH ► ► ► (to PRB; PROB on a CE) 3:nCr 3 ENTER → 10.0000; × .4 ^ 3 × .6 x² ENTER → .2304. Or 2nd VARS (DISTR) A:binompdf( (0:binompdf( before OS 2.30) 5 , .4 , 3 ) ENTER → .2304.

Leave out 5C3 and you get 2.30%, the chance of one particular order. “At least 3” would be 23.04% + 7.68% + 1.02% = 31.74%.

2. Normal tail (Day 30) · calculator. A portfolio’s annual returns are normal with mean 8% and standard deviation 10%. What is the probability of a year worse than −5%? As a percent, to two decimals. (For the BA II Plus, a z-table excerpt, P(Z ≤ z): 1.25 → 0.8944; 1.30 → 0.9032; 1.35 → 0.9115.)

%
Hint Standardize first: z = (X − μ) / σ. The table gives the area left of a positive z; for a negative z use symmetry, 1 minus the table value.
Solution

z = (−5 − 8) / 10 = −1.30. P(Z < −1.30) = 1 − P(Z ≤ 1.30) = 1 − 0.9032 = 0.0968 = 9.68%: about one year in ten. (Leave out the mean, z = −0.50, and you get 30.85%.)

BA II Plus: ( 5 +|− − 8 ) ÷ 10 = → −1.3000; table: P(Z ≤ 1.30) = 0.9032; 1 − .9032 = → 0.0968.

TI-84 Plus: 2nd VARS (DISTR) 2:normalcdf( (−) 1 2nd , (EE) 99 , (−) 5 , 8 , 10 ) ENTER → .0968. The line reads normalcdf(−1E99,−5,8,10): from minus infinity up to −5, then μ and σ.

3. Confidence band (Day 30). Annual returns on a portfolio are normally distributed with a mean of 9% and a standard deviation of 12%. The interval expected to contain 95% of annual returns is closest to:

  • That is 9 ± 1.645 × 12, the 90% band: 1.645 leaves 5% in each tail.

  • Right: 9 ± 1.96 × 12 = 9 ± 23.52.

  • That is 9 ± 2.58 × 12, the 99% band.

Explanation

95% in the middle leaves 2.5% in each tail, and the z that cuts off 2.5% in one tail is 1.96. 1.645 is for 5% in one tail, the value-at-risk number.

4. Skew and tails (Day 30). A series of monthly returns has a mean of 0.8%, a median of 1.2% and a sample excess kurtosis of 1.8. Compared with a normal distribution, the series is best described as:

  • Right. The mean sits below the median, so a long left tail is dragging it down; excess kurtosis above 0 means fatter tails than the normal (leptokurtic).

  • The mean–median order read backwards. A long right tail would pull the mean above the median; here it is below.

  • 1.8 is already the excess kurtosis (kurtosis − 3). The kurtosis itself is 4.8, and any excess above 0 means fat tails.

Explanation

A long tail drags the mean toward it while the median barely moves: mean 0.8% < median 1.2% puts the tail on the left. Compare kurtosis with 3, or excess kurtosis with 0, never excess kurtosis with 3. Fat tails mean extreme months come more often than the normal curve predicts.

5. Log return (Day 31) · calculator. A share closes at 64, then 72 the next day, then 60 the day after. What is its two-day continuously compounded return? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint r = ln(Pend / Pstart). Add the two daily log returns, or take one log of the whole move: the same number.
Solution

ln(60 / 64) = ln 0.9375 = −0.0645 = −6.45%. Day by day: ln(72 / 64) = 0.1178 and ln(60 / 72) = −0.1823, which add to the same −0.0645. (Adding the simple returns, +12.50% and −16.67%, gives −4.17%; the holding-period return is −6.25%. Neither is continuously compounded.)

BA II Plus: 60 ÷ 64 = LN → −0.0645; × 100 = → −6.4539.

TI-84 Plus: LN 60 ÷ 64 ) ENTER → −.0645; × 100 ENTER → −6.4539.

6. Simulation (Day 31). An analyst assumes a fund’s monthly returns are normal with a mean of 0.7% and a standard deviation of 4.5%. She draws 10,000 random 12-month paths from that distribution and records each path’s annual return. This is best described as:

  • The bootstrap draws, with replacement, from the fund’s actual past returns, not from an assumed distribution.

  • Historical simulation is built from the actual past returns; it assumes no distribution.

  • Right: random draws from an assumed probability distribution, repeated many times, then summarized.

Explanation

The giveaway is “assumes … normal with a mean of … and a standard deviation of …”: a chosen distribution. Monte Carlo can produce returns never seen before, but it is only as good as that assumption. The bootstrap and historical simulation keep the real shape of past returns, but cannot produce a return the sample never saw.

7. Portfolio risk (Day 32) · calculator. A portfolio holds 50% in an equity fund (σ = 22%) and 50% in a bond fund (σ = 9%). The correlation between them is −0.2. What is the portfolio’s standard deviation? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint Two own terms, w2σ2, plus the cross term, 2w1w2ρσ1σ2, which is negative here. Add, then take the square root. Work in percent.
Solution

0.52 × 222 = 121; 0.52 × 92 = 20.25; 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 × (−0.2) × 22 × 9 = −19.80. Variance 121.45 (%2); σp = √121.45 = 11.02%. The weighted average of the SDs, 15.50%, would hold only at ρ = +1. (Forget the 2: 11.46%. Drop the minus sign: 12.69%.)

BA II Plus: .5 x² × 22 x² = STO 1 → 121.0000; .5 x² × 9 x² = STO 2 → 20.2500; 2 × .5 × .5 × .2 +|− × 22 × 9 = STO 3 → −19.8000; RCL 1 + RCL 2 + RCL 3 = → 121.4500; √x → 11.0204.

TI-84 Plus, in one line: 2nd x² (√) .5 x² × 22 x² + .5 x² × 9 x² + 2 × .5 × .5 × (−) .2 × 22 × 9 ) ENTER → 11.0204.

8. Covariance from a joint table (Day 32) · calculator. Next year brings one of three states. Boom (probability 0.25): fund X returns 18%, fund Y 2%. Normal (0.50): X 8%, Y 5%. Slump (0.25): X −6%, Y 11%. What is the covariance of the two funds’ returns? To two decimals, in %2.

%²
Hint Expected returns first. Then, state by state, probability × X’s surprise × Y’s surprise, and add. No n − 1: the probabilities are the whole distribution.
Solution

E(X) = 0.25 × 18 + 0.5 × 8 + 0.25 × (−6) = 4.5 + 4 − 1.5 = 7.00%. E(Y) = 0.5 + 2.5 + 2.75 = 5.75%.

Boom: 0.25 × 11 × (−3.75) = −10.3125. Normal: 0.5 × 1 × (−0.75) = −0.375. Slump: 0.25 × (−13) × 5.25 = −17.0625. Cov = −27.75 (%2), or −0.002775 in decimals.

Every state votes “opposite”: with σX = 8.54% and σY = 3.27%, ρ = −0.99. (Leave out the probabilities: −110.25. Treat the three states as a sample of three years and divide by n − 1: −55.00.)

BA II Plus: .25 × 18 + .5 × 8 + .25 × 6 +|− = STO 1 → 7.0000; .25 × 2 + .5 × 5 + .25 × 11 = STO 2 → 5.7500; then .25 × ( 18 − RCL 1 ) × ( 2 − RCL 2 ) + .5 × ( 8 − RCL 1 ) × ( 5 − RCL 2 ) + .25 × ( 6 +|− − RCL 1 ) × ( 11 − RCL 2 ) = → −27.7500.

TI-84 Plus: .25 × 18 + .5 × 8 + .25 × (−) 6 STO→ ALPHA A ENTER → 7.0000; .25 × 2 + .5 × 5 + .25 × 11 STO→ ALPHA B ENTER → 5.7500; then .25 × ( 18 − ALPHA A ) × ( 2 − ALPHA B ) + .5 × ( 8 − ALPHA A ) × ( 5 − ALPHA B ) + .25 × ( (−) 6 − ALPHA A ) × ( 11 − ALPHA B ) ENTER → −27.7500.

9. Minimum-variance weight (Day 33) · calculator. Asset 1 has σ = 18%, asset 2 has σ = 10%, and the correlation between them is 0.3. What weight in asset 1 gives the minimum-variance portfolio? As a percent, to two decimals.

%
Hint Cov = ρσ1σ2 first. Then w1* = (σ22 − Cov) / (σ12 + σ22 − 2Cov): the other asset’s variance on top.
Solution

Cov = 0.3 × 18 × 10 = 54 (%2). w1* = (100 − 54) / (324 + 100 − 108) = 46 / 316 = 0.1456 = 14.56%, with 85.44% in asset 2. In decimals, (0.01 − 0.0054) / (0.0324 + 0.01 − 0.0108) = 0.0046 / 0.0316: the same 0.1456. That mix’s σp is 9.66%, below even the safer asset’s 10%. (Type ρ where Cov belongs and you get 23.55%; the ρ = −1 shortcut gives 35.71%.)

BA II Plus: .3 × 18 × 10 = STO 1 → 54.0000; ( 10 x² − RCL 1 ) ÷ ( 18 x² + 10 x² − 2 × RCL 1 ) = STO 2 → 0.1456; × 100 = → 14.5570. The lowest σp, if you want it: 1 − RCL 2 = STO 3; RCL 2 x² × 18 x² + RCL 3 x² × 10 x² + 2 × RCL 2 × RCL 3 × RCL 1 = → 93.3038; √x → 9.6594.

10. Utility (Day 34). An investor with a risk-aversion coefficient of 4 must choose one of three portfolios. P1: expected return 5%, standard deviation 5%. P2: expected return 9%, standard deviation 12%. P3: expected return 14%, standard deviation 24%. Based on mean–variance utility, the investor chooses:

  • That is what σ in place of σ2 gives (U = −5.0%, −15.0%, −34.0%), and so do percents in place of decimals (−45, −279, −1,138): the penalty swamps the return, so the least risky looks best.

  • Right. U = 0.05 − ½ × 4 × 0.052 = 4.50%; 0.09 − 2 × 0.0144 = 6.12%; 0.14 − 2 × 0.0576 = 2.48%. P2 scores highest.

  • The highest expected return: the choice of a risk-neutral investor (A = 0). At A = 4, P3’s risk costs 11.52 points of return.

Explanation

U = E(r) − ½Aσ2, decimals in, σ squared. Penalties: P1 0.50 points, P2 2.88, P3 11.52. Moving from P2 to P3 adds 5 points of expected return but 8.64 points of penalty. A less averse investor, with A below about 2.31, would pick P3.

BA II Plus (AOS): .09 − .5 × 4 × .12 x² = → 0.0612, and the same keys with each portfolio’s numbers. TI-84 Plus: .09 − .5 × 4 × .12 x² ENTER → .0612.
