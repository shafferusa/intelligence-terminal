$2.04 billion or $997.6 million today? The Powerball choice, and the payment that comes first

The $2.04 billion ticket from Altadena

The Powerball draw of Monday 7 November 2022 ran about 10 hours late. The Minnesota Lottery needed more time to verify its sales, so the numbers were drawn on the morning of 8 November.

The jackpot was a record $2.04 billion, and a single ticket won it. It had been sold at Joe’s Service Center in Altadena, California. In February 2023 the California Lottery named the winner: Edwin Castro.

He had a choice. He could take $997.6 million in cash. Or he could take the annuity: one payment straight away, then 29 more, once a year, each 5% bigger than the last. Those 30 payments add up to $2.04 billion.

He took the cash.

Which raises two questions. Why is “$2.04 billion” worth less than half that in cash? ($997.6 million is 48.9% of it.) And the annuity’s first payment came straight away, not a year later. How much does that matter?

The idea

Day 5’s ordinary annuity pays at the end of each period, so the first payment is a period away. Plenty of payments run the other way. Rent is due at the start of the month. Leases, insurance premiums and school fees are paid in advance. The Powerball annuity pays its first installment straight away.

Equal payments at the start of each period make an annuity due. Same number of payments, same size. The only change: each one comes a period sooner.

Money sooner is worth more, because it can earn a period of interest. So each payment is worth (1 + r) times what it was worth in the ordinary annuity, and so is the whole stream.

Picture two tenants, each paying $1,000 a month for a year, one at the start of the month and one at the end. Twelve payments each, but the landlord prefers the first: every check arrives a month sooner.

The warm-up’s $2,000 a year was worth $7,985.42 paid at year-ends. Paid at the start of each year, it is worth 7,985.42 × 1.08 = $8,624.25.

On the calculator it is one switch: BGN mode. And when a lump sum is offered instead, ask at what rate the two are worth the same: solve for I/Y.

Why it works

Start from Day 5. An ordinary annuity pays A at the end of periods 1 to N. Its PV is N single sums, each discounted for the periods it waits:

PVordinary = A/(1 + r) + A/(1 + r)2 + … + A/(1 + r)N = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r

Slide every payment one period earlier. An annuity due pays the same A at t = 0, 1, …, N − 1. Still N payments, but each waits one period less:

PVdue = A + A/(1 + r) + … + A/(1 + r)N−1

Match the terms. Multiply any ordinary term by (1 + r) and one power of (1 + r) cancels. A/(1 + r) becomes A; A/(1 + r)2 becomes A/(1 + r); the last, A/(1 + r)N, becomes A/(1 + r)N−1. That is the annuity due, term for term.

So the totals match. Every term × (1 + r) means the sum × (1 + r): PVdue = PVordinary × (1 + r).

See it. $1,000 a year for 3 years at 10%. Ordinary, paid at t = 1, 2, 3: 909.09 + 826.45 + 751.31 = 2,486.85. Due, paid at t = 0, 1, 2: 1,000.00 + 909.09 + 826.45 = 2,735.54. And 2,486.85 × 1.1 = 2,735.535, which rounds to 2,735.54: the same.

Another way to see it. Split off the first payment: paid today, it is worth A. The other N − 1 payments start a period from now, an ordinary annuity. Here: 1,000 + 1,000 × 1.735537 = 2,735.54, the same.

Future value. Day 5’s FVordinary values each payment at t = N, the date of the last one. Made a period earlier, each payment grows one period longer: FVdue = FVordinary × (1 + r), still valued at t = N. Here the three payments grow 3, 2 and 1 years: 1,331 + 1,210 + 1,100 = 3,641.00, which is the ordinary 3,310.00 × 1.1.

In plain words: same payments, each a period sooner, so each is worth one period’s interest more, and so is the whole stream. BGN mode tells the calculator exactly that.

PVdue = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r × (1 + r)

FVdue = A × [(1 + r)N − 1] / r × (1 + r)

Split: PVdue = A + A × [1 − (1 + r)−(N−1)] / r

A
the level payment, made at the start of each period
r
the interest rate per period, as a decimal
N
the number of payments (and of periods)
PVdue
value at t = 0, the moment of the first payment
FVdue
value at t = N, the end of the last period: one period after the last payment
(1 + r)
one period of interest: what each payment gains by arriving a period sooner
Memorize
  • PVdue = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r × (1 + r)
  • FVdue = A × [(1 + r)N − 1] / r × (1 + r)
  • PVdue = A + PV of an ordinary annuity of N − 1 payments
  • BA II Plus, annuity-due mode: 2ND BGN 2ND SET 2ND QUIT (shows BGN); repeat to return to END
  • Does 2ND CLR TVM reset BGN/END? No — clearing TVM leaves BGN/END and P/Y unchanged

Worked example

Problem. Rent of $1,200 is due at the start of each year for 3 years. At 5%, what is it worth today, and how does that compare with the same payments at the end of each year? What is it worth at the end of year 3?

By hand
  1. Annuity due, as single sums (paid at t = 0, 1, 2): 1,200 + 1,200/1.05 + 1,200/1.052.
  2. = 1,200.0000 + 1,142.8571 + 1,088.4354 = 3,431.29.
  3. Ordinary, paid at t = 1, 2, 3: 1.05−3 = 0.8638376, so the factor is (1 − 0.8638376) / 0.05 = 2.723248.
  4. 1,200 × 2.723248 = 3,267.90.
  5. Ratio: 3,431.29 / 3,267.90 = 1.0500, exactly 1 + r.
  6. Split check: 1,200 today + an ordinary annuity of 2 payments = 1,200 + 1,200 × 1.859410 = 3,431.29.
  7. At the end of year 3: 1,200 × 1.053 + 1,200 × 1.052 + 1,200 × 1.05 = 1,389.15 + 1,323.00 + 1,260.00 = 3,972.15, also 3,431.29 × 1.053.

Back to the story

Why is “$2.04 billion” worth less than half that in cash? Because $2.04 billion is 30 payments simply added up, as if money 29 years away were worth as much as money today.

Spread evenly and discounted at 4%, the payments are worth $1,222.9 million. The first one coming today adds $47.0 million: if each came a year later, they would be worth $1,175.9 million, a ratio of exactly 1.04. Against the cash, the level stream breaks even at 5.94% a year.

The real schedule is more back-loaded: about $30.7 million first, rising 5% a year to about $126.4 million. Only about $386 million of it arrives in the first 10 years (level: $680 million). So it matches the $997.6 million cash at a rate of only about 4.4%: on these numbers, anyone who can earn more than that on the cash does better taking it. Most of the headline $2.04 billion is money far in the future.

Exam traps
  • Leaving the calculator in BGN. The next ordinary annuity comes out (1 + r) too big: the warm-up’s 7,985.42 would show as 8,624.25. Switch back to END the moment you finish, and look for the small BGN before every TVM problem.
  • Treating payments made in advance as an ordinary annuity. Rent, leases and insurance premiums are usually paid at the start of the period: the worked example’s rent is worth 3,431.29, not 3,267.90.
  • Applying (1 + r) twice. BGN mode already includes it; multiplying a BGN answer by 1.05 again turns 3,431.29 into 3,602.85.
  • Counting today’s payment as an extra one. Thirty payments, the first today, is N = 30, not 31. Only the split uses N − 1 = 29, for its ordinary part.

2. Why. A 10-year annuity due and a 10-year ordinary annuity pay the same amount each year, at the same rate r. Why is the annuity due worth exactly (1 + r) times as much today?

  • Both make 10 payments: the annuity due at t = 0 to 9, the ordinary annuity at t = 1 to 10. It gains one today but has none at year 10.

  • Right. A/(1 + r)t × (1 + r) = A/(1 + r)t−1, for every one of the 10 payments.

  • The same r discounts both. The whole difference is timing, not risk.

Explanation

Line them up. Ordinary: t = 1, 2, …, 10. Due: t = 0, 1, …, 9. It can look like an extra payment, because the due has one today, but it also has none at t = 10: the count is still 10. What changes is that each payment waits one period less, so each is worth (1 + r) times more, and a sum of terms that are each (1 + r) times bigger is (1 + r) times bigger.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: when is a stream of payments an annuity due, and how does that change its value?

Model answer

It is an annuity due when each payment comes at the start of the period, as with rent, leases and insurance premiums, so the first one is today. Each payment then arrives a period sooner than in an ordinary annuity and is discounted one period less, so its PV and FV are the ordinary values × (1 + r); on the calculator, set BGN, and switch back to END afterwards.

A complete answer says: (1) the payments come at the start of each period (rent, leases, premiums); (2) each is discounted one period less, so the value is the ordinary annuity’s × (1 + r); (3) BGN mode on the calculator, then back to END.
