Four datasets, one line, one R²: Anscombe’s 1973 warning about what a good fit really means

__DATELINE__ · 20 min

Every regression splits the variation in Y into the part the line explains and the part it misses, SST = SSR + SSE. R2, the standard error of the estimate and the F-statistic are three ways of reading that split.

Warm-up · from earlier lessons

Two quick ones, from Days 47 and 41, so they stick. First try counts.

From Day 47. A stock’s monthly returns (Y) are regressed on the market’s (X). The covariance of the two is 18 (%2), the market’s variance is 15 (%2), and the average monthly returns are 1.0% for the market and 1.4% for the stock. What return does the least-squares line predict for the stock in a month when the market returns 3.0%? In %, to two decimals.

%
Hint Slope first: b̂1 = Cov(X, Y) / Var(X). The line passes through the two means, and that gives the intercept.
Solution

b̂1 = 18 / 15 = 1.2. b̂0 = Ȳ − b̂1X̄ = 1.4 − 1.2 × 1.0 = 0.2. Prediction: 0.2 + 1.2 × 3.0 = 0.2 + 3.6 = 3.80%.

The two usual slips: Var / Cov = 15 / 18 = 0.8333 as the slope (it gives 3.07%), and forgetting the intercept (1.2 × 3 = 3.60%).

From Day 41. A bond has an annual modified duration of 8.0 and an annual convexity of 90. If its yield falls by 100 bp, the estimated percentage price change is closest to:

  • That gives the convexity term a minus sign because yields fell. (ΔYield)2 is positive whichever way yields move, so the convexity term always adds.

  • That forgets the ½: 90 × 0.012 = 0.90%, but the term is ½ × 90 × 0.012 = 0.45%.

  • Right: −8.0 × (−0.01) = +8.00%, plus ½ × 90 × (−0.01)2 = +0.45%.

%ΔPV ≈ −AnnModDur × ΔYield + ½ × AnnConvexity × (ΔYield)2 = −8.0 × (−0.01) + ½ × 90 × 0.0001 = 0.0800 + 0.0045 = +8.45%.

Anscombe’s quartet: four datasets, one line

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published a short paper, “Graphs in Statistical Analysis”, in The American Statistician. In it he built four small datasets of 11 points each.

On paper the four are twins. The same mean of X (9) and of Y (7.50). The same variance of X (11), and almost the same variance of Y (4.12 to 4.13). The same correlation, 0.816. And the same least-squares line: Y = 3.00 + 0.500X.

Plotted, they look nothing alike.

Set I is a fuzzy straight line, the kind of cloud a regression is made for. Set II is a smooth curve. Set III is a perfect straight line, knocked off course by one outlier. Set IV is a vertical stack of points, with the slope set entirely by a single far-off point.

Feed any of the four to a regression and it prints the same line. Today you will see it also prints the same R2, the number analysts quote for “how good is the fit”.

So: if all four have the same line and the same R2, does the regression fit all four equally well? And what does R2 actually measure?

The idea

Day 47 drew the line. Today asks how good it is.

Picture guessing every Y with no information at all. Your best single guess is the average, Ȳ, every time. Square each miss and add them up: that is SST, the total sum of squares. It is all the variation there is to explain.

Now guess with the line instead: Ŷ for each X. Some misses remain. Squared and added, they are SSE, the sum of squared errors (the residuals). What the line took away is SSR, the regression sum of squares: the explained part. So SST = SSR + SSE.

Three numbers read that split:

  • R2 (the coefficient of determination) = SSR / SST: the share of the variation the line explains, from 0 to 1.
  • SEE (the standard error of the estimate): the size of a typical miss that is left, in Y’s own units.
  • F = MSR / MSE: is the explained part bigger than a line through pure noise would manage?

The exam lays this out as an ANOVA table (analysis of variance): rows for regression, error (residual) and total; columns for degrees of freedom, sums of squares and mean squares.

Why it works

No line. Without X, every guess is Ȳ, and the squared misses add to SST = Σ(Y − Ȳ)2. That is Day 24’s variance before dividing: SST = (n − 1)sY2.

Split each miss. Y − Ȳ = (Ŷ − Ȳ) + (Y − Ŷ). The first piece is how far the line moved the guess off the average: explained. The second is the residual e (Day 47): still missed.

Square and add. (a + b)2 = a2 + b2 + 2ab, so Σ(Y − Ȳ)2 = Σ(Ŷ − Ȳ)2 + Σ(Y − Ŷ)2 + 2Σ(Ŷ − Ȳ)e.

The cross term is zero. Least squares makes the residuals add to zero and not move with X. Since Ŷ − Ȳ = b̂1(X − X̄), the cross term is 2b̂1Σ(X − X̄)e = 0. So SST = SSR + SSE: Pythagoras, with the two pieces at right angles.

R2 is a share. R2 = SSR / SST. With one X it equals r2 (Day 25): SSR = b̂12Σ(X − X̄)2, and b̂1 = r × sY/sX, so SSR = r2 × (n − 1)sY2 = r2 × SST.

Divide by degrees of freedom. Day 24 used n − 1 because one estimate, X̄, used up one degree of freedom. The residuals rest on two estimates, b̂0 and b̂1: MSE = SSE / (n − 2), and SEE = √MSE is their standard deviation. One slope, so MSR = SSR / 1.

F: does the line beat noise? As on Day 46, F compares two variance estimates and sits near 1 when nothing is going on: with a true slope of zero, MSR and MSE both measure only noise. Written out, F = (SSR / SSE) × (n − 2): explained against unexplained, scaled by the residuals’ df.

In plain words: total miss = explained + left over. R2 is the explained share, SEE the typical leftover miss, and F asks whether the explained part beats luck.

SST = SSR + SSE

R2 = SSR / SST

SEE = √MSE = √[SSE / (n − 2)]

F = MSR / MSE = (SSR / 1) / [SSE / (n − 2)], df = 1 and n − 2

SST
total sum of squares, Σ(Yi − Ȳ)2: all the variation in Y around its mean
SSR
regression (explained) sum of squares, Σ(Ŷi − Ȳ)2
SSE
sum of squared errors (unexplained), Σ(Yi − Ŷi)2: the residuals squared
R2
coefficient of determination: the share of SST explained, 0 to 1; equals r2 with one independent variable
MSR, MSE
mean square regression and mean square error: each sum of squares divided by its df, k = 1 and n − k − 1 = n − 2
SEE
standard error of the estimate: the residuals’ standard deviation, in Y’s units
n
number of observations
Memorize
  • Decomposition of variation: SST = SSR + SSE
  • Coefficient of determination: R2 = SSR / SST (= r2 with one independent variable)
  • Mean squared error: MSE = SSE / (n − 2)
  • Standard error of estimate: SEE = √MSE = √[SSE / (n − 2)]
  • F-statistic in simple regression: F = MSR / MSE = (SSR / 1) / [SSE / (n − 2)], df = 1 and n − 2

Worked example

Problem. Day 47’s money growth X = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and inflation Y = 2, 3, 5, 4, 6 (%), with line Ŷ = 1.3 + 0.9X. Build the ANOVA and test the slope at 5%.

By hand
  1. Ȳ = 4. SST = Σ(Y − Ȳ)2 = 4 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 4 = 10.
  2. Fitted values Ŷ = 2.2, 3.1, 4.0, 4.9, 5.8; residuals −0.2, −0.1, 1.0, −0.9, 0.2. SSE = 0.04 + 0.01 + 1.00 + 0.81 + 0.04 = 1.90.
  3. Ŷ − Ȳ = −1.8, −0.9, 0, 0.9, 1.8. SSR = 3.24 + 0.81 + 0 + 0.81 + 3.24 = 8.10. Check: 8.10 + 1.90 = 10, and the cross products add to 0.36 + 0.09 + 0 − 0.81 + 0.36 = 0.
  4. R2 = 8.10 / 10 = 0.81. Here r = 0.9, and 0.92 = 0.81.
  5. MSE = 1.90 / (5 − 2) = 0.6333; SEE = √0.6333 = 0.7958.
  6. F = (8.10 / 1) / 0.6333 = 12.79, df (1, 3). Critical F(1, 3) at 5% = 10.13. 12.79 > 10.13: reject H0: b1 = 0. The slope is significant.
  7. As the exam’s ANOVA table: Regression df 1, SS 8.10, MS 8.10; Error df 3, SS 1.90, MS 0.6333; Total df 4, SS 10.00; F = 12.79.

Back to the story

Run Anscombe’s set I through today’s split. SST = 41.27 (10 × Y’s variance of 4.127), SSR = 27.51 and SSE = 13.76.

R2 = 27.51 / 41.27 = 0.667: two-thirds explained. SEE = √(13.76 / 9) = 1.237. F = 27.51 / (13.76 / 9) = 17.99, far above the 5% critical value of 5.12 for (1, 9) df.

Sets II, III and IV give the same: R2 0.67, SEE 1.24, F about 18. On every number in today’s lesson, the four fits are identical.

They are not equally good. R2 only reports the share of Y’s variation the line accounts for. It cannot say whether a straight line is the right shape (set II), whether one point is doing all the work (sets III and IV), or whether the errors behave. That is why Anscombe insisted on plotting the data, and why regression’s assumptions (Day 47) are checked with residual plots.

Exam traps
  • n − 1 instead of n − 2. In the walkthrough, 0.006 / 29 gives SEE 0.0144 and F 19.33 instead of 0.0146 and 18.67. Two coefficients were estimated, so the residuals have n − 2 df.
  • SEE is not the slope’s standard error. SEE is the typical miss of one Y around the line, in Y’s units. The standard error of the slope, which Day 50 uses to test b̂1, is SEE / √Σ(X − X̄)2: in the worked example 0.7958 / √10 = 0.2517, not 0.7958.
  • A high R2 proves neither cause nor a good model. Anscombe’s four sets share R2 = 0.67, and only set I suits a straight line. R2 is a share of variation, not evidence that X drives Y.
  • R2 = SSR / SST, not SSR / SSE. In the worked example 8.10 / 1.90 = 4.26, which is impossible: R2 can never exceed 1.

1. Your turn, on the calculator. A regression on 40 quarterly returns has SSR = 0.0125 and SSE = 0.0375. What is the F-statistic for the slope? To two decimals.

Hint Same keystrokes as steps 2 and 4: n − 2 is now 38, and MSR = SSR / 1 = 0.0125. Store the MSE rather than retyping it.
Solution

MSE = 0.0375 / 38 = 0.000987; F = 0.0125 / 0.000987 = 12.67, df (1, 38). Shortcut: (SSR / SSE) × (n − 2) = (1/3) × 38 = 12.67. Also R2 = 0.0125 / 0.0500 = 0.25 and SEE = 0.0314.

The 5% critical F(1, 38) is about 4.10, so 12.67 > 4.10: reject, the slope is significant (TI-84 p = 0.0010).

2. Why. Why is SSE divided by n − 2, not n − 1, to get the MSE?

  • The numerator df is k = 1, one slope. n − 2 is the denominator’s df, and it comes from the residuals, not the other way round.

  • Right. The residuals must add to zero and be uncorrelated with X: two conditions, so only n − 2 of them are free to vary.

  • That is SST / (n − 1) = sY2, Day 24’s variance, with one estimate (Ȳ). The MSE measures the scatter around the line, not around the mean.

Explanation

Day 24 divided by n − 1 because deviations from the mean must add to zero: one condition. Least-squares residuals obey two, Σe = 0 and Σ(X − X̄)e = 0, one for each coefficient. Know any n − 2 residuals and the last two are forced. With n = 5, as in the worked example, only 3 residuals are free: MSE = 1.90 / 3.

3. Twist: work backwards from R2. A regression on 20 observations has SST = 0.0500 and R2 = 0.64. What is the standard error of the estimate? To four decimals.

Hint R2 is the explained share, so 1 − R2 is the share left in SSE. Then divide by n − 2 = 18 and take the square root.
Solution

SSE = (1 − 0.64) × 0.0500 = 0.0180. MSE = 0.0180 / 18 = 0.0010. SEE = √0.0010 = 0.0316.

The same table gives SSR = 0.64 × 0.0500 = 0.0320 and F = 0.0320 / 0.0010 = 32.00, df (1, 18): far above the 5% critical value of 4.41.

BA II Plus: ( 1 − .64 ) × .05 ÷ 18 = √x → 0.0316.

TI-84 Plus: 2nd x² (√) ( 1 − .64 ) × .05 ÷ 18 ) ENTER → .0316.

4. Exam style. A simple linear regression on 25 monthly observations has a sum of squared errors (SSE) of 0.0460. The standard error of the estimate is closest to:

  • Right: √[0.0460 / (25 − 2)] = √0.0020 = 0.0447.

  • That divides by n − 1 = 24, the sample-variance habit. A regression estimates two coefficients: divide by n − 2.

  • That is the MSE, 0.0460 / 23. The standard error is its square root.

Explanation

SEE = √[SSE / (n − 2)] = √(0.0460 / 23) = √0.0020 = 0.0447.

BA II Plus: .046 ÷ 23 = √x → 0.0447. TI-84 Plus: 2nd x² (√) .046 ÷ 23 ) ENTER → .0447.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what does R2 measure, and what does Anscombe’s quartet show it cannot tell you?

Model answer

R2 = SSR / SST is the share of the variation in Y that the regression explains. Anscombe’s four datasets have the same line and the same R2 (0.67), yet only one suits a straight line: R2 cannot tell you whether a line is the right shape, whether an outlier or one influential point drives the fit, or whether X causes Y, so plot the data and the residuals.

A complete answer says: (1) R2 is the share of Y’s variation explained by the regression, SSR / SST, from 0 to 1; (2) it says nothing about whether the linear form is right, about outliers or influential points, or about causation; (3) so plot the data and the residuals.
