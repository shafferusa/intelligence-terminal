Austria’s century bond

September 2017. Austria sold €3.5 billion of bonds that it will not repay until 20 September 2117, a hundred years later.

No euro-zone government had sold a century bond publicly by syndication before. (Belgium and Ireland had placed small ones privately.) The coupon was 2.1% a year, priced to yield about 2.11%, and investors put in more than €11 billion of orders.

Bloomberg’s Gadfly columnists pointed out what that meant: a one-basis-point move in the yield, 0.01 percentage point, would shift the price by about 43 cents per 100 of face value.

Then euro yields sank. By 2020 the bond traded at more than twice its face value. From 2022 yields climbed, and it slid. In 2026 it has traded between about 54 and 61 in Milan: 58.75 on 24 April, about 57 in October.

Austria’s credit hardly changed. Yet from the peak, the bond lost more than two-thirds of its value.

What makes a 100-year, low-coupon bond so sensitive to interest rates? And how do you measure that sensitivity with one number?

The idea

A bond pays you in pieces: small coupons along the way, the principal at the end. Macaulay duration asks: on average, how long do you wait for your money?

Each payment counts by its present value, its share of today’s price. Picture a plank along a timeline, each payment’s PV stacked as a weight at its date. MacDur is where the plank balances.

A zero has one weight, at maturity, so its MacDur equals its maturity. Coupons are small weights placed earlier; they pull the balance point toward today.

Why care? The further away a payment is, the harder a change in yield hits its PV. So the average wait is also the bond’s sensitivity to rates. Divide it by (1 + r) for modified duration: about how many percent the price falls per 1-percentage-point rise in yield.

A second meaning. If rates rise just after you buy, the price falls, but your coupons are reinvested at the higher rate. At a horizon equal to MacDur the two roughly cancel. Buy the walkthrough’s bond below at 102.78 and hold it 2.86 years, reinvesting coupons: you end up with 114.98 whether the yield stays at 4%, jumps to 5% or drops to 3%.

So duration gap = MacDur − your horizon. Positive: price risk dominates, and rising rates hurt. Negative: reinvestment risk dominates, and falling rates hurt.

Why it works

Start with one payment, then build the bond.

  1. One year of waiting. A payment due in a year is worth CF/(1 + r). Raise the yield by Δ and the divisor becomes (1 + r + Δ) = (1 + r) × [1 + Δ/(1 + r)]: one extra factor. So the PV shrinks by about Δ/(1 + r). At r = 4%, a 1-point rise costs about 0.01/1.04 = 0.96%.
  2. t years of waiting. A payment t years away is divided by (1 + r) t times, so it takes that hit t times: it loses about t × Δ/(1 + r). Check: 105 due in 3 years is 93.3446 at 4% and 90.7029 at 5%, down 2.83%; the rule says 3 × 0.96 = 2.88%. Time is sensitivity.
  3. A bond is a bundle of payments (Day 10). Its % change is the average of theirs, each weighted by its share of the price, wt = PV(CFt)/PV, just as a portfolio’s return averages its assets’ returns (Day 32).
  4. Add them up. %ΔPV ≈ Σ wt × [−t × Δ/(1 + r)] = −[Σ wt × t] × Δ/(1 + r).
  5. Name the bracket. Σ wt × t, the PV-weighted average time, is the Macaulay duration; divided by (1 + r) it is the modified duration. So %ΔPV ≈ −ModDur × ΔYield.
  6. Semiannual. Count t in half-years with r = yield/2, so MacDur comes out in half-years. AnnModDur = MacDur(periods)/(1 + r/2)/2.

Anything that moves PV weight later raises duration: a longer maturity, a lower coupon, a lower yield (far payments are discounted less, so they keep more weight).

In plain words: the longer you wait for your money, the more a change in interest rates changes what it is worth today, and duration is the average wait.

MacDur = Σ t × [CFt/(1 + r)t] / PV = Σ t × wt

ModDur = MacDur / (1 + r)

%ΔPV ≈ −AnnModDur × ΔYield

t
the period a cash flow arrives in: 1, 2, …, N
CFt
the cash flow at t: a coupon, or coupon + principal at N
r
the yield per period, as a decimal
PV
the bond’s price: Σ CFt/(1 + r)t
wt
that payment’s share of the price, PV(CFt)/PV; the weights add to 1
MacDur
Macaulay duration: the PV-weighted average time to the cash flows, in periods
ModDur
modified duration; AnnModDur is it in years (divide by periods per year)
ΔYield
the change in the annual yield, as a decimal (50 bp = 0.0050)
Memorize
  • Macaulay duration: MacDur = Σ t × [CFt/(1 + r)t] / PV
  • Modified duration: ModDur = MacDur / (1 + r)
  • Percentage price change from duration: %ΔPV ≈ −AnnModDur × ΔYield
  • Duration gap: Duration gap = Macaulay duration − investment horizon
  • Macaulay duration of a zero-coupon bond: Equal to its time to maturity
  • Duration of a semiannual-pay bond: Compute in periods with r/2, then divide by 2 for years; AnnModDur = MacDur(periods) / (1 + r/2) / 2

Worked example

Problem. A 2-year bond pays a 6% annual coupon and yields 5%. Find its price, Macaulay duration and modified duration.

By hand
  1. PV1 = 6/1.05 = 5.7143.
  2. PV2 = 106/1.052 = 106/1.1025 = 96.1451.
  3. Price = 5.7143 + 96.1451 = 101.8594.
  4. Weights: 5.7143/101.8594 = 0.0561 and 96.1451/101.8594 = 0.9439. They add to 1.
  5. MacDur = 1 × 0.0561 + 2 × 0.9439 = 1.9439 years.
  6. ModDur = 1.9439/1.05 = 1.8513: a 1-point rise in yield cuts the price by about 1.85%.
  7. A 2-year zero would have MacDur = 2 exactly. The year-1 coupon pulls the average slightly earlier.

Back to the story

The century bond’s value sits mostly in small 2.1 coupons spread over decades. Weighted by PV, its payments balanced at 42.4 years at issue: its MacDur. ModDur = 42.4/1.02112 = 41.5. So each basis point moved the price about 0.415%, roughly 41 cents per 100, close to Bloomberg’s 43 cents. The walkthrough’s bond has ModDur 2.75. (Day 15’s Disney century bond, with a 7.55% coupon, had a MacDur of only about 13.7 years: big coupons pull the balance point in.)

Then lower yields raised the duration further. At a price of 200, with about 97 years left, the yield was about 0.69% and ModDur about 57.

Duration alone predicts a 171% fall for the rise to 3.67%, the yield at the April 2026 price of 58.75. Impossible: a price cannot go below zero. The real fall, from 200 to 58.75, was 71%. Duration is a straight line laid on a curve, good for small moves only. The gap is convexity, Day 41.

Exam traps
  • Semiannual bonds: periodic yield, then annualize. MacDur of 9 half-years at a 5% annual yield: AnnModDur = 9/1.025/2 = 4.3902. Dividing the years by the annual rate, 4.5/1.05 = 4.2857, is wrong; so is stopping at 9/1.025 = 8.7805, which is still in half-years.
  • The minus sign. Yield up, price down. In the walkthrough, +50 bp gives −1.38%, not +1.38%.
  • Macaulay is not the sensitivity. Divide by (1 + r) first. 2.8615 × 0.5 = 1.43% overstates the walkthrough’s move; 2.7514 × 0.5 = 1.38% is the estimate.
  • Units. 50 bp is 0.0050 as a decimal or 0.50 in percentage points. 2.7514 × 0.0050 = 0.0138, a decimal (1.38%); 2.7514 × 50 = 137.57 is nonsense.

2. Why. Three bonds all mature in 10 years and all yield 5%. Which has the highest Macaulay duration?

  • The lowest of the three (MacDur 7.54): its big early coupons carry a lot of the PV weight and pull the balance point toward today.

  • Right: all its value is paid at year 10, so its plank balances exactly there. MacDur = 10.00, the most any 10-year bond can have.

  • Longer than the 8% bond (MacDur 9.01), because its coupons are smaller. But any coupon puts some weight before year 10, so it stays below the zero.

Explanation

MacDur is the PV-weighted average time to the cash. Same maturity, same yield: the only difference is how much weight sits early. 8% coupon: 7.54 years. 2% coupon: 9.01. Zero: 10.00. Lower coupon, higher duration; a zero’s MacDur equals its maturity.

4. Exam style. A bond has a Macaulay duration of 7.80 and an annual-pay yield to maturity of 4%. If its yield rises by 40 basis points, its price will change by approximately:

5. Say it back. You will need your money in 5 years, and you hold a bond with a Macaulay duration of 7. In one or two sentences: what is your duration gap, and what does it tell you?

Model answer

The duration gap is 7 − 5 = +2 years. Macaulay duration is the horizon at which price risk and reinvestment risk cancel; my horizon is shorter, so price risk dominates: if rates rise, the lower price I sell at in year 5 outweighs the extra income from reinvesting coupons, and my money at year 5 is lower. (A negative gap would mean reinvestment risk dominates, and falling rates would hurt.)

A complete answer says: (1) gap = MacDur − horizon = +2 years; (2) Macaulay duration is the horizon at which price risk and reinvestment risk offset; (3) a positive gap means price risk dominates, so a rise in rates lowers the horizon value.
