The 5–4 vote at Salt Lake City

On 11 February 2002, at the Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City, Canada’s Jamie Salé and David Pelletier skated a pairs free program that was widely described as clean.

Russia’s Elena Berezhnaya and Anton Sikharulidze made a visible error: Sikharulidze stepped out of a double Axel.

The judges gave the gold to the Russians anyway, five votes to four.

Within days the French judge, Marie-Reine Le Gougne, was reported to have said she had been pressured by her federation to vote for the Russians. She later retracted the claim.

On 15 February the IOC and the International Skating Union (ISU) awarded Salé and Pelletier a second set of gold medals; the Russians kept theirs. In April the ISU suspended Le Gougne and the French federation’s head, Didier Gailhaguet, for three years.

One judge’s vote was enough to move a gold medal. If any one judge might be leaning on the scale, how do you combine nine scores so that no single mark, however extreme, can decide the result?

The idea

The mean (Day 12) gives every number the same say. That is its strength and its weakness: one wild number drags it.

The fix is to sort the data, smallest to largest, and look at positions:

  • Median: the middle value. Half the data sit below it, half above.
  • Mode: the most frequent value.
  • Quantiles cut the sorted data into equal groups: quartiles into 4 (Q1 = 25th percentile, Q2 = median, Q3 = 75th), quintiles into 5, deciles into 10, percentiles into 100.
  • Interquartile range, IQR = Q3 − Q1: the width of the middle half. A box plot draws it as a box, with a line at the median and whiskers out to the extremes (some versions stop the whiskers at 1.5 × IQR and dot anything beyond as an outlier).
  • Trimmed mean: drop a set share of the lowest and highest values, average the rest.
  • Winsorized mean: replace those extremes with the nearest values you kept, then average all of them.

An everyday picture: a teacher who drops your best and your worst quiz before averaging is using a trimmed mean.

Why it works

Start with the median, the middle of n sorted values.

  1. Odd n. Number the sorted values 1 to n. With n = 9 the middle is the 5th: four below, four above. And 5 = (9 + 1)/2.
  2. Even n. With n = 12 no value is in the middle; position 6.5 has six on each side. And 6.5 = (12 + 1)/2. So the middle is always at (n + 1)/2.
  3. Why n + 1? Picture a ruler from 0 to n + 1, with the sorted values standing at 1, 2, …, n and both ends empty. The median stands halfway along: (n + 1) × 50/100.
  4. Any percentile stands y% of the way along the same ruler: Ly = (n + 1) × y/100. For nine marks, Q1 is at 10 × 0.25 = 2.5 and Q3 at 7.5.
  5. Symmetry check. 2.5 is 1.5 places in from the 1st value; 7.5 is 1.5 in from the 9th. Mirror images, as Q1 and Q3 should be. With n instead, 2.25 and 6.75 sit 1.25 and 2.25 in: lopsided.
  6. Between two values. Location 2.5 falls between the 2nd and 3rd values: start at the lower one and add 0.5 of the gap, X2 + 0.5 × (X3 − X2).
  7. The plain mean, Σx/n, gives every mark a weight of 1/n. A mark d points too high lifts the mean by d/n, however big d gets: 2 points among nine moves it 0.2222.
  8. Trimmed: drop the k lowest and k highest, average the n − 2k left. A dropped mark counts zero, so pushing it further out changes nothing.
  9. Winsorized: reset the k lowest to the lowest kept value and the k highest to the highest kept value, then divide by n. The extremes still count, but only as much as their nearest neighbour.
  10. The median is trimming taken to the limit: drop everything but the middle.

In plain words: line the numbers up and judge by the middle of the line, not by the loudest numbers at its ends.

Ly = (n + 1) × y / 100

Py = Xbelow + (Ly − whole part of Ly) × (Xabove − Xbelow)

IQR = Q3 − Q1

Trimmed mean = sum of the kept values / number kept;   Winsorized mean = sum with the extremes reset / n

n
number of observations
y
the percentile wanted: 25 for Q1, 50 for the median, 75 for Q3
Ly
position of the y-th percentile, counting from 1 in the data sorted smallest to largest
Xbelow, Xabove
the sorted values at the whole positions just below and just above Ly
Py
the y-th percentile itself
Q1, Q3
first and third quartiles: the 25th and 75th percentiles
Memorize
  • Location of the y-th percentile in n sorted observations: Ly = (n + 1) × y / 100
  • Interpolating a percentile at a non-integer location: P = X(below) + (Ly − whole part) × (X(above) − X(below))
  • Trimmed mean: discard a stated % of the lowest and highest observations, then average the rest (a 5% trimmed mean drops the lowest 2.5% and the highest 2.5%)
  • Winsorized mean: replace the extreme observations with the nearest retained values, then average (a 95% winsorized mean resets the bottom and top 2.5%)
  • Interquartile range: IQR = Q3 − Q1

Worked example

Problem. Twelve monthly fund returns (%): 2.1, −0.8, 1.5, 3.4, −4.6, 0.9, 1.2, −1.1, 2.6, 0.4, 7.9, −0.3. Find the median, Q1, Q3, the IQR and the 10th percentile; then the mean, the trimmed mean that drops one value at each end, and the winsorized mean that resets them.

By hand
  1. Sort (n = 12): −4.6, −1.1, −0.8, −0.3, 0.4, 0.9, 1.2, 1.5, 2.1, 2.6, 3.4, 7.9. Every location is 13 × y/100.
  2. Median: L = 13 × 0.50 = 6.5 → 0.9 + 0.5 × (1.2 − 0.9) = 1.05%.
  3. Q1: L = 13 × 0.25 = 3.25 → −0.8 + 0.25 × (−0.3 − (−0.8)) = −0.675%.
  4. Q3: L = 13 × 0.75 = 9.75 → 2.1 + 0.75 × (2.6 − 2.1) = 2.475%. IQR = 2.475 − (−0.675) = 3.15 percentage points.
  5. 10th percentile: L = 1.3 → −4.6 + 0.3 × (−1.1 − (−4.6)) = −3.55%.
  6. Mean = 13.2/12 = 1.10%. Trimmed (drop −4.6 and 7.9) = 9.9/10 = 0.99%. Winsorized (−4.6 → −1.1, 7.9 → 3.4) = 12.2/12 = 1.0167%.
  7. Names: one of 12 at each end is 8.3%, so this is a 16.7% trimmed mean and an 83.3% winsorized mean.

The 7.9% month sits furthest out, so the plain mean ends up above the trimmed one.

On your calculator

Problem. Nine judges’ marks for one program (illustrative): 8.50, 8.25, 9.75, 8.75, 8.00, 8.50, 6.50, 8.75, 8.50. Find n, the mean and Σx; sort and find the median and the quartiles with (n + 1) × y/100; then the trimmed mean (drop the highest and lowest) and the winsorized mean. Sx and σx are tomorrow’s subject. The TI-84 finds Q1 and Q3 as the medians of the lower and upper halves. With n odd, as here, that matches the CFA formula; with n even it does not, so always locate quartiles by hand.

  4. Sort the marks, smallest first

    BA II Plus no sort key: write them in order on paper
    TI-84 Plus STAT 2:SortA( 2nd 1 (L1) ) ENTER, then STAT 1:Edit… to read L1

    Display: Done, then L1 runs 6.50, 8.00, 8.25, 8.50, 8.50, 8.50, 8.75, 8.75, 9.75 (TI-84). Positions 1 to 9. The mode is 8.50 (three times).

  5. Locate: L = (9 + 1) × y/100 gives 2.5 (Q1), 5 (median) and 7.5 (Q3). The median is the 5th mark, 8.50. Q3 is halfway between the 7th and 8th, both 8.75, so 8.75. Q1 is halfway from the 2nd (8.00) to the 3rd (8.25): interpolate

    BA II Plus 2ND QUIT 8 + .5 × ( 8.25 − 8 ) = (AOS multiplies before it adds)
    TI-84 Plus 2nd MODE (QUIT) 8 + .5 × ( 8.25 − 8 ) ENTER

    Display: 8.1250 on both. Q1 = 8.125, median 8.50, Q3 = 8.75: IQR = 0.625. The middle half of the panel sits within 0.625 points.

  6. Trimmed mean: take the lowest (6.50) and highest (9.75) out of Σx, divide by the 7 left

    BA II Plus ( 75.5 − 6.5 − 9.75 ) ÷ 7 =
    TI-84 Plus ( 75.5 − 6.5 − 9.75 ) ÷ 7 ENTER (the subtraction key, not (−))

    Display: 8.4643 on both. Divide by 7, not 9: the two dropped marks are gone.

  7. Winsorized mean: reset 6.50 to 8.00 (the lowest kept) and 9.75 to 8.75 (the highest kept), divide by all 9

    BA II Plus ( 75.5 − 6.5 − 9.75 + 8 + 8.75 ) ÷ 9 =
    TI-84 Plus ( 75.5 − 6.5 − 9.75 + 8 + 8.75 ) ÷ 9 ENTER

    Display: 8.4444 on both. The sum inside the brackets is 76.

Back to the story

In 2004 the ISU replaced the 6.0 system with a new one, created in response to Salt Lake City. Up to nine judges mark each element; the highest and lowest marks are thrown out and the rest averaged. With nine judges that is the middle seven: one in nine (11.1%) dropped at each end, about a 22% trimmed mean.

In the walkthrough’s panel the 9.75 and 6.50 pull the plain mean to 8.3889. The trimmed mean is 8.4643; the median, 8.50.

Let the top judge lean harder: 10.00 instead of 9.75. The plain mean rises by 0.25/9 = 0.0278. The trimmed mean does not move: the most extreme judge is always the one thrown away.

Trimming caps the damage; it does not remove it. An 8.75 nudged to 9.00, inside the pack, still lifts the trimmed mean by 0.25/7 = 0.0357.

(The 2002 event was judged on ordinal placings under 6.0; these marks only illustrate the method.)

Exam traps
  • Using n × y/100 instead of (n + 1) × y/100. In the worked example that puts Q1 at position 3, −0.80%, instead of 3.25, −0.675%.
  • Forgetting to sort first. Reading position 3.25 in the twelve returns as reported gives Q1 = 1.975%, nonsense for a quartile that should sit below the median.
  • Trusting the TI-84’s Q1 and Q3. They agree with the CFA formula only when n is odd; for the twelve returns it shows −0.55 and 2.35 instead of −0.675 and 2.475.
  • Mixing up trimmed (drop the extremes, divide by fewer) and winsorized (replace them, divide by all n), and misreading the names: a 5% trimmed mean drops 2.5% at each end; a 95% winsorized mean resets 2.5% at each end.

2. Why. One of nine judges gives a mark far above all the others. Why does a trimmed mean that drops the highest and lowest marks resist that judge, when the plain mean does not?

  • That is the winsorized mean. A trimmed mean does not replace the extreme marks; it drops them.

  • Right. A dropped mark carries zero weight, however far out it is; in the plain mean each mark carries 1/n.

  • That is the median. The trimmed mean here averages seven marks, not one.

Explanation

The plain mean is Σx/n: a judge who marks d points too high lifts it by d/n, and the bigger d, the bigger the lift. Sorting first and dropping the top and bottom marks gives the most extreme judge a weight of zero, so pushing the mark further out changes nothing. In the walkthrough, 9.75 → 10.00 moves the plain mean by 0.0278 and the trimmed mean by 0.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what is the difference between a trimmed and a winsorized mean, and why does figure skating’s scoring throw out the highest and lowest marks?

Model answer

Both limit the pull of extreme values: a trimmed mean drops a set share of the lowest and highest observations and averages the rest, while a winsorized mean replaces them with the nearest values kept and averages all n. Skating drops the top and bottom marks so that no single judge’s extreme mark, however far out, can move the score.

A complete answer says: (1) both limit the influence of extremes; (2) trimmed drops them (and divides by fewer), winsorized replaces them with the nearest retained values (and divides by n); (3) with the top and bottom marks dropped, the most extreme judge carries no weight.
