A portfolio’s expected return is the weighted average of its assets’ returns, but its risk is lower than the average risk whenever the assets don’t move in lockstep.

Markowitz in the library

As a graduate student at the University of Chicago, Harry Markowitz spent an afternoon in the library reading John Burr Williams’s 1938 book, The Theory of Investment Value.

Williams said a stock is worth the present value of its expected dividends. Markowitz saw a hole in that. If investors cared only about expected value, each would put every dollar into the single stock with the best expected return.

Nobody does that. People spread their money around, so they must care about risk too.

In March 1952, aged 24, he published “Portfolio Selection” in the Journal of Finance (pages 77–91). Its argument: judge a portfolio as a whole, by its expected return and its variance, not one stock at a time.

He later recalled that at his dissertation defence Milton Friedman objected that this was not economics; Markowitz took him to be only half serious. In 1990 the work won Markowitz a share of the Nobel prize in economics, with Merton Miller and William Sharpe.

Here is the puzzle. Say you hold only bonds, whose returns have a standard deviation of 8%. You move some money into stocks, whose SD is 20%. Surely the mix gets riskier? Markowitz says it can get safer. How can adding a risky asset make a portfolio less risky?

The idea

Put 60% of your money in stocks and 40% in bonds. Two questions: what return do you expect, and how much will the result wobble?

Return is an average. Each dollar earns what its own asset earns. Stocks expected to make 9% and bonds 4% give the mix 0.6 × 9% + 0.4 × 4% = 7%. No surprises.

Risk is not. Think of a home’s heating bill and its cooling bill. Each swings a lot with the weather, but a freezing month is rarely a scorching one, so the total swings much less than either bill. The ups and downs partly cancel.

Assets do the same, unless they move in perfect lockstep. How much cancels is set by Day 25’s correlation, ρ. For the 60/40 mix (SDs 20% and 8%), the portfolio’s SD, σp, is: at ρ = +1, 15.20%, the plain average of the SDs; at ρ = 0.2, 13.02%; at ρ = 0, 12.42%; at ρ = −1, 8.80%.

The expected return stays 7% every time. Only the risk moves. That is Markowitz’s point: judge assets by what they do together.

Why it works

You already have the three parts: a probability-weighted average (Day 26), variance as the average squared surprise (Days 24 and 26), and covariance as the average product of two surprises (Day 25).

Step 1: the return. In any one outcome, 60 cents of each dollar earns the stock return and 40 cents the bond return: Rp = w1R1 + w2R2. Average over all outcomes and the weights come along: E(Rp) = w1E(R1) + w2E(R2).

Step 2: the surprise. Subtract the second line from the first. Call each asset’s surprise d (its return minus its expected return). The portfolio’s surprise is w1d1 + w2d2.

Step 3: square it. Variance averages the squared surprise, and (a + b)2 = a2 + b2 + 2ab:

(w1d1 + w2d2)2 = w12d12 + w22d22 + 2w1w2d1d2

Picture a square with side a + b. It splits into an a-by-a square, a b-by-b square and two a-by-b rectangles. That is the 2.

Step 4: average each piece. The average d12 is σ12; the average d22 is σ22; the average d1d2 is the covariance, Day 25’s “votes”. So σp2 = w12σ12 + w22σ22 + 2w1w2Cov12.

Step 5: bring in ρ. Day 25 defined ρ = Cov / (σ1σ2). Turned round: Cov12 = ρ12σ1σ2.

Step 6: why risk falls below the average. At ρ = +1 the three pieces are exactly (w1σ1 + w2σ2)2, so σp is the weighted average of the SDs. Any ρ below +1 shrinks the cross term, so σp drops below that average. At ρ = −1 the pieces become (w1σ1 − w2σ2)2, which can even be zero.

From a probability table. When states and probabilities replace past data, “average” means Day 26’s probability-weighted sum: Cov = Σ P × dA × dB. No n − 1: the probabilities describe the whole distribution, so nothing is estimated from a sample.

In plain words: returns simply average. Risks average only when the assets move in lockstep; otherwise their wobbles partly cancel, and the cross term measures how much.

E(Rp) = w1E(R1) + w2E(R2)

σp2 = w12σ12 + w22σ22 + 2w1w2ρ12σ1σ2

Cov(RA, RB) = Σ P(s) × [RA,s − E(RA)] × [RB,s − E(RB)]

Memorize
  • Expected return of a portfolio: E(Rp) = Σ wi × E(Ri)
  • Variance of a two-asset portfolio: σp2 = w12σ12 + w22σ22 + 2 w1 w2 ρ12 σ1 σ2
  • Covariance from correlation: Cov12 = ρ12 × σ1 × σ2
  • Covariance from a joint probability table: Cov(RA, RB) = Σ P(RA,i, RB,j) × [RA,i − E(RA)] × [RB,j − E(RB)]

Worked example

Problem. Stocks (A) and bonds (B) in three states of the economy. Boom, probability 0.3: A = 25%, B = 4%. Normal, 0.5: A = 10%, B = 8%. Recession, 0.2: A = −15%, B = 5%. Find the covariance and the correlation of their returns, working in percent.

By hand
  1. Expected returns: E(RA) = 0.3 × 25 + 0.5 × 10 + 0.2 × (−15) = 7.5 + 5 − 3 = 9.5%. E(RB) = 0.3 × 4 + 0.5 × 8 + 0.2 × 5 = 1.2 + 4 + 1 = 6.2%.
  2. Boom: 0.3 × (25 − 9.5) × (4 − 6.2) = 0.3 × 15.5 × (−2.2) = −10.23. Stocks above average, bonds below: a vote for “opposite”.
  3. Normal: 0.5 × (10 − 9.5) × (8 − 6.2) = 0.5 × 0.5 × 1.8 = 0.45.
  4. Recession: 0.2 × (−15 − 9.5) × (5 − 6.2) = 0.2 × (−24.5) × (−1.2) = 5.88. Both below average: a vote for “together”.
  5. Add the votes: Cov = −10.23 + 0.45 + 5.88 = −3.90 (%2). In decimals, divide by 1002: −0.00039.
  6. SDs the same way (Day 26): σA = √(0.3 × 15.52 + 0.5 × 0.52 + 0.2 × 24.52) = √192.25 = 13.8654%. σB = √(0.3 × 2.22 + 0.5 × 1.82 + 0.2 × 1.22) = √3.36 = 1.8330%.
  7. ρ = Cov / (σAσB) = −3.90 / (13.8654 × 1.8330) = −0.1534: the two lean, a little, toward moving in opposite directions.

Back to the story

Markowitz’s whole insight lives in the cross term. The 60/40 portfolio expects the plain average, 7.0%, but its σp is 13.02%, not 15.2%.

Now the puzzle. Start with all bonds: 4% expected, σ = 8%. Move 10% of the money into stocks.

E(Rp) = 0.1 × 9 + 0.9 × 4 = 4.5%. σp2 = 0.01 × 400 + 0.81 × 64 + 2 × 0.1 × 0.9 × 0.2 × 20 × 8 = 4 + 51.84 + 5.76 = 61.6, so σp = 7.85%.

More return and less risk, by adding the riskier asset. The weights are squared: a 10% slice of stocks adds only 0.12 × 400 = 4 of its own variance, while the bonds’ term falls by 12.16. With ρ = 0.2, the cross term (5.76) cannot make up the gap.

That is what diversifying buys, and why nobody owns just one stock. Day 33 finds the mix with the least risk of all.

Exam traps
  • Averaging the SDs. 0.6 × 20 + 0.4 × 8 = 15.2% is σp only when ρ = +1; at ρ = 0.2 it is 13.02%. The weighted-average SD is a ceiling, not the answer.
  • Forgetting the 2 in the cross term: 144 + 10.24 + 7.68 = 161.92 gives 12.72% instead of 13.02%. Remember the two rectangles in the (a + b)2 square.
  • Mixing percent and decimals. Stay in one unit: 20 and 8 give a variance of 169.6 (%2), 0.20 and 0.08 give 0.01696; both roots are 13.02%. A covariance quoted as 0.0032 is 32 in %2: typed unchanged into a percent sum, it gives 12.42%.
  • Dividing a probability-table covariance by n − 1. Weight each state by its probability and add; n − 1 is for a sample of past returns (Day 25). Treating the worked example’s three states as a sample gives −1.90, not −3.90.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why can adding a risky asset lower a portfolio’s risk?

Model answer

A portfolio’s variance is not just each asset’s own variance weighted in: it also has a cross term, 2w1w2ρσ1σ2, set by how the assets move together. When ρ is below +1 their ups and downs partly cancel, so σp is below the weighted average of the SDs (13.02% instead of 15.2% for the 60/40 mix), while the expected return is still the plain weighted average.

A complete answer says: (1) portfolio variance includes a covariance (cross) term, not only each asset’s own variance; (2) when ρ < +1 that term is smaller than for perfectly correlated assets, so σp is below the weighted-average σ; (3) the assets’ ups and downs partly offset, while the expected return is still the weighted average.
