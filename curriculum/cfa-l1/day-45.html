To compare two groups, test the gap between their means against a pooled standard error; when the data come in matched pairs, test the differences instead, and the noise the pairs share cancels out.

One twin in space, one on Earth

On 27 March 2015 the NASA astronaut Scott Kelly launched to the International Space Station. He stayed 340 days and came home on 1 March 2016. (Both are US dates; at the launch and landing sites in Kazakhstan it was already the next day.)

His identical twin, Mark Kelly, a retired astronaut, stayed on Earth.

NASA ran the same battery of tests on both brothers before, during and after the flight. The results appeared in Science on 12 April 2019, from Francine Garrett-Bakelman and colleagues.

Among the findings: Scott’s telomeres, the caps on the ends of his chromosomes, lengthened in space and shortened again after he came home. And by NASA’s count, 91.3% of his gene expression returned to its preflight baseline within six months of landing.

People differ enormously from one another. So why measure Scott against his own twin, and against his own preflight self, rather than against a crowd of strangers? And when should two sets of numbers be tested as matched pairs instead of as two independent groups?

The idea

On Day 44 you tested one mean against a fixed number. Today there are two groups, and the question is whether their means differ: H0: μ1 = μ2.

The recipe is Day 44’s: the gap you see, divided by its standard error. What changes is the standard error, and that depends on how the data were collected.

  • Independent samples. The groups have nothing to do with each other: Fund X over one set of months, Fund Y over another. Test the difference in means, with a pooled variance.
  • Dependent (paired) samples. Every observation has a partner: the same months, the same firms, the same person before and after. Subtract inside each pair and test the mean of the differences.

An everyday picture: do new shoes make runners faster? Time 20 people in old shoes and 20 others in new ones, and fast and slow runners drown out the shoe. Time the same 20 people in both kinds of shoe, and each runner’s own speed cancels. Only the shoe is left.

Why it works

1. Day 44’s recipe. t = (what you saw − what H0 says) / (standard error of what you saw).

2. What you saw is a gap: X̄1 − X̄2. H0 says the true gap μ1 − μ2 is 0.

3. How much does a gap wobble? Day 32’s portfolio variance with weights +1 and −1 (long one, short the other): Var(X̄1 − X̄2) = Var(X̄1) + Var(X̄2) − 2Cov(X̄1, X̄2).

4. Independent samples: unrelated groups have no covariance, so the last term is 0. Day 43: Var(X̄) = σ²/n. So the gap’s variance is σ1²/n1 + σ2²/n2.

5. Pool. Assume both populations share one variance, σ²: then both samples estimate it, so use both. (n − 1)s² is just a sample’s sum of squared deviations (Day 24). Add the two sums, and divide by the degrees of freedom left: each sample spent one on its own mean, so n1 + n2 − 2. That is sp²: an average of s1² and s2² in which the bigger sample counts more.

6. The pooled t. Put sp² in place of σ² in both terms and take the root: SE = √(sp²/n1 + sp²/n2), with n1 + n2 − 2 df.

7. Paired samples: now the covariance in step 3 is large, and step 4 is wrong. Subtract first: di = Ai − Bi, one difference per pair. The n differences are a single sample, and Day 44’s test applies as it stands: t = d̄/(sd/√n), with n − 1 df.

8. Why pairing wins. Var(A − B) = sA² + sB² − 2Cov(A, B). In the walkthrough sA² = 5.73 and sB² = 5.53: without the covariance the differences would have a variance of about 11.3 (SD 3.36). With it, 0.0377 (SD 0.19). The swings the two share cancel.

In plain words: a test asks whether the gap is big compared with how much it wobbles. Pairing compares each thing with its own partner, so almost nothing is left to wobble.

Memorize
  • Pooled variance: sp² = [(n1 − 1)s1² + (n2 − 1)s2²] / (n1 + n2 − 2)
  • t-test for a difference in means (independent samples, equal variances): t = [(X̄1 − X̄2) − (μ1 − μ2)] / √(sp²/n1 + sp²/n2), df = n1 + n2 − 2 (under H0: μ1 = μ2 the numerator is just X̄1 − X̄2)
  • Paired-comparisons t-test: t = (d̄ − μd0) / sd̄, with sd̄ = sd / √n, df = n − 1 (usually μd0 = 0)
  • Which test for matched data (same months, same firms)? The paired-comparisons test (samples are dependent)

Worked example

Problem. (a) Fund X: 24 monthly returns, mean 1.10%, s = 2.0%. Fund Y: 20 different months, mean 0.60%, s = 1.8%. Assuming equal population variances, test H0: μX = μY at 5%. (b) Suppose instead X and Y had been measured over the same 20 months, with mean difference 0.50% and sd = 0.80%. Test H0: μd = 0 at 5%.

By hand
  1. (a) Pooled variance: sp² = [23 × 2.0² + 19 × 1.8²]/(24 + 20 − 2) = (92 + 61.56)/42 = 3.6562.
  2. (a) Standard error: √(3.6562/24 + 3.6562/20) = √(0.1523 + 0.1828) = 0.5789.
  3. (a) t = (1.10 − 0.60)/0.5789 = 0.8637, df = 42. Critical ±2.018: fail to reject.
  4. (b) Paired: sd̄ = 0.80/√20 = 0.1789; t = 0.50/0.1789 = 2.7951, df = 19. Critical ±2.093: reject.
  5. The same 0.50-point gap. The paired design strips out the month-to-month noise the two funds share.

Back to the story

Matching cancels whatever the pair shares. Scott and Mark share their genes; Scott before the flight and Scott after it share everything but the flight. A crowd of strangers brings its own differences, which could bury the effect of a year in space.

One pair of brothers is far too few for a t-test, but the logic of the design is exactly today’s.

The walkthrough showed it in returns. A and B ride the same market (r = 0.9968). Each swings by about 2.4 points a month, which swamps a 0.48-point edge: as independent samples, t = 0.35. Subtract month by month and the shared swings cancel: sd = 0.19, and t = 6.10, far past 2.571.

Same numbers, opposite verdict. How the data were collected decides which test is right.

Exam traps
  • Running the pooled test on paired data. Same months, same firms, before and after: the samples are dependent, so test the differences. In the walkthrough the pooled test (t = 0.35) misses the edge the paired test finds (t = 6.10).
  • The wrong degrees of freedom. Pooled: n1 + n2 − 2 (10 above). Paired: n − 1, with n the number of pairs (5 above), not 2n − 2.
  • Forgetting what pooling assumes: independent samples from populations with equal variances. Without both, the pooled formula is the wrong tool.
  • Dividing d̄ by sd instead of by sd/√n. Above, 0.4833/0.1941 = 2.49 would fail to reject at 2.571: the wrong verdict.

2. Why. In the walkthrough the same six months of returns gave a pooled t of 0.35 but a paired t of 6.10. Why can the paired test find a difference the pooled test misses?

  • Right. The swings the two share cancel inside each difference: sd = 0.19 against about 2.4 for each series.

  • Backwards. It has fewer: n − 1 = 5 against n1 + n2 − 2 = 10, so its critical value is higher (2.571 vs 2.228). It wins in spite of that.

  • Pairing does not need equal variances, but an assumption is not a number in the formula. What raised t is the far smaller standard error.

Explanation

The numerator is the same in both tests: the mean of the differences always equals the difference of the means (0.4833). Only the denominator changes: 1.3704 pooled, 0.0792 paired, about 17 times smaller. It shrinks because the covariance term removes what the two series share.

4. Exam style. An analyst compares two portfolios over the same 36 months. Their mean monthly returns are 1.12% and 0.70%, with standard deviations of 4.1% and 3.9%; the standard deviation of the 36 monthly differences is 1.2%. For H0: no difference in mean returns, the appropriate test statistic and its degrees of freedom are closest to:

  • The pooled test on dependent samples: sp² = 16.01, SE = 0.9431, t = 0.42/0.9431 = 0.45. It ignores that the months are shared.

  • The right statistic with the pooled test’s df. A paired test has n − 1 = 35 df, with n the 36 pairs.

  • Right. Same months, so paired: d̄ = 1.12 − 0.70 = 0.42; sd̄ = 1.2/√36 = 0.20; t = 2.10, with 36 − 1 = 35 df.

Explanation

“Same 36 months” signals dependent samples, and the question even hands you sd. t = 0.42/(1.2/6) = 2.10, above the critical ±2.030 for 35 df: reject at 5%. The two SDs (4.1% and 3.9%) are there to tempt you into the pooled test.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: when do you use the paired-comparisons test rather than the pooled t-test, and what exactly do you test?

Model answer

When the observations come in matched pairs (the same months, the same firms, before and after), the samples are dependent, so I take the difference inside each pair and test whether the mean difference is zero: t = d̄/(sd/√n) with n − 1 df. The pooled t-test is for independent samples from populations with equal variances.

A complete answer says: (1) paired when observations are matched, so the samples are dependent; (2) test the mean of the differences, with df = n − 1 (n = number of pairs); (3) pooled needs independent samples and equal variances.
