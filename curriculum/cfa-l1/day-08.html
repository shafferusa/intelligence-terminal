The bond Britain could keep forever

In 1917 Britain sold the 5% War Loan to pay for the First World War. In 1932 it was converted into a new stock, the 3½% War Loan: £3.50 a year for every £100, with no date by which it had to be repaid.

The government could choose to repay it at any time from 1 December 1952. It never had to. So, generation after generation, it simply paid the interest.

Then, on 3 December 2014, the UK Debt Management Office announced that the War Loan would be repaid at par, £100 for every £100 of stock, on Monday 9 March 2015. £1,938.6 million of it was still in issue. HM Treasury titled its announcement “Chancellor to repay the nation’s First World War debt”.

On 5 July 2015 the last of Britain’s undated gilts went too, among them the 2½% Consolidated Stock: the “consols”, with roots in 18th-century borrowing. That completed the repayment of about £2.6 billion of historical debt.

Here is the puzzle. A bond that never has to be repaid pays £3.50 a year, forever. What is that worth? And why, 83 years after 1932, did it suddenly pay the Treasury to hand back £100 for it?

The idea

A perpetuity is a level payment that never stops: A every period, the first one a period from now, forever. Undated bonds like the War Loan are perpetuities. So is a non-callable preferred share with a fixed dividend.

Forever sounds like an infinite amount of money. Its value is not, because money far away is worth almost nothing today. At 2%, £3.50 due in 100 years is worth 48p now. Due in 300 years, less than a penny. So the total settles on a number.

That number is A / r. Here it is in one picture. Put £175 in an account paying 2%. Each year it earns £3.50. Take the £3.50 out, and the £175 is still there to earn £3.50 again. Forever. So £3.50 a year forever costs exactly £175, which is 3.50 / 0.02.

Turn it round and a price tells you the rate: r = A / PV. £3.50 a year forever that sells for £100 offers 3.5%.

And if the first payment comes later? A / r still works. It just lands one period before that first payment, and you discount it back to today like any single sum (Day 2).

Why it works

Start from Day 5. N level payments, the first at t = 1, are worth PV = A × [1 − (1 + r)−N] / r.

Let N run on. (1 + r)−N is Day 2’s discount factor: what £1 paid at N is worth today. It shrinks toward zero. At 2%: 0.8203 for N = 10, 0.1380 for N = 100, 0.00005 for N = 500.

So the bracket becomes 1. With (1 + r)−N gone, PV = A × (1 − 0) / r = A / r.

Watch it settle. £3.50 a year at 2%: for 30 years, 78.39; 100 years, 150.84; 500 years, 174.99; forever, 175.00.

A second proof, in one line. Call the perpetuity’s value P. A period from now you collect A and still own a perpetuity, worth P again. So today P = (A + P) / (1 + r). Multiply out: P + rP = A + P, so rP = A and P = A / r. Check: (3.50 + 175) / 1.02 = 175.00.

Why A / r sits one period before the first payment. The formula values payments at t = 1, 2, 3, … from t = 0, one period earlier. Slide the stream so the first payment is at t, and A / r is its value at t − 1. From there it is a single sum: divide by (1 + r)t−1.

In plain words: A / r is the deposit whose interest is exactly A. Spend only the interest and the money never runs out.

Two relatives, for later. A payment that is A1 a period from now and then grows at g forever is worth A1 / (r − g), only if r > g: shares, on Day 19. And in property, the cap rate is net operating income (NOI) / value, so value = NOI / cap rate: $500,000 of NOI at a 5% cap rate is worth $10 million.

Memorize
  • PV = A / r (a level perpetuity, first payment one period from now)
  • r = A / PV (the implied rate of a perpetuity)
  • PV0 = (A / r) / (1 + r)(t − 1) (first payment at time t)
  • PV = A1 / (r − g), valid only if r > g (growing perpetuity, preview)
  • Cap rate = NOI / property value, so value = NOI / cap rate (a perpetuity with A = NOI; low priority)

Worked example

Problem. A perpetuity pays $6 a year, the first payment one year from now. (a) At 8%, what is it worth? (b) If it trades at $80, what rate does it offer? (c) At 8%, what is a $6 perpetuity worth today if its first payment comes at the end of year 4?

By hand
  1. (a) PV = A / r = 6 / 0.08 = 75.00.
  2. (b) r = A / PV = 6 / 80 = 0.075 = 7.50%. Sanity check: a price above 75 means a rate below 8%.
  3. (c) A / r values the payments one period before the first one: 6 / 0.08 = 75.00 at t = 3.
  4. Discount 3 periods: 1.083 = 1.259712, so PV0 = 75.00 / 1.259712 = 59.54.
  5. Check by subtraction: the 3 missing payments (t = 1 to 3) are worth 6 × 2.577097 = 15.46, and 75.00 − 15.46 = 59.54.

Back to the story

The 3½% War Loan was a perpetuity: £3.50 a year per £100, no end date. So the stream was worth A / r, with r the market’s rate for very long money, not the 3.5% printed on it.

The coupon rate is the break-even. At 3.5%, the stream is worth 3.50 / 0.035 = £100.00, exactly what the Treasury would pay to end it. Above 3.5% it is worth less: at 4%, £87.50. Whenever long rates were above 3.5%, repaying at par would have been a gift to the holders.

In December 2014 the 30-year gilt yield, a fair stand-in for a rate on money lent forever, hit a record low of 2.639%. At that rate £3.50 forever is worth £132.63. The option was the government’s: it could end that stream for £100 and borrow the £100 at well under 3.5%. On £1,938.6 million, 3.5% was about £67.9 million a year. So it repaid.

Exam traps
  • Discounting a late-starting perpetuity t periods instead of t − 1. A / r already sits one period before the first payment: in the worked example, 3 periods give 59.54; 4 give 55.13.
  • Using the coupon rate instead of the market rate. At its own coupon rate every perpetual bond is worth exactly par (3.50 / 0.035 = 100). Use the market’s required rate: at 2%, 175.
  • Forgetting that A / r counts payments from t = 1 on and leaves out any payment today. If one is paid now too, add it: 6 + 75 = 81, which is 75 × 1.08, Day 6’s (1 + r) again.
  • Typing the rate as a percent in plain arithmetic. In I/Y you type 2; in A ÷ r you divide by 0.02. 3.5 ÷ 2 = 1.75 is the slip.

2. Why. A perpetuity pays forever, yet its value today is a finite number, A / r. Why?

  • Right. Shrinking by a fixed factor each period is a geometric series, and with r > 0 it has a finite sum.

  • A / r assumes the payments never stop and nothing is ever repaid. The War Loan never had to be repaid, and A / r still valued it.

  • There is no inflation in A / r. Discounting alone does it: any positive rate makes far-off payments worth almost nothing today, even with prices flat.

Explanation

At 5%, each discounted payment is 0.952381 times the one before. All the payments after year 100 together are a perpetuity starting at year 101, worth (A / r) / 1.05100: just 0.76% of the total. The tail keeps going but adds almost nothing. If r were 0, the terms would never shrink and the sum would be infinite, which is why A / 0 has no value.

dollars
Hint Two steps. First get the payment from the price: A = PV × r. Then value that payment at the new rate: A / r.
Solution

A = 80 × 0.05 = 4.00 a year. At 4%: 4.00 / 0.04 = 100.00.

BA II Plus: 80 × 0.05 = (shows 4.0000), then ÷ 0.04 = → 100.0000. TI-84 Plus: 80 × 0.05 ENTER (shows 4.0000), then ÷ 0.04 ENTER (the screen writes Ans/0.04) → 100.0000.

The rate fell by a fifth (5% to 4%) and the price rose by a quarter (80 to 100, +25.00%). Perpetuities are very sensitive to rates, which is the story’s whole point.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: why do you discount a perpetuity whose first payment is at time t for t − 1 periods, not t?

Model answer

A / r always gives a perpetuity’s value one period before its first payment, because the formula assumes the first payment comes one period after the date it values. With the first payment at t, A / r is the value at t − 1, so it only needs discounting t − 1 periods back to today, as a single sum: PV0 = (A / r) / (1 + r)t−1.

A complete answer says: (1) A / r is the value one period before the first payment; (2) with the first payment at t, that is time t − 1; (3) from there it is a single sum, discounted t − 1 periods.
