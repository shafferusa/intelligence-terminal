To price a stream that changes every year, discount each payment on its own, add, and take off the price; the one rate that makes that total exactly zero is the return the buyer earns.

From Day 2. What is the discount factor for a cash flow due in 3 years at 8% a year?

  • Right: DF = 1 / 1.083 = 1 / 1.259712 = 0.7938. A dollar due in 3 years is worth about 79 cents today.

  • That is 1.083, the growth factor: it runs money forward. Discounting divides by it.

Why

Keep this factor in mind: today every cash flow gets its own discount factor like this one. In the worked example, $150 due in year 3 at 8% is worth 150 × 0.7938 = 119.07.

Selling the tobacco settlement

On 23 November 1998 the four largest US cigarette makers, Philip Morris, R.J. Reynolds, Brown & Williamson and Lorillard, signed the Master Settlement Agreement with 46 states, the District of Columbia and five territories. It settled the states’ claims for the health-care costs of smoking.

The companies agreed to pay about $206 billion over the first 25 years, in annual instalments. Each instalment is adjusted for inflation and for how many cigarettes are sold. So the payments are anything but level.

Many states did not want to wait. They sold their future payments for cash now. In October 2007 Ohio’s Buckeye Tobacco Settlement Financing Authority sold $5.53 billion of bonds backed by Ohio’s share, reported at the time as the state’s largest borrowing ever. Investors paid cash up front; Ohio’s tobacco payments were to repay them.

Days 5 to 9 priced streams of equal payments. This one changes every year. How do you put one price on it, and what return does the buyer actually earn?

The idea

Equal payments allowed shortcuts: the annuity and perpetuity formulas. Real streams are lumpy: a project, a business, a settlement. There is no shortcut for them, and you don’t need one.

Net present value (NPV). Bring every cash flow back to today with its own Day 2 discount, then add them all, counting what you pay today as a negative. The total is what the deal adds to your wealth, in today’s dollars, at the rate r you require.

Read the sign. NPV > 0: the stream earns more than r. NPV = 0: exactly r. NPV < 0: less than r.

Internal rate of return (IRR). The one rate that makes the NPV exactly zero. It is the stream’s own return, and it needs no outside rate at all.

An everyday picture: a friend asks to borrow $470 and promises back $120, $120, $115, $110 and $105 over five years. Your savings account pays 6%. NPV says whether the friend beats the bank, and by how much. IRR says what rate the friend is really paying.

Why it works

Start from Day 2. One cash flow CFt, due in t years, is worth CFt / (1 + r)t today.

A stream is just several single sums. Discount each one from its own date. The friend’s payments at 6%:

120 / 1.06 = 113.21
120 / 1.062 = 106.80
115 / 1.063 = 96.56
110 / 1.064 = 87.13
105 / 1.065 = 78.46

Now add. All five are in today’s dollars, so they add: 482.16. That is cash-flow additivity: amounts at the same date can be added, like converting every currency to dollars before totalling a bill. Amounts at different dates never add directly.

Count today’s cash too. The $470 is paid at t = 0, and (1 + r)0 = 1, so it enters undiscounted, as a negative: NPV = −470 + 482.16 = 12.16.

Why NPV > 0 means “beats r”. To pay yourself those same five amounts out of a 6% bank account, you would have to deposit 482.16. The friend’s deal gives them to you for 470. That saving, in today’s dollars, is the NPV.

Now the IRR. Raise r and every future term shrinks (Day 2: a higher rate lowers PV), so the NPV falls: 100.00 at 0% (just 570 − 470), 38.90 at 4%, 12.16 at 6%, −0.38 at 7%. It crosses zero just below 7%, at 6.9689%. That crossing is the IRR.

No algebra isolates r here (it sits inside five different powers), so the calculator finds it by fast trial and error.

Check it. Put 470 in an account paying 6.9689%. After a year it holds 502.75; take out 120, leaving 382.75. Keep going, and the fifth withdrawal of 105 empties it: 0.00 left. The IRR is the interest the stream pays on the money still tied up in it.

In one sentence: turn every future payment into today’s money, add, and take off what you pay. That is the NPV. The IRR is the rate that makes it come out to exactly zero.

NPV = CF0 + CF1/(1 + r) + CF2/(1 + r)2 + … + CFN/(1 + r)N

NPV = Σt=0N CFt / (1 + r)t

0 = Σt=0N CFt / (1 + IRR)t

CFt
the cash flow at time t: positive if you receive it, negative if you pay it
CF0
today’s cash flow (t = 0), usually the price or outlay, so negative; it is not discounted
t
when each cash flow arrives, in periods from today (0, 1, 2, …, N)
r
the discount rate per period (your required return), as a decimal
N
the period of the last cash flow
Σ
“add up”, for every t from 0 to N
IRR
the rate per period that makes the sum exactly zero
Memorize
  • NPV = Σt=0N CFt / (1 + r)t
  • IRR is the r that makes Σ CFt / (1 + IRR)t = 0
  • Cash-flow additivity: PVs of cash flows measured at the same point in time can be added
  • TI-84: npv(rate in %, CF0, {CF1, CF2, …}[, {freq1, freq2, …}]); irr(CF0, {CF1, CF2, …}[, {freqs}]) returns a percent
  • BA II Plus: CF 2ND CLR WORK; CFo ENTER ↓ C01 ENTER ↓ F01 ENTER ↓ …; NPV I ENTER ↓ CPT; IRR CPT

Worked example

Problem. A project costs 500 today and returns 200, 250 and 150 at the ends of years 1 to 3. At 8%, what is its NPV? Is its IRR above or below 10%?

By hand
  1. Year 1: 200 / 1.08 = 185.19.
  2. Year 2: 250 / 1.082 = 250 / 1.1664 = 214.33.
  3. Year 3: 150 / 1.083 = 150 / 1.259712 = 119.07.
  4. Add, with the cost: NPV = −500 + 185.19 + 214.33 + 119.07 = 18.59. Positive, so the project earns more than 8%.
  5. Try 10%: −500 + 181.82 + 206.61 + 112.70 = 1.13. Still positive, so the IRR is above 10%.
  6. Try 11%: the NPV is −7.24, negative. The zero lies between 10% and 11%; the calculator puts it at 10.13%.
  7. Answer: NPV = 18.59 at 8%; the IRR is above 10% (10.13%).

Back to the story

One price for a lumpy stream: discount each payment from its own year, add, and compare with the price. In the illustration, five payments worth 482.16 at 6% cost 470: NPV +12.16, so the buyer earns more than 6%. The IRR says how much more: 6.97% a year.

But an IRR assumes the payments arrive as projected. If each came in 10% short, the same 470 would earn only 3.06%.

That is what happened to tobacco bonds. Smoking fell faster than projected, and because the payments follow cigarette sales, they came in short. In July 2012 Moody’s projected defaults on many tobacco bonds if sales kept falling about 3% a year. Ohio’s bonds drifted toward default until a refinancing of about $5.35 billion in February 2020 took them off that track.

Exam traps
  • Not clearing the worksheet. Press CF 2ND CLR WORK first: old C0x values stay in memory and quietly join the new stream.
  • Forgetting CF0, or its sign. Without the −470 you get 482.16, the PV of the inflows, not the NPV. With no negative cash flow at all, the IRR has no answer (BA II Plus Error 5, TI-84 ERR:NO SIGN CHNG).
  • Miscounting frequencies. 120 for two years is C01 = 120 with F01 = 2; left at 1, a year drops out and every later payment moves one year earlier. On the TI-84 the frequency list needs one entry per amount, in the same order.
  • Typing the rate as a decimal. npv(.06,…) means 0.06%, and returns 98.9994 instead of 12.1557. Both calculators take percent: I=6 on the BA II Plus, npv(6,… on the TI-84.

2. Why. A project costs money today and pays back cash in later years. At a discount rate of 8% its NPV is positive. Its IRR must be:

  • This reverses the NPV–rate link. A higher rate shrinks every future inflow, so the NPV falls as the rate rises. It is still positive at 8%, so it can only reach zero further up.

  • This confuses NPV > 0 with NPV = 0. The IRR is the rate where the NPV is zero, and at 8% it is not zero yet.

  • Right. With the outlay first and inflows after, the NPV falls as the rate rises. It is still above zero at 8%, so the rate that brings it to zero, the IRR, lies above 8%.

Explanation

The worked example shows the slide: NPV 18.59 at 8%, 1.13 at 10%, −7.24 at 11%, so the zero sits at 10.13%. For an outlay followed by inflows, a positive NPV at r means the IRR is above r: the stream earns more than r.

4. Exam style. An investment costs $1,000 today and pays $400 at the end of each of the next 3 years, then $300 at the end of year 4. At a discount rate of 10%, its NPV is closest to:

  • Right: C01 = 400 with F01 = 3, C02 = 300, CF0 = −1,000, at 10%.

Explanation

BA II Plus: CF 2ND CLR WORK 1000 +|− ENTER ↓ 400 ENTER ↓ 3 ENTER ↓ 300 ENTER NPV 10 ENTER ↓ CPT → NPV = 199.6448.

TI-84 Plus: APPS 1:Finance 7:npv( and complete the line to read npv(10,−1000,{400,300},{3,1}), then ENTER → 199.6448.

5. Say it back. In one or two sentences: what are NPV and IRR, and how does the sign of the NPV tell you whether a stream beats your required rate?

Model answer

NPV brings every cash flow back to today from its own date and adds them, counting today’s cost as negative; a positive NPV means the stream earns more than the discount rate, because a bank at that rate would need more money up front to pay out the same amounts. The IRR is the one rate that makes the NPV exactly zero: the stream’s own return.

A complete answer says: (1) discount each cash flow from its own date and add them (cash-flow additivity: only values at the same date add); (2) NPV > 0 means the stream earns more than the discount rate (NPV < 0, less; NPV = 0, exactly that rate); (3) the IRR is the rate that makes the NPV zero.
