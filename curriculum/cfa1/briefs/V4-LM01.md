## Learning outcomes

1. Describe the steps of the financial statement analysis framework. Section 2, pp.5–9 (PDF 17–21).
2. Describe the roles of financial statement analysis. Section 3, pp.9–12 (PDF 21–24).
3. Describe the importance of regulatory filings, notes and supplementary information, management commentary and audit reports. Section 4, pp.13–26 (PDF 25–38).
4. Describe implications of alternative reporting systems (mainly IFRS vs US GAAP) and why analysts must monitor changes in standards. Section 5, pp.27–29 (PDF 39–41).
5. Describe information sources used besides annual and interim reports. Section 6, pp.30–31 (PDF 42–43).

## Core principles

- **Purpose first, numbers second.** Six phases: purpose and context; collect data; process data; analyze/interpret; develop and communicate conclusions; follow-up. Ratios are listed among the outputs of processing and feed into interpretation (pp.6–8, PDF 18–20).
- **Process is adjusted, not raw.** Comparability problems (leasing vs buying, accounting policy choices, tax) must be adjusted for before comparing firms; databases often do not adjust. Forecasts and valuations are phase-4 outputs per Exhibit 1, although the text also mentions them as processing tools (p.7, PDF 19).
- **Analysis serves decisions by different capital providers.** Equity holders are paid from residual profit and care about profitability and per-share value. Lenders are paid fixed interest and principal, so they care about cash generation and leverage. Analysis looks at past and current results to form expectations and assess risk (pp.5, 9–12).
- **Regulated sources trade timeliness against assurance.** Annual filings (audited) are slower and more reliable. Interim filings are unaudited and faster. Current-event filings are fastest. Pay and voting information sits in the proxy, not the earnings release (pp.14–17, PDF 26–29).
- **Notes carry the choices that make statements comparable or not.** Policy flexibility fits diverse businesses but hurts comparability, so read the notes and adjust (p.18, PDF 30).
- **Segment data shows where profit and loss really arise.** A big-revenue segment can lose money. Segment definitions rest on management judgement (pp.19–20).
- **An audit gives reasonable, not absolute, assurance.** Sampling and estimates mean a clean opinion is no guarantee. The investor still bears the risk of misstatement or fraud, so keep healthy skepticism. KAMs/CAMs flag judgement areas, not necessarily the analyst's top concerns (pp.23, 26, PDF 35, 38).
- **Standards differ and are not reconciled.** Without a required IFRS-to-US GAAP reconciliation, analysts cannot always fully adjust, so they must monitor standard-setting and new transaction types from a user's view (pp.27–29, PDF 39–41).

## Vocabulary

- Top-down approach — start with the economy, then industry, then company (p.7).
- Common-size statements — statements expressed as proportions of a base such as sales (p.8).
- Financial ratios — measures from statement items for profitability, liquidity, leverage, efficiency, valuation (p.8).
- IOSCO — association of securities regulators setting benchmark principles, not a legal regulator (p.13).
- SRO — self-regulatory organization with delegated oversight, itself supervised (p.14).
- PCAOB — US overseer of public-company auditors, created by Sarbanes-Oxley (p.14).
- Registration statement — filing before a new issue, including risk factors and audited statements (pp.15–17).
- Form 10-K / 20-F / 40-F — annual filing by US / other non-US / certain Canadian issuers (pp.15–17).
- Form 10-Q / 6-K — interim reports, unaudited (pp.15–17).
- Form 8-K — current report of major events (pp.15–17).
- DEF-14A — proxy statement: votes, ownership, directors, executive pay (p.15).
- Proxy — permission for another person to vote a holder's shares (p.15).
- Forms 3, 4, 5 and 144 — insider holdings, changes, annual summary; notice of proposed restricted-stock sale (p.17, PDF 29).
- Reg FD — selective disclosure of material non-public information must also be made public (p.16; endnote p.36).
- Operating segment — component with its own revenue, expenses and discrete data, reviewed by top management (pp.19–20).
- MD&A — management's narrative on results, liquidity, trends, risks (p.21).
- Reasonable assurance — high but not absolute confidence in no material misstatement (p.23).
- Unqualified / qualified / adverse / disclaimer — the four audit opinion types (p.23).
- Key / Critical Audit Matters — high-judgement areas flagged by the auditor (international / US) (p.23).
- Exposure draft / comment letter — proposed standard / public feedback on it (p.29).

## Traps

- Starting with ratios. Purpose and decision come first (p.7).
- Placing ratios in a single phase. Check where Exhibit 1 and the text put them (pp.6–8).
- Reading a clean opinion as a guarantee of accuracy. It is reasonable assurance (p.23).
- Treating the glossy annual report as the Form 10-K. The annual report is not SEC-required (p.15).
- Treating MD&A as audited. It usually is not (Germany is an exception) (p.21).
- Saying IOSCO eliminates systemic risk or protects all users. It aims to reduce risk and protect investors (p.13).
- Mixing opinion types. Qualified means an exception or scope limit; adverse means a material departure; disclaimer means no opinion. Opinion labels belong to audits only (p.23).
- Assuming IFRS and US GAAP are fully converged (p.27), or that interim means monthly (interim reports are quarterly or semiannual, p.15).

## Original practice seeds

All hypothetical.

1. **Segment tests.** Firm revenue 100 (millions, one year): A 60, B 25, C 8, D 7; assume C and D also fail the profit and asset tests. A and B pass the 10% test. Reportable revenue is 60 + 25 = 85, which is 85% of the total and clears the 75% floor. Two reportable segments. Now revenues A 45, B 25, C 9, D 9, E 7, F 5: A + B = 70%, below 75%, so add segments. Adding C gives 79%, enough. Three reportable segments.
2. **Asset test.** A segment has 8% of combined revenue but 12% of combined segment assets. The 10% asset test is met, so it is reportable regardless of the revenue test.
3. **Classification.** Firm X (US GAAP) and firm Y (IFRS) each pay 10 interest, with 100 cash flow before interest. X must show operating cash flow of 90. Y may show 100 if it classifies interest as financing, or 90 if operating. Align classification before comparing.
