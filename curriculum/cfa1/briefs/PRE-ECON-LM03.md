## Core principles

- **Three views, one number.** Everything produced is bought and is someone's income, so output, spending and income match; households ultimately own the capital and receive profit (pp.69–70).
- **Value added avoids double counting.** Summing every sale overstates output; count final sales or each stage's sales less input purchases. Used goods and transfers are out; imputed rent is in (pp.71–73).
- **Nominal can rise on price alone.** Real GDP fixes prices at a base year; the deflator is nominal over real, and its percentage change is economy-wide inflation (pp.74–77).
- **Saving must go somewhere.** Private saving funds investment, the government deficit or lending abroad, so a fiscal deficit needs private saving above investment, a trade deficit, or both. It is an after-the-fact identity; it does not say which variable adjusts (pp.79–82).
- **AD slopes down via price-level channels** (money fixed): a lower price level raises real money holdings, lowers rates (investment and big purchases rise) and makes domestic goods cheaper abroad (pp.83–86).
- **SRAS slopes up, LRAS is vertical.** Sticky input costs widen margins in the short run; later wages catch up and output returns to potential. Money expansion lifts output only temporarily, then only prices (pp.87–88, 93).
- **Gaps and shocks.** AD falls: output, prices drop (recessionary gap). AD overshoots: wages later push SRAS left (inflationary gap). SRAS left causes stagflation; stimulating AD cures output at the price of permanently higher prices. (pp.102–109).
- **Diminishing returns make TFP the lasting driver.** More capital per worker gives shrinking gains, so poor countries should, all else equal, converge. Sustainable growth is potential growth, unobservable; estimate it as trend hours growth plus trend productivity growth. Actual growth above potential signals inflation pressure, rate rises and falling bond prices (pp.112–113, 121–123).

## Vocabulary

- GDP — market value of final goods and services produced domestically in a period (p.70).
- Value added — a stage's sales less its input purchases (pp.71–72).
- GDP deflator — nominal/real x 100, an economy-wide price index (p.75).
- Wealth, interest rate, real exchange rate effects — why AD slopes down (pp.83–85).
- Recessionary gap, inflationary gap, stagflation — output below potential; above potential; falling output with rising prices (pp.103, 106, 108).
- Potential GDP — capacity output; unobservable (pp.111–112).
- TFP — output growth unexplained by measured inputs; a residual (pp.112, 115).
- Capital deepening, convergence — more capital per worker; poorer countries catching up (p.113).
- Labor productivity — real GDP per hour worked; observable (p.119).

## Formulas

1. Output = income = expenditure (p.69).
2. Nominal GDP_t = P_t x Q_t; Real GDP_t = P_B x Q_t (P price, Q quantity, B base year; single-good form) (pp.74–75).
3. Deflator = nominal/real x 100; Real = Nominal / (deflator/100); inflation = deflator_t / deflator_(t−1) − 1. Base = 100 (pp.75, 77).
4. GDP = C + I + G + (X − M); G excludes transfers; I includes inventories (p.77).
5. Y + F − S_B − R = C + S_H, giving Y = C + S + T, where T = R − F, S = S_B + S_H (pp.79–80).
6. S = I + (G − T) + (X − M), or G − T = (S − I) − (X − M); an identity (p.80).
7. % change in unit labor cost ≈ % change in wages − % change in productivity; a rise shifts SRAS left (p.96).
8. National income = GDP − capital consumption allowance − statistical discrepancy; disposable income = personal income − personal taxes (Summary pp.124–125).
9. Y = A F(L,K); assumes constant returns, diminishing marginal productivity, no externalities (p.112).
10. Potential growth = TFP growth + W_L x labor growth + W_C x capital growth; income-share weights, W_L + W_C = 1, about 0.7 and 0.3 in developed economies (p.113).
11. Per capita growth = TFP growth + W_C x growth in capital-to-labor ratio (pp.113–114).
12. TFP growth = potential growth − [W_L x labor growth + W_C x capital growth] (p.115).
13. Labor productivity = real GDP / hours; Y/L = A F(1, K/L) (p.119).
14. Potential GDP = hours x productivity; growth ≈ trend hours growth + trend productivity growth, an approximation for small rates (p.121).

## Traps

- Transfers are not G; unsold goods are inventory investment in I.
- Deflate nominal by dividing, not subtracting; annualise multi-year changes by compounding, not simple division.
- The deflator covers all domestic output, not a consumer basket.
- The saving identity is not causal.
- Price-level changes move along curves; other factors shift them. A stronger domestic currency shifts AD left but SRAS right.
- Higher wages shift SRAS left, never LRAS, unless relative input prices change permanently. Any LRAS shift also moves SRAS.
- Stagflation is a supply problem; demand shifts create gaps. Long-run money expansion only raises prices.
- TFP is not pure technology and is unobservable, labor productivity is observable; capital's weight is about 0.3, not 1; participation gains are a one-time lift.

## Original practice seeds

**A (hypothetical).** A farmer sells grain for 0.80 to a miller, who sells flour for 2.00 to a baker, who sells bread for 5.00; the farmer buys no inputs. Value added is 0.80, 1.20, 3.00, totalling 5.00. Summing sales gives 7.80, a double count.

**B (hypothetical).** Base year: 1,000 units at 10.00. Next year: 1,050 units at 10.80. Nominal 11,340; real 10,500 (+5%); deflator 108.0; inflation 8%; nominal growth 13.4% = 1.05 x 1.08 − 1.

**C (hypothetical).** W_L = 0.7, W_C = 0.3; labor +1.0%, capital +3.0%, potential output +2.4%. Inputs add 0.7 + 0.9 = 1.6%, so TFP growth = 0.8%. With capital at +2.0% instead, inputs add 1.3% and TFP is 1.1%.
