## Core principles
- **Gains come from opportunity cost, not productivity.** The country with the smaller sacrifice in making a good should export it. A country with no absolute advantage can still gain (pp.195–196, PDF 203–204). Fails if relative prices do not actually change on opening.
- **Both countries gain only if the trade price lies strictly between the two autarkic prices.** A seller gains more the closer the world price sits to the partner's autarkic price; the two gains sum to the gap between autarkic prices (p.196, PDF 204).
- **Comparative advantage moves** with capital, skills, technology, resources and policy (pp.197–198, PDF 205–206).
- **Ricardo vs Heckscher–Ohlin.** Ricardo: labour is the only variable input; technology gaps create advantage. H-O: capital and labour both vary, technology is the same, and a country exports the good using its relatively abundant factor intensively (pp.198–199, PDF 206–207). In H-O, the export price rises and the import price falls, so derived demand shifts toward the abundant factor: it gains, the scarce factor loses. The models are complementary, not rivals.
- **BOP is double-entry bookkeeping.** The accounts sum to zero (pp.200–203, PDF 208–211).
- **A current account gap is a saving–investment gap.** A deficit means the country spends and invests more than it produces and borrows abroad (pp.204–207, PDF 212–215). A deficit funding investment can raise future repayment capacity; one funding consumption does not.
- **Deficits carry financing risk.** Persistent deficits build foreign claims, investors may demand a higher risk premium, and currency pressure can move from short-run appreciation to long-run depreciation (p.206, PDF 214; Summary pp.208–210).
- **FDI vs FPI.** FDI is long-term ownership of productive assets abroad (makes an MNC); FPI is shorter-term holdings of foreign securities (p.193, PDF 201).

## Vocabulary
- GDP — final output produced inside the borders, whoever owns the factors (p.190).
- GNP — final output produced by a country's citizens' factors, wherever located (p.190).
- Terms of trade — export price index over import price index (p.190, fn 2 p.191).
- Net exports — exports minus imports; positive is a surplus, negative a deficit (p.191).
- Autarky / closed economy — no trade; autarkic prices (p.191).
- Open economy — trades at the world price (p.191).
- Free trade — no restrictions; trade protection — tariffs, quotas and similar (p.191).
- Intra-firm trade — between a parent and its affiliates (pp.193–194).
- Absolute advantage — fewer resources per unit of a good than a partner (p.195).
- Comparative advantage — lower opportunity cost than a partner (p.195).
- Derived demand — demand for an input arising from demand for its product (p.199).
- Unilateral transfers — one-way transfers such as remittances or aid (p.202).
- Inter-temporal trade — swapping future consumption for present consumption through borrowing (p.205).
- Government saving (Sg) — taxes minus government purchases minus net transfers (p.205).

## Formulas
- **Terms of trade** = P_X / P_M (price indices; units of imports per unit of exports). Currency cancels. Higher is better. p.191 fn 2 (PDF 199).
- **Opportunity cost** of good A in units of B = output of B ÷ output of A per worker. Lower OC means comparative advantage. p.195 (PDF 203).
- **Eq. 1** Y = C + I + G (closed economy). p.204 (PDF 212).
- **Eq. 2** Y = C + I + G + X − M (open economy). p.204.
- **Eq. 3** CA = X − M = Y − (C + I + G); treats net exports as the current account, ignoring income and transfers (fn 12). p.205 (PDF 213).
- **Eq. 4** Yd = Y + R − T (R net transfers; T taxes). p.205.
- **Eq. 5** Yd = C + Sp (Sp private saving). p.205.
- **Eq. 6** C = Yd − Sp = Y + R − T − Sp. p.205.
- **Eq. 7** CA = Sp − I + (T − G − R). p.205.
- **Eq. 8** Sp + Sg = I + CA, with Sg = T − G − R. p.205.
- **Eq. 9** Sp = I + CA − Sg. p.206 (PDF 214).
- **Eq. 10** CA = Sp + Sg − I. p.206.
- BOP identity: CA + capital account + financial account (+ statistical discrepancy) = 0.

## Traps
- Higher terms of trade is an improvement. GDP is location-based; GNP is ownership-based.
- Absolute advantage does not set trade patterns; compare opportunity costs (other good ÷ this good) and export the lower-OC good.
- Gains need a price between autarkic prices; the closer to the partner's autarkic price, the more you gain.
- Debit is not "bad": in this reading a debit is an increase in assets or decrease in liabilities, so an import is a debit. Exhibit tables later use credits as + and debits as −.
- Classify BOP items carefully: sales of non-produced, non-financial assets belong in the capital account, while fees paid for services and investment income belong in the current account (pp.201–203).
- X − M is not strictly the current account, though the reading treats them as one.
- A deficit is not automatically bad; ask whether it funds investment or consumption. CA = Sp + Sg − I is an identity, not proof that a fiscal deficit causes a CA deficit.
- H-O effect: the abundant factor gains, the scarce factor loses; the Ricardian model cannot show this.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. Country P makes 30 shirts or 10 phones per worker per day; Q makes 20 shirts or 5 phones. Opportunity cost of a phone: P = 30/10 = 3 shirts, Q = 20/5 = 4 shirts. P has an absolute advantage in both but comparative advantage in phones; Q in shirts. At 3.5 shirts per phone, P gains 0.5 shirt per phone sold and Q saves 0.5 per phone bought. At 3.9, P gains 0.9 and Q 0.1; gains always total 1.
2. Billions per year: Sp = 120, Sg = −30, I = 100. CA = 120 − 30 − 100 = −10, a deficit, financed by a net capital inflow of 10. If Sg = 0, CA = +20; the change of 30 equals the change in Sg.
3. On 1 March a country imports goods worth 5m on 60-day credit: debit goods 5m, credit short-term liability 5m. On 30 April: debit liability 5m, credit cash 5m. Net: goods in, cash out, liability zero.
