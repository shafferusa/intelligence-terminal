## Core principles
- **Avoid, then disclose, then mitigate (VI(A), p.208).** The test is whether a reasonable client or employer would doubt that advice is unbiased. Bias enters through the member's own money, the firm's business ties, or pay structure. Disclosure is the fallback, not the cure: it must be prominent, plain and informative, and refreshed when the conflict changes materially. The conclusion fails if disclosure is boilerplate or goes to only one party.
- **Two audiences (pp.208–209).** The employer needs enough detail to judge impact and may ban the activity; the member must comply. Clients need disclosure of issuer ties (directorship, banking, market making, material beneficial ownership) and of firm benefits such as fee rebates. Telling the employer alone does not protect clients.
- **Beneficial ownership (p.209).** A direct or indirect financial interest, or power to vote or dispose. Money flows to the member or family, so objectivity is at risk. Owning covered stock is the most common conflict; avoid it or disclose it.
- **Board service (p.210).** Three conflicts: duty to clients versus duty to the company's shareholders; pay in company securities or options; access to material nonpublic information, or the appearance of it. Fix with firewalls between the director and investment decision makers.
- **Pay structures (p.208; Ex.5 p.212).** A bonus tied to short-term relative results rewards risk-taking that may not fit clients' long-term objectives. The incentive changes behaviour, so it must be disclosed and managed.
- **Priority of transactions (p.213).** Client and employer orders go first, then the firm's, then personal. Co-investing with clients is not automatically wrong. Personal profit is acceptable if clients are not disadvantaged, the member does not profit from client trades, and regulation is met. A personal sale against client advice can be justified (for example, a genuine personal cash need) when clients are not hurt. Never trade on, or pass on, knowledge of pending client trades. A family account that is a real client account gets normal client treatment, neither favoured nor penalised (p.213; Ex.3 p.216).
- **Personal-trading controls (pp.214–215).** Limit IPOs and private placements (they look like a taken client opportunity or a reward from the offerer), use blackout periods against front-running, and require holdings reports at hire and periodically, duplicate confirmations (they show actual trade flow, not only holdings) and preclearance. Disclose the policies to clients and prospects prominently, with fuller description on request.
- **Referral fees (pp.217–218).** A payment for a referral can bias the recommendation and raise the client's total cost. Disclose both benefits received and fees paid, before any formal service agreement, stating the nature (flat or percentage, one-off or continuing, cash, soft dollar or in kind) plus an estimated dollar value. Employers should ban or approve programmes, with participants updating the employer at least quarterly (p.218).

## Vocabulary
- Conflict of interest — a matter that could reasonably impair objectivity or raise a question of bias (p.208).
- Beneficial ownership — pecuniary interest, or power to vote or dispose; VI(B) also covers control and immediate family (p.209; p.214).
- Full and fair disclosure — prominent, plain-language, informative notice (pp.207–208).
- Mitigation — reducing a conflict and saying how (p.208).
- Firewall — barrier separating a director or banker from investment decisions (p.210).
- Market making — dealing from the firm's own account, so the firm has a price interest (p.210).
- Front-running — trading personally ahead of clients (p.215).
- Blackout / restricted period — time when investment staff may not trade (p.215).
- Preclearance — approval before a personal trade (p.215).
- Duplicate confirmations — broker copies of staff trades sent to the employer (p.215).
- Referral fee — any compensation or benefit paid or received for recommending a product or service (p.217; PDF 231).
- Soft dollars — one of the forms of consideration listed in the VI(C) disclosure content (p.218; PDF 232). The text does not define it.
- Priority of transactions — client and employer orders before personal ones (p.213).

## Traps
- "Disclosure cures everything": avoid first; disclose and mitigate only if avoidance is not reasonable (p.208).
- Telling only the employer: clients must be told too (Ex.6 p.212).
- Putting family accounts last as the "safe" choice: that breaches duty to them (Ex.3 p.216).
- "Owning what clients own is a violation": co-investing is not inherently wrong (p.213).
- Thinking referral rules cover outside parties only: internal departmental payments count (Ex.2 p.219).
- Disclosing after the client signs: it must come before the formal agreement (p.217).
- Boilerplate policy statements: they must be informative (p.215).
- Over-reading "conflict": only matters that could reasonably impair objectivity count (p.208); a spouse's holding in a covered stock does (Ex.3 p.211).
- A sound outcome does not excuse an undisclosed double payment (Ex.4 pp.219–220).

## Original practice seeds
1. Hypothetical: analyst Ana covers Zed Corp. Her spouse holds 2,000 Zed shares at US$50, worth 2,000 x 50 = US$100,000. She reports it to her employer and discloses it in the report; best practice is reassignment. If the spouse sold everything before the report, this ownership disclosure is no longer triggered, though the firm's trading policy still applies.
2. Hypothetical: PM Ben wants to buy 1,000 shares at US$20 personally (1,000 x 20 = US$20,000) while ordering 50,000 for clients. The client order goes first; he observes any blackout and preclears. Selling his own shares for tuition while clients buy is acceptable if clients are not harmed.
3. Hypothetical: an adviser pays a referrer 0.25% of assets a year on a US$4,000,000 client: 4,000,000 x 0.0025 = US$10,000 a year. Disclose before signing: percentage basis, continuing, about US$10,000. If assets reach US$6,000,000, the estimate becomes 6,000,000 x 0.0025 = US$15,000 a year, so update the disclosure.
