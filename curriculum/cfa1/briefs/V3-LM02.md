2. Describe the stakeholder groups of a company and compare their interests (Corporate Stakeholders and Governance, pp.48–54).
3. Describe the environmental, social and governance factors investors weigh when assessing issuers (Corporate ESG Considerations, pp.55–61).

## Core principles
- **Claims ordering.** Debt promises fixed interest and principal on fixed dates and ranks ahead of equity, including in liquidation. Equity has no end date, no promised payout, and receives what remains. Cash goes first to suppliers, employees, lenders and tax authorities; dividends come last and are at board discretion. If the firm is insolvent, equity gets nothing. (pp.39–41, PDF 47–49)
- **Debt is riskier for the issuer; equity is riskier for the investor.** The issuer must pay debt regardless of results and risks default and covenant breach. Equity creates no obligation but dilutes existing owners. The investor holding debt has priority and a capped return. (pp.37–38, 43)
- **Leverage magnifies ROE both ways.** A fixed interest bill is spread over a smaller equity base. If return on assets exceeds the borrowing rate, the surplus accrues to shareholders and ROE rises; if profit falls, interest is still due and ROE can turn negative. Higher ROE is not free: outcomes spread wider and default risk rises. Taxes ignored. (pp.42–43, PDF 50–51)
- **Funding a new investment.** Issuing shares dilutes and can leave ROE unchanged if the project only earns the existing ROE; borrowing helps if the project beats the interest rate; using idle cash avoids both dilution and interest and helps most. (p.44)
- **Payoff asymmetry.** Both investors can lose at most their stake. Equity holds open-ended upside; debt is capped at the promised amount, and in default receives roughly what the firm is worth. The two payoffs sum to firm value. Hence shareholders favour risk, leverage and bigger dividends; lenders favour safe projects and use covenants since they have no vote. (pp.45–47)
- **Shareholder versus stakeholder theory.** Shareholder theory: the board answers to shareholders and others matter via their effect on shareholder value. Stakeholder theory: balance all interests, with ESG an explicit goal. Challenges: balancing goals, measuring non-shareholder goals, competing with unconstrained rivals, cost of higher standards. The two need not conflict over the long run. (pp.49–50)
- **Each stakeholder's cash-flow angle.** Private lenders have inside access and renegotiate; bondholders rely on public information and have little influence short of restructuring. Suppliers are short-term creditors, so leverage and distress hurt them. Government ownership or voting rights can decide whose interests win. Managers (agents) may not act for shareholders (principals); independent boards and aligned pay mitigate this. (pp.50–54, 65)
- **ESG enters valuation through cash flows and discount rates.** Once costs formerly pushed onto others are internalised, they hit the firm. Equity reacts first and hardest; debt suffers when ability to pay is threatened (higher cost of debt, downgrades). Effects can differ by debt maturity. Analysts adjust forecasts, discount rates, and run scenarios, focusing on material factors. (pp.55–61)

## Vocabulary
- Debt — borrowing with a maturity date and contractual interest and principal; senior to equity (p.38, 40)
- Equity — permanent, residual ownership capital with no promised payout (p.38, 41)
- Priority claim — paid before lower-ranked claims, including in liquidation (pp.40–41)
- Residual claim — whatever remains after all other claims (p.41)
- Financial leverage — more debt relative to equity (p.43)
- Return on equity (ROE) — net income over equity for the period (p.42)
- Dilution — lower fractional ownership after new shares are issued (p.43)
- Limited liability — owners cannot lose more than their stake (p.43)
- Solvent / insolvent — firm value above or below debt owed (p.45)
- Covenant — lender-imposed restriction, e.g. coverage or leverage limits (p.47)
- Stakeholder — anyone with something at stake in how the company does (p.48)
- Shareholder vs stakeholder theory (p.49)
- Independent vs inside director (p.51)
- Staggered board — directors elected in rotating groups, slowing change of control (p.51)
- Negative externality — cost imposed on others rather than the firm (p.56)
- Materiality — significant effect on results or business model (p.57)
- Physical vs transition risk; stranded assets (p.57)
- Principal-agent relationship (PDF 73, solutions)

## Formulas
No numbered formulas; these working relationships are used (taxes ignored in the book's examples; p.42, p.44, PDF 50, 52):
- Net income = revenue − operating expenses − interest expense. Currency per period. Holds without taxes; with taxes, deductible interest saves tax.
- ROE = net income / total equity (percent per period). Uses book equity; after a share issue use the enlarged equity. Intuition: profit per unit of owner money.
- Interest = debt x interest rate. Charged on debt, not total assets (PDF 72).
- Return on assets = operating profit / assets. Leverage raises ROE only if this exceeds the interest rate.
- Shareholder payoff = max(V - D, 0); debtholder payoff = min(V, D), where V is firm value and D debt owed at maturity. Ignores bankruptcy costs. Intuition: lenders give up the upside for priority (p.45, PDF 53).

## Traps
- "Debt is riskier" is viewpoint-dependent: issuer versus investor.
- Same maximum loss does not mean same payoff: debt is capped, equity is not.
- Dividends are not owed; interest is.
- Leverage helps ROE only when return on assets beats the interest rate; check the sign before claiming a gain.
- Interest is charged on debt, not on assets; ROE divides by equity, not assets.
- A share issue leaves ROE unchanged only when the project earns exactly the existing ROE.
- Stranded assets are transition risk, not physical risk.
- Stakeholder theory is not automatically opposed to shareholder value; suppliers count as creditors.
- Equity falls first on bad ESG news; debt moves mainly through credit risk and maturity.

## Original practice seeds
1. (Hypothetical, one year, no taxes.) Assets 300, operating profit 45. All equity: ROE 45/300 = 15%. Debt 150 at 10%, equity 150: interest 15, NI 30, ROE 20%. If profit falls to 12: all-equity 4%; levered NI = 12 - 15 = -3, ROE -2%. If the rate is 16% (interest 24) with profit 45: NI 21, ROE 14%, below 15%.
2. (Hypothetical, at maturity.) Debt owed 100. Firm value 250: debtholders 100, shareholders 150. Value 150: 100 and 50. Value 80: 80 and 0. Sum always equals firm value.
