## Core principles
- **Trust is the asset an investment firm sells.** Clients hand over savings only if they believe the manager is reliable. One person's misconduct can cost the whole firm revenue, and it adds expense too (investigations). Firm, staff and clients pay, not only the wrongdoer. Clients cannot easily verify everything (knowledge gap). pp.3–4, 11 (PDF 17–18, 25).
- **Ethics is judged by society's expectations, weighing self-interest against effects on others.** A short-term gain to a few can become long-term harm to all. Widely shared principles: honesty, transparency, fairness, diligence, respect for others' rights. pp.4–5 (PDF 18–19).
- **Code of ethics states values; standards of conduct set the minimum behaviors.** Members may exceed standards. Joining means agreeing to both, with periodic written recommitment; breaches can bring discipline or expulsion. p.6 (PDF 20).
- **A profession earns trust through a package of features.** Practitioners meet entry standards, hold expert knowledge, keep learning, put clients and society above themselves, police their own conduct, and are overseen by a recognized (often not-for-profit) body. Ethics and a service mission separate a profession from a guild or trade body. pp.7–9 (PDF 21–23).
- **Situations beat character more often than people think.** Overconfidence leads people to credit their own virtue and underrate pressures (money, prestige, above all loyalty to bosses and colleagues), and the immediate pull crowds out long-term integrity. A pure compliance culture can itself become such a pressure because it asks what is allowed, not what is right. pp.13–14 (PDF 27–28).
- **Law and ethics overlap only partly.** Laws lag market practice, can be vague, narrow, conflicting or jurisdiction-specific, and get gamed. So an act can be legal but unethical (hypothetically, trading on inside information where no ban exists) or ethical but illegal (whistle-blowing that breaches policy or local law). Ethics needs judgment among stakeholders, including reputational risk. pp.15–17 (PDF 29–31).
- **A framework makes ethical analysis routine, like a profit and loss check.** Identify (facts, stakeholders and duties, principles, conflicts), Consider (situational influences, guidance, alternatives), Decide and act, Reflect. Iterative; no guarantee; may leave no fully acceptable option. Seek advice from someone free of the same pressures. pp.18–20, 24 (PDF 32–34, 38).
- **Client first, but market integrity overrides.** In a conflict, clients outrank employer and colleagues; duty to market integrity outranks the client. pp.11, 26 (PDF 25, 40).

## Vocabulary
- Stakeholder — anyone directly or indirectly affected by a decision (p.4).
- Trust — confidence that someone can be relied on (p.4).
- Ethical conduct — acting on moral principles while weighing own interest against effects on others (p.5).
- Suitability standard vs best-interest (fiduciary) standard — recommend something that fits vs the best option for the client (pp.5–6).
- Code of ethics — written statement of a group's values (p.6).
- Standards of conduct — specific minimum behaviors; a floor, not a ceiling (p.6).
- Profession — occupational community with training, expert knowledge and a behavior framework that supports trust (p.7).
- Fiduciary duty — high standard of care when acting for another's benefit (p.8).
- Practice analysis — process that keeps the body of knowledge current (p.11).
- Overconfidence bias — overrating one's own ethics; a behavioral bias (p.13).
- Situational influences — external factors that shape behavior (p.13).
- Whistle-blowing — exposing an organization's wrongdoing; may be illegal yet ethical (p.16).
- Wall crossing / quiet period — rules limiting confidential deal information and written material around an offering (p.23).
- Market integrity — duty that overrides client interest in a conflict (pp.11, 26).

## Traps
- "Ethical equals legal." Know one example each of legal-but-unethical and ethical-but-illegal.
- "Good character is enough." Situational influences usually dominate; overconfidence hides this.
- Money, prestige and loyalty are external; overconfidence is internal.
- A strong compliance program does not solve ethics; it can oversimplify and make people ask what is permitted.
- Standards of conduct are minimum benchmarks, not ideals, and not the same as the code of ethics or laws.
- Employers, regulators and trade bodies are not professional bodies; fiduciary duty belongs to professions.
- The framework is not a fixed sequence, not limited to confirmable facts, and gives no guarantee; it reduces unanticipated consequences.
- Loyalty to the employer does not win: client first, market integrity above that.

## Original practice seeds
1. (Hypothetical) Fund A charges 1.20% a year and pays the adviser 0.40% of assets; Fund B charges 0.60% and pays nothing; both suit the client. On 100,000 for one year, fees are 1,200 vs 600, so A costs the client 600 more, and the adviser gains 400. A suitability-only rule permits A; a best-interest standard points to B. If A's fee falls to 0.60%, the extra cost is 0 but the 400 conflict remains, so disclosure still matters.
2. (Hypothetical) A salesperson on 4% commission sells 200,000 of fund shares: pay is 8,000. Cut to 1%: pay is 2,000, down 6,000 (75%). Money pressure falls, yet loyalty to teammates can still push the same behavior.
3. (Hypothetical) Country X does not ban trading on inside information; a trader could earn 50,000 legally. It is legal-only in the Venn diagram. If X adds a ban, the act becomes illegal and unethical, so it leaves the Legal circle and sits outside both; the overlap is unchanged and the Legal-only region shrinks.
