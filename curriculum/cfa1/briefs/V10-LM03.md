## Learning outcomes
All three are stated on p.53 (PDF 67) and taught across the whole module.
1. Apply the Code and Standards to situations involving professional integrity (Standards I(A)–I(E) and their examples, pp.54–89).
2. Recommend practices and procedures that prevent violations (each Compliance Practices block: pp.58, 65, 74, 83, 87).
3. Distinguish conforming from violating conduct (application examples and practice problems, pp.59–91).

## Core principles
- **Law is the floor (I(A), pp.54–57).** Where law and the Code differ, follow the stricter one; stricter means more restriction or more action to protect clients. Find which country's law applies, then compare: absent or weaker law means the Code governs; tougher law governs itself.
- **Dissociation (I(A), pp.55–56, 59).** If you know or should have known others are breaking the rules, try to stop it (the person, then supervisor or compliance), then step away: remove your name, change assignment, decline the client, possibly leave. Staying silently attached can look like participation. Reporting to authorities is encouraged but not required unless law demands it.
- **Independence is bought or pressured away (I(B), pp.61–64).** A gift, access, business or pay changes the incentive behind an opinion, and the client pays through a biased view. Modest gifts are tolerable; lavish ones from brokers, issuers or managers are not. A client gift is closer to extra pay, so disclose it to the employer.
- **Remove the conflict rather than disclose it (I(B), p.65; solutions pp.92–94).** Restricted list for issuers tied to banking; pay your own commercial travel; flat fees for issuer-paid research, never pay tied to the conclusion. Avoidance beats disclosure, which beats accepting. In manager selection neither side offers or asks for gifts or contributions (pay-to-play, p.64).
- **Misrepresentation is about impression (I(C), pp.70–73).** An untrue statement, an omission or a misleading framing, in any medium. "Knowingly" includes should have known. No guaranteeing returns unless the product itself carries the guarantee. Cherry-picking accounts or averaging away context misleads even if each number is true.
- **Honest error versus ignored error (I(C), p.75).** An unknowing mistake is not a violation, but you must stop distributing it and correct it; leaving a known error uncorrected is a violation.
- **Scope of misconduct (I(D), p.82).** Dishonesty, fraud, deceit or acts harming reputation, integrity or competence count only when tied to professional conduct (workplace, markets, academia, or use of the charter, membership or candidacy). Legal conduct can still violate; unrelated personal wrongs fall outside.
- **Competence is diligence, not outcomes (I(E), p.86).** Keep knowledge, skills and abilities suited to your current role and extend them when the role changes. A loss does not prove incompetence; a credential alone does not prove competence.

## Vocabulary
- Applicable law — the law governing the conduct on the facts; identify it, then compare with the Code (p.55).
- More strict — imposes greater limits or more client-protective action (p.55).
- Dissociate — separate from a violation by escalating, then stepping away (pp.55–56).
- Whistle-blowing — voluntary report to authorities; not required unless law says so (p.56).
- Independence and objectivity — advice not bent by outside benefits or inside pressure (p.61).
- Restricted list — issuers on which a firm publishes no opinion (p.65).
- Pay-to-play — managers giving value to officials who pick managers (p.64).
- Issuer-paid research — research funded by the company covered; disclose and use a flat fee (p.64).
- Misrepresentation — false statement, omission or misleading impression (p.71).
- Knowingly — knew or should have known (p.71).
- Plain language — wording an ordinary reader can follow (p.70).
- Plagiarism — using others' work, ideas, charts or models without credit (p.73).
- Cherry-picking — showing only the best accounts (p.72).
- Composite — grouped accounts of one strategy used for performance reporting (pp.59, 70).
- Professional conduct — activity tied to analysis, investment management or the charter (p.82).
- Competence — knowledge plus skills plus abilities for the role (p.86).

## Formulas
None in this module. The one structured item is the law-versus-Code matrix (printed pp.57–58, PDF 71–72). Logic: step 1, determine which country's law applies; step 2, follow the stricter of that law and the Code.

## Traps
- "Legal means ethical": the Code can be stricter than the law (p.55).
- Legal counsel's comfort does not remove your duty to act (I(A) Ex 1, p.59).
- Stricter is not always the Code; sometimes local law wins (I(A) Ex 5, p.60).
- Dissociation is required; reporting to authorities usually is not (p.56).
- Client gifts are allowed if disclosed; lavish broker or issuer gifts are not. Paid travel is a judgment call, though paying your own way is best practice (pp.62, 64, 65).
- A true statement can still mislead through omission or averaging (p.72).
- A "guarantee" on an insured product can be accurate if explained; "guaranteed" returns on a risky product are not (p.71).
- Citing a media outlet is not enough; cite and obtain the original source (I(C) Ex 11, p.78). Moving an analyst or softening wording does not cure pressure.
- Out-of-scope conduct (civil disobedience, personal misdeeds unrelated to work) is not an I(D) violation, but invoking the charter pulls conduct in (I(D) Ex 4, 6, 8, pp.84–85).

## Original practice seeds
1. (Hypothetical) An adviser lives in Country N with no securities law and serves a client in Country M, whose law governs and requires written referral-fee disclosure before signing. The Code also requires such disclosure but sets no timing. Answer: M's law is stricter, so follow M's law. Had N's law applied, the Code would govern and disclosure is still required.
2. (Hypothetical) A client sends a US$150 restaurant voucher; a broker who earns the manager's commissions offers a US$15,000 trip. Answer: accept the voucher after telling the employer (before if possible); refuse the trip.
3. (Hypothetical) Three accounts return 6%, 7% and 71% in one year. Simple average is (6+7+71)/3 = 28%. Without the 71% account, held against the adviser's advice, the average is (6+7)/2 = 6.5%. Advertising "28% average" without that context misleads.
