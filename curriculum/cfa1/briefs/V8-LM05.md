## Learning outcomes

1. Explain what raw land, timberland and farmland are and what characterises them as investments (printed pp.121–128, PDF 129–136).
2. Describe commodities and their investment characteristics: forms of access, pricing, cost of carry, convenience yield (printed pp.129–134, PDF 137–142).
3. Analyze where risk, return and diversification come from across commodities, farmland and timberland, including inflation hedging (printed pp.135–141, PDF 143–149).

## Core principles

- **Land earns from output and price change, not from buildings.** Raw land pays only through price appreciation (plus any lease or royalty). Farmland and timberland add a cash stream from crops or timber sales. Unlike real estate, value comes from soil, climate and geology, transport access matters, and specialist skill is needed (hence TIMOs). Fails if output prices drop or weather ruins a harvest (pp.124–126).
- **Timber is a factory and a warehouse; crops are not.** Ripe crops must be harvested on schedule, while standing trees keep growing and can be left uncut when lumber prices are weak. That gives the timber owner a timing option the farmer lacks. Return = output price times quantity, plus change in land price (p.126).
- **Owner versus operator changes the risk.** Leasing out row-crop land gives the owner fixed rent and the tenant carries crop risk. Operating permanent-crop land (orchards, vineyards) gives variable cash flows plus operating risk. A farm is naturally long its crop and can sell futures to hedge (p.126).
- **Commodities pay no cash flow and cost money to hold.** Return is price appreciation net of storage, transport and insurance. Most investors therefore use exchange-traded derivatives, ETPs, CTAs or specialised funds rather than the physical good. (pp.130–131).
- **Forward price = spot, plus what the physical owner pays, minus what the owner gains.** No-arbitrage says an owner of the physical good must be compensated through a higher forward for financing and storage costs. Convenience yield is a non-cash benefit of holding physical goods and is larger when inventories are low; it lowers the forward. Contango (forward above spot) when costs exceed the benefit, backwardation (spot above forward) when the benefit exceeds costs. A long-only holder tends to lose from rolling in contango and gain in backwardation (pp.132–134).
- **Land looks calmer than it is.** Land prices are infrequent and often appraisal-based, so measured volatility is flattered. Commodities price continuously on exchanges. Supply responds slowly (growing cycles, years to build mines or wells) so booms produce shortages and slowdowns gluts. (pp.122, 136–137).
- **Commodities hedge inflation; land diversifies.** Energy and food prices feed into consumer prices, so commodity returns rise when inflation rises and fall when it falls. Farmland and timberland stay positive in every regime, so their correlation with inflation is low because they do not fall, not because they hurt. Commodities have the higher correlation with stocks of the three and so diversify equities least; land has near-zero correlation with stocks and bonds. (pp.138–141).

## Vocabulary

- Natural resources — commodities plus land used for farming and timber (p.121).
- Hard vs soft commodities — mined or extracted versus grown (p.121).
- Raw land — undeveloped land, return mainly from price change (pp.122, 124).
- Timberland as factory and warehouse — growing trees that can be stored uncut (p.126).
- TIMO — timberland investment management organization that acquires and manages forest for institutions (p.125).
- Owner vs owner-operator — lease out for fixed rent versus keep operating control (p.126).
- Cost of carry — interest plus physical holding costs (storage, transport, insurance) (pp.130, 132).
- Convenience yield — non-cash benefit of holding the physical good, rising as inventories fall (p.132).
- Backwardation — spot above forwards, downward-sloping curve (p.133).
- Contango — forwards above spot, upward-sloping curve (p.133).
- ETP (ETF/ETN) — exchange-traded commodity exposure through a brokerage account (p.131).
- CTA — managed-futures adviser taking directional futures positions (p.131).
- Private energy partnership — private-equity-like energy fund, long-lived, fee charged on committed capital (p.132).

## Formulas

**Forward price on a physical commodity (Equation 1, printed p.132, PDF 140):**
F_0(T) = S_0 · e^((r + c − i)·T)

- F_0(T): forward price today for delivery at T. S_0: spot price (currency per unit). r: continuously compounded risk-free rate. c: annual storage-type carry cost (transport, storage, insurance), read as the non-interest cost. i: annual convenience yield. T: years to expiry.
- Assumes continuous compounding, a physically deliverable commodity, no arbitrage frictions.
- Intuition: forward above spot if r + c > i (contango), below if r + c < i (backwardation).
- Verbal form on PDF 143: forward = spot + carry costs − ownership benefits.

## Traps

- Assuming the forward always sits above spot. With low inventories the convenience yield can push it below (pp.122, 133).
- Adding convenience yield to the forward. It benefits the physical owner and lowers the forward (p.132).
- Mixing up the vocabulary: backwardation is spot above forward, contango is forward above spot; low inventories point to backwardation, full storage to contango (pp.132–134).
- Reading low measured land volatility as low risk. It reflects infrequent, appraisal-based pricing (pp.122, 136–137).
- Treating raw land like farmland: no crop income, more risk (p.137).
- Calling land a poor inflation hedge because its inflation correlation is low. Commodities are the stronger hedge, but land holds up in low inflation (p.140).

## Original practice seeds

1. (Hypothetical) Spot 100 per barrel, r = 3%, c = 2%, i = 1%, T = 0.5 year. Exponent = (0.03 + 0.02 − 0.01) × 0.5 = 0.02. F = 100 × e^0.02 = 102.02, above spot: contango.
2. (Hypothetical) Same inputs but inventories fall and i = 6%. Exponent = (0.03 + 0.02 − 0.06) × 0.5 = −0.005. F = 100 × e^−0.005 = 99.50, below spot: backwardation. A long who buys the six-month future at 102.02 in example 1 and sees spot unchanged at 100 at expiry loses 2.02; at 99.50 the same long gains 0.50.
3. (Hypothetical) A timber owner has 1,000 acres, 20 tons per acre harvestable, so 20,000 tons. Lumber is 50 per ton now and 40 in a slump. Harvest now: 1,000,000; harvest in the slump: 800,000. Waiting one year with 5% growth gives 21,000 tons; if the price recovers to 50, revenue is 1,050,000. A farmer with ripe crops has no such option. Ignores discounting and holding costs.
