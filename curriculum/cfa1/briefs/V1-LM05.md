## Core principles
- **Mean versus median.** The mean uses every value, so one extreme observation drags it; the median uses only rank, so it resists outliers but ignores magnitudes. Mean above median hints at a few large positive values, below at large negative ones. pp.192–194.
- **Outliers need a decision.** Check for errors first; then keep, trim (drop a fixed share from both ends) or winsorize (replace extremes with cut-off values, keeping the count). Use returns, not prices, which are non-stationary. pp.196–198.
- **Dispersion is the yardstick of risk.** Raw deviations cancel to zero, so square them (variance) or take absolute values (MAD). SD is in return units. A sample divides by n-1; this makes variance unbiased, but SD stays slightly low. pp.203–208.
- **Time scaling.** With independent periods, variance grows with horizon and SD with its square root. Real returns often break this. p.212.
- **Shape.** Negative skew: longer left tail, frequent small gains and rare large losses. Positive excess kurtosis: heavy tails both ways, so normal-based models understate tail losses. pp.217–220.
- **Co-movement.** Covariance shows direction only, in product units; correlation divides by both SDs, giving a unit-free number from -1 to +1 for linear association only. It can arise by chance or from a third variable, and outliers distort it. pp.225–230.
- **Downside and relative risk.** Semi-deviation counts only shortfalls below a target (a higher target raises it). CV is SD per unit of mean return; it breaks down when the mean is near zero or negative. pp.235–237.

## Vocabulary
- Population / sample — all possible observations vs the subset held (p.192).
- Robust — reliable despite outliers; the median is, the mean is not (p.193).
- Trimmed / winsorized mean — drop, or replace with cut-off values, a stated share of extremes, then average (p.196).
- Non-stationary — properties drift over time; prices are, returns closer to stationary (p.198).
- Quantile (quartile, quintile, decile, percentile) — cut-points for equal-count groups; IQR = Q3 − Q1 (pp.200–201).
- Box-and-whisker plot — Q1, median, Q3, whiskers, flagged outliers (p.202).
- Mean / median absolute deviation — average, or median, of absolute gaps from the center (p.204).
- Variance, SD, volatility (pp.207–208); Bessel's correction, degrees of freedom (p.208).
- Root-of-time rule — SD scales with the square root of the horizon (p.212).
- Skewness (p.217); kurtosis, excess kurtosis; leptokurtic, mesokurtic, platykurtic (pp.219–220).
- Covariance (p.225), correlation coefficient (p.227), spurious correlation (p.230).
- Target semi-deviation (p.235); coefficient of variation (p.237).

## Formulas
1. Mean = Σx_i / n (p.192). Not robust.
2. Median: odd n, the middle value; even n, the average of the two middle values (p.193). Sorted data. The book misprints the even-n indices; use the verbal rule.
3. Range = max − min (p.203).
4. Mean absolute deviation = Σ|X_i − X̄| / n (p.204).
5. Median absolute deviation = median(|X_i − median(X)|) (p.204). Robust.
6. Population variance σ² = Σ(X_i − μ)² / n; σ its root (p.208). Squared units.
7. Sample variance s² = Σ(X_i − X̄)² / (n−1); s its root (p.208). Conversion: s² = σ² × n/(n−1).
8. Time scaling: variance_T = T × variance_1; SD_T = √T × SD_1 (p.212). Assumes independence.
9. Skewness = (1/n) Σ((X_i − X̄)/s)³ (p.218). Unit-free; 0 is symmetric. The later exhibit and pre-test write it loosely.
10. Excess kurtosis = (1/n) Σ((X_i − X̄)/s)⁴ − 3 (p.220). Stated for large samples (100 or more). Normal: kurtosis 3, excess 0.
11. Covariance σ_XY = Σ(X_i − μ_X)(Y_i − μ_Y) / n (p.225). Sample (n−1) form appears only as a spreadsheet function.
12. Correlation ρ = σ_XY / (σ_X σ_Y) (p.227).
13. Semi-variance = Σ over X_i < X̄ of (X_i − X̄)² / n (p.235).
14. Target semi-deviation s_T = √( Σ over X_i ≤ B of (X_i − B)² / (n−1) ), n = all observations, B = target (p.236).
15. CV = s / X̄ (p.237). Unit-free.
16. Geometric mean = (Π(1+r_t))^(1/n) − 1 (p.210). Variance is still measured around the arithmetic mean.

## Traps
- Variance is in squared units; never compare it directly with a mean or SD (p.208).
- Population divides by n, sample by n−1; SD stays slightly biased low (p.208).
- Semi-deviation: n−1 uses the total count, not the number below target (p.235).
- Positive skew means mean > median > mode, negative the reverse. Negative skew does not mean mostly negative returns (pp.217–218).
- Check whether software reports kurtosis or excess kurtosis; positive excess means both tails are heavy (p.220).
- Zero correlation does not mean independence; correlation is not causation. Positive covariance means positive correlation even when average returns are negative (pp.191, 230).
- Scale SD by √T, never by T (p.212).
- CV: lower is less risk per unit of return, but it fails for a mean near zero or negative; annualize first (p.237).
- Quantile labels: spreadsheets number low to high, while "first-quartile manager" usually means best (p.203).

## Original practice seeds
(All hypothetical.)
1. Five monthly returns: 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, 40%. Mean = 10%, median = 3%. If the 40% were a typo for 4%, the mean becomes 2.8% and the median stays 3%.
2. Returns 3, 5, 7, 9 (%). Mean 6; squared deviations sum to 20; sample variance 6.67, s = 2.58%; population variance 5, SD 2.24%. Replace 9 with 15: mean 7.5, sum 83, variance 27.67, s = 5.26%.
3. Covariance 0.0012, SDs 0.04 and 0.05: ρ = 0.60. Second SD rises to 0.06: ρ = 0.50. CV for mean 0.8%, SD 4%: 5.0; mean 1.0%: 4.0.
4. Returns 5, 1, −3, 4 (%), target 2%: shortfalls −1 and −5, squares sum 26; 26/3 = 8.667; root 2.94%. Target 4%: squares 9, 49, 0, sum 58; 58/3 = 19.33; root 4.40%. Scaling: daily SD 1% over 22 days gives 1% × √22 ≈ 4.69%.
