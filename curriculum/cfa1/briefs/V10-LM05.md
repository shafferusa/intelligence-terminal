## Learning outcomes
The three outcomes are generic to every Standards module.
1. Apply the Code and Standards to situations involving professional integrity: every Application section and the Examples (printed pp.121–149), practised in the 12 problems (pp.150–153).
2. Recommend practices and procedures that prevent violations: the Compliance Practices sections, III(A) pp.120–121, III(B) pp.127–129, III(C) p.136, III(D) pp.140–141, III(E) pp.145–146.
3. Distinguish conforming from violating conduct: the Guidance sections and the 43 examples with their comments.

## Core principles
- Client first. The client hands over money or advice to someone who knows more, which creates vulnerability. The client's interest outranks the employer's and your own, and you act as carefully as you would for your own affairs (pp.116–117). Follow the stricter of law and Code.
- Identify the client. For a pension plan or trust the client is the beneficiaries, not the sponsor or whoever hired you (p.118; Ex.1 p.121). For an index or mandate fund you follow the mandate. Only advisers to individuals owe them loyalty (Ex.9 p.124). An execution-only broker is not a fiduciary but owes best execution within instructions (Ex.11 p.125).
- Commissions belong to the client. The client pays through higher commissions. Soft dollars are acceptable only if the goods or services benefit the client and are reasonable in value; they fail if they cover the manager's overhead, education or trips (p.119; Ex.2, Ex.5). Directed brokerage is allowed with disclosure that best execution may be lost. Churning to hit a commission target is a violation (Ex.6 p.123).
- Fair is not equal. Premium service tiers are fine if disclosed, open to all and not harmful to others; a broad release first, personal calls later is acceptable (p.125; Ex.6 p.131). Allocation is prorated across suitable accounts, decided before execution, in round lots; the manager's own shares are excluded if oversubscribed. A normal fee-paying family account is treated like any other (Ex.7 pp.123–124). Disclosing a patently unfair procedure does not cure it (p.127).
- Suitability uses the total portfolio and the information the client actually gave (pp.133–135). An IPS records objectives (risk, return) and constraints (liquidity, time horizon, tax, legal, preferences such as ESG), reviewed at least annually and on big life changes (p.134; p.136). For an unsolicited unsuitable request: educate, document, update the IPS if the change is material, and consider ending the relationship if the client insists without a change (p.135).
- Performance must be fair, accurate and complete (p.140). Claiming GIPS compliance falsely is a violation. Without GIPS: use composites, include terminated accounts, label simulated, prior-firm and gross/net figures, keep records (pp.140–141).
- Confidentiality covers present, past and prospective clients, with three exceptions: the client's illegal activity, a legal duty to disclose, the client's permission (pp.143–144). Law overrides the Code; ask compliance when unsure. For vulnerable investors, name a secondary contact at onboarding; unusual choices alone do not show diminished capacity (Ex.7 p.149).

## Vocabulary
- Loyalty, prudence, care — acting solely for the client, as a careful professional (p.116).
- Fiduciary duty — legally imposed enhanced trust; the Code requires obeying it (p.117).
- Custody — ability, direct or indirect, to reach client assets (p.117).
- Prospective client — someone who has shown interest or been solicited (p.118).
- Soft dollars — client brokerage used to buy goods or services (p.119).
- Best execution — trading to leave the portfolio as valuable as possible within the client's limits (p.119).
- Directed brokerage — client dictates the broker (p.119).
- Fair dealing — non-discriminatory, not identical, treatment (p.125).
- Investment recommendation — any buy, sell or hold view in any medium (p.126).
- Selective disclosure — some clients get a view earlier or fuller (pp.129–132).
- Pro rata allocation, minimum lot — fills scaled to orders but not below a tradable minimum (pp.128, 131).
- Suitability — fit with the client's circumstances in the total portfolio (p.133).
- IPS — written objectives, constraints, review schedule (pp.133–136).
- Unsolicited trade — client-initiated request that may conflict with the IPS (p.135).
- Composite — group of similar portfolios shown together (p.140).
- GIPS — global performance presentation standards (p.140).
- Secondary contact — person pre-authorised for contact if capacity is in doubt (p.144).

## Formulas
None in this module. The only arithmetic is prorating an allocation with a minimum lot (III(B) Ex.7, p.131): fill fraction = shares available / total requested; scale each order by it, then lift any fill below the minimum lot to the minimum and re-share the rest in proportion.

## Traps
- "Everyone is a fiduciary": III(A) is a minimum standard; follow any legal fiduciary duty (p.117; Ex.11 p.125).
- Naming the sponsor or hirer as the client in pension or trust cases (p.118).
- Treating soft dollars as always banned, or always fine. Test: does it benefit the client, at a reasonable price? Overhead and vacations fail (Ex.2, Ex.5).
- "Fair means equal", and "disclosure fixes it": tiers are fine if disclosed and open to all, but a patently unfair allocation stays unfair (pp.125, 127).
- Excluding a family account to avoid appearance of conflict: a normal fee-paying account gets its share (Ex.7).
- Judging suitability security by security, or ignoring the client's broader wealth (p.133; Ex.2 p.137).
- Ignoring a client's own order after a changed recommendation has been disclosed to them (p.126).
- Assuming prior-firm track records are barred (allowed with source and role disclosed, Ex.3 pp.141–142) or that confidentiality is absolute (pp.143–145).

## Original practice seeds
1. Hypothetical: a bond issue gives a manager US$60,000 of an oversubscribed placement. Four suitable accounts ask for 20,000, 20,000, 40,000 and 80,000 (total 160,000). Fill fraction is 60,000/160,000 = 37.5%, so pure pro rata gives 7,500, 7,500, 15,000, 30,000. With a documented 10,000 minimum lot, the two small accounts get 10,000 each, and the remaining 40,000 splits 40:80, giving 13,333 and 26,667. Departing from strict pro rata needs a written policy.
2. Hypothetical: a manager wants US$1,000 commissions per account per quarter for free research, but the strategy needs only US$400 of trading. Extra cost is 600 per quarter, 2,400 a year, or 120,000 a year across 50 accounts. That is churning (III(A)), paid by clients.
3. Hypothetical: a client's IPS caps equity at 40% of a US$300,000 managed account (120,000). She inherits US$900,000 held outside it, so total wealth is 1,200,000 and the account is 25% of it. The adviser must review the IPS; allowing 60% equity (180,000, or 15% of total wealth) could now be suitable because risk capacity rose.
