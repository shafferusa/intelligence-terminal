## Core principles
- **Cash lags activity.** Receivables and payables are recorded when a sale or delivery happens and cleared only when cash moves; inventory is recorded on taking ownership and removed when sold. The days each item sits on the balance sheet are DOH, DSO and DPO, and the CCC is the gap between paying suppliers and collecting from customers (pp.101–102). A longer gap needs more financing; a negative gap means suppliers fund the firm.
- **Levers on the CCC.** Cut DOH (forecasting, dropping slow lines), cut DSO (early-payment discounts, tighter credit, deposits), raise DPO (longer terms, depends on bargaining power) (pp.101–103). Tighter credit can lose sales.
- **Stretching payables is borrowing.** Skipping a supplier's early-payment discount means paying the supplier late at an implicit annual interest rate, compounded over the extra days. If a bank loan costs less than that implied rate, borrow and take the discount (pp.104–105). It fails if the bank will not lend.
- **Liquidity is about short-run payment ability, not profit.** Primary sources (cash and securities, borrowing, operating cash flow) are used in the normal course; secondary sources (cutting dividends, delaying capex, issuing equity, renegotiating, selling assets, bankruptcy protection) are costly and signal weakness (pp.113–115). Profitable firms can still run short (p.126).
- **Drags slow inflows; pulls speed outflows or remove credit.** Uncollected receivables, obsolete inventory and borrowing limits are drags; early payments, suppliers cutting credit and banks capping lines are pulls (p.116).
- **Three ratios, one denominator.** Current, quick and cash ratios all divide by current liabilities, so cash ≤ quick ≤ current. Inventory builds can lift the current ratio while the stricter ratios fall, so the strictest ratio is the most conservative reading (pp.117–122). All ignore asset quality.
- **Objective: maximize firm value while meeting obligations.** Management shortens the CCC and keeps surplus liquidity small (pp.123–124).
- **Funding style trades cost against rollover risk.** Permanent current assets are the base needs; variable ones are seasonal or growth peaks. Conservative funds even variable needs with long-term debt and equity: most flexible, costliest. Aggressive funds variable and some permanent needs short-term: cheapest, but exposed to refinancing failure and swinging interest cost. Moderate (matched) funds permanent needs long-term and variable needs short-term (pp.124–126).

## Vocabulary
- Operating cycle — sequence of buying inputs, producing, selling, collecting (p.101).
- Days of inventory on hand (DOH) — average days inventory sits before sale (p.102).
- Days sales outstanding (DSO) — average days to collect receivables (p.102).
- Days payable outstanding (DPO) — average days to pay suppliers (p.102).
- Cash conversion cycle (CCC) — DOH + DSO − DPO, in days (p.102).
- Trade credit — financing from delaying payment to a supplier; cost is the forgone discount (pp.104–105).
- Terms "2/10, net 30" style — discount if paid by the first date, otherwise full amount due on the later date (p.104).
- Total working capital — current assets minus current liabilities (p.107).
- Net working capital — same, excluding cash, marketable securities and short-term debt (p.107).
- Liquidity — nearness to cash for an item; ability to meet short-term obligations for an issuer (p.112).
- Primary / secondary liquidity sources — normal-course versus costly, signalling fallbacks (pp.113–115).
- Liquidation cost — fees plus the price discount from a quick sale (p.115).
- Drag / pull on liquidity — slowed inflows / accelerated outflows or lost credit (p.116).
- Current, quick, cash ratios — three liquidity ratios, increasingly strict (p.118).
- Permanent vs. variable current assets — base needs vs. seasonal or growth needs (p.123).
- Conservative / moderate / aggressive approach — long-term funded, matched, short-term funded (pp.124–126).
- Rollover risk — risk that short-term debt cannot be refinanced when due (pp.124–125).

## Formulas
1. CCC = DOH + DSO − DPO. Days. Assumes consistently averaged ratios. (p.102, PDF 110)
2. Total working capital = current assets − current liabilities. Currency. (p.107, PDF 115)
3. Net working capital = (current assets − cash − marketable securities) − (current liabilities − short-term/current debt); often divided by annual sales. (p.107, PDF 115)
4. EAR of forgoing a discount = (1 + d/(1 − d))^(365/(N − D)) − 1. d = discount as a fraction, N = net due day, D = discount day. Annual effective rate; assumes payment on day N, 365-day year. (p.105, PDF 113)
5. Cash flow from operations = cash from customers + interest and dividends received − cash paid to employees and suppliers − taxes paid − interest paid. Currency. Interest/dividend classification depends on the accounting standard (general knowledge). (p.114, PDF 122)
6. Free cash flow = CFO − investment in long-term assets. An alternative version adds back interest paid. (p.114, PDF 122)
7. Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities. (p.118, PDF 126)
8. Quick ratio = (cash + short-term marketable instruments + receivables) / current liabilities. (p.118, PDF 126)
9. Cash ratio = (cash + short-term marketable instruments) / current liabilities. (p.118, PDF 126)
10. Net proceeds = fair value − liquidation cost, where cost = fair value × liquidation %. Not a numbered equation. (p.115, PDF 123)

## Traps
- Sign of DPO: DPO is subtracted. Higher DSO or DOH lengthens the CCC; higher DPO shortens it (p.102).
- A negative CCC is possible when DPO exceeds DOH + DSO (p.103).
- Cash and securities on hand do not enter the CCC (p.98); they are also excluded from net working capital (p.106).
- Longer DPO is not automatically good: forgoing a discount can imply a very high annual rate; the nominal discount percent is not the rate (pp.104–105).
- Higher current ratio does not mean more liquid on every measure; an inventory build can raise it while quick and cash ratios fall (pp.119–120).
- Drag versus pull: slow or failed inflows are drags; faster outflows or lost credit are pulls. Extending customers' payment time is a drag (p.116).
- Conservative is not simply "best": it is the costliest; aggressive is cheapest but carries rollover risk (pp.124–125).
- Supplier trade credit and borrowing are primary sources; equity issuance and asset sales are secondary (p.100).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
A. Issuer X: DOH 40, DSO 30, DPO 25 days, so CCC = 45 days. If DPO rises to 35, CCC = 35 days, ten fewer days of cash tied up.
B. Supplier terms 1/10, net 40: d = 1%, period = 30 days. EAR = (1.010101)^(365/30) − 1 ≈ 13.0%. A 9% bank loan: borrow and take the discount. A 15% loan: forgo it. If net moves to day 60 (50 days), EAR ≈ 7.6%, so the 9% loan is now dearer and the decision flips.
C. Cash 30, securities 20, receivables 50, inventory 80, prepaid 5; payables 60, accrued 20, short-term debt 20 (current liabilities 100). Cash ratio 0.50, quick 1.00, current 1.85, NWC = 135 − 80 = 55. After a 40 inventory write-off: current ratio 1.45, quick and cash unchanged, NWC 15.
