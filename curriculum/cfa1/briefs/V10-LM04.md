## Learning outcomes

1. Apply the Code and Standards to situations involving professional integrity (examples pp.101–105 and pp.107–109; problems pp.110–111).
2. Recommend practices and procedures that prevent violations (II(A) compliance pp.99–101; II(B) compliance pp.106–107).
3. Distinguish conforming from violating conduct (the 17 examples and the practice problems).

All three are stated on p.95 (PDF 109) and taught across the whole module.

## Core principles

- **Why the standard exists (p.95; pp.96, 105–106).** If insiders can trade ahead of the public, or prices and volumes can be faked, ordinary investors demand higher risk premiums or stay out. Capital is then allocated worse; the rule protects market trust, not one client.
- **II(A) needs two limbs (pp.96–97).** Information is a violation risk only if it is both material and nonpublic. Material means a reasonable investor would want it, or it would likely move the price. Weigh specificity, difference from what is public, nature and reliability. The ban covers any instrument, including derivatives, funds and ETFs. A weak or unreliable source can make information nonmaterial.
- **Public means the market generally (p.97).** A reasonable expectation that it has spread is enough, with no need to wait for the slowest channel. A briefing for a few holders or a room of analysts is still nonpublic. Information given for due diligence on a deal may be used only for that deal.
- **Mosaic theory (p.98).** Analysts may combine public data with nonmaterial nonpublic scraps and reach a conclusion that would be material if an insider had said it outright. This makes prices more informative. It stops at material nonpublic pieces. Keep research documented.
- **Experts and research reports (p.99).** Expert-network agreements do not move the duty; the member must not seek or use confidential information and must judge materiality. If MNPI arrives, no trading until it is public. A respected analyst's rating change can be material, but it comes from public data and skill, the client paid for it, and it need not be made public.
- **When you hold MNPI (pp.99–101).** Push for public release, usually through the issuer. If that fails, tell only supervisors or compliance and do not trade or change recommendations. A firewall restricts flow to need-to-know staff through a clearance area, watch, restricted and rumor lists, documented and enforced procedures, and tighter proprietary trading. Crossing the wall goes through compliance. Tipping is a violation even if the recipient never trades.
- **II(B) is decided by intent (pp.105–106).** Information-based manipulation spreads false or misleading information. Transaction-based manipulation covers actions the member knows or should know could distort price or volume, or using a dominant position in a related derivative or underlying. A genuine view on mispricing is not manipulation even if it looks aggressive. Indicators are non-exhaustive: very short-lived orders, orders with no change in beneficial ownership, and orders cancelled before execution.
- **II(B) controls (p.106).** Firm procedures, surveillance across platforms, automated pretrade controls, algorithm controls and exception reporting.

## Vocabulary

- Material information — likely to affect price or matter to reasonable investors (p.96).
- Nonpublic information — not yet available to the market generally (p.97).
- Dissemination — making information known to the market, for example by release plus filing (p.97).
- Selective disclosure — giving information to a small group before the public (p.97).
- Mosaic theory — conclusions built from public and nonmaterial nonpublic data (p.98).
- Expert network — service linking investors with industry specialists (p.99).
- Firewall / information barrier — restricts confidential information to need-to-know staff (p.100).
- Watch list, restricted list, rumor list — tools to monitor or bar employee trading (p.100).
- Looking over the wall — controlled sharing through a compliance officer (p.101).
- Information-based manipulation — spreading false or misleading information (p.106).
- Pump and dump — inflating price with misleading positives, then selling (p.106).
- Transaction-based manipulation — trades that distort price or volume (p.106).
- Wash trade — named on p.106 without a definition; generally trades with no real change in beneficial ownership.
- Quiet period — window before earnings when a company avoids comment; used in II(B) Example 3 (p.108).

## Traps

- Telling a few large holders or a room of analysts is not public disclosure (p.97).
- A loss does not cure a violation, and a profit does not make it worse (Ex.8, p.104).
- Tipping is a violation by itself, even if the recipient never trades (Ex.1 p.101; Ex.7 p.103).
- Derivatives, funds and ETFs count; do not limit the ban to shares (p.96).
- Mosaic theory covers only public and nonmaterial nonpublic pieces (p.98). Do not stretch it to material inside information.
- Expert-network contracts do not shift responsibility (p.99; Ex.11 p.105).
- Reliability is part of materiality, so an unverified whisper may be noise (Ex.4, Ex.9), but verify and consult compliance rather than assume.
- II(B) turns on intent. Benefit to fund holders does not excuse a cross trade (Ex.4, p.108), while genuine mispricing trades are fine.

## Original practice seeds

A. Hypothetical: an analyst holds 20,000 shares of AlphaCo at 50 each. The CFO says a takeover offer at 70 comes Monday. She buys 10,000 more at 50. The tipped trade gains 10,000 x (70 − 50) = 200,000 if the price reaches 70. This is still a violation, and the CFO also violated by passing the tip. Correct step: do not trade, encourage dissemination, otherwise tell compliance only.

B. Hypothetical: the same tip comes from a stranger at a bar who "heard it from a customer". Only source reliability changed, so it may be nonmaterial and trading may be permitted. Still consult compliance.

C. Hypothetical: a stock averages 5,000 shares a day. A manager moves 2,000 shares a day between two accounts she controls for 10 days. Added volume = 10 x 2,000 = 20,000 shares, which is 20,000 / 50,000 = 40% of the normal 10-day volume (10 x 5,000 = 50,000). No change in beneficial ownership is an indicator of manipulation. Selling 1,000 shares a day (20% of average volume) through genuine arm's-length trades over 50 days is not manipulation.
