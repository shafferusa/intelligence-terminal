## Learning outcomes

1. Explain why effective duration and effective convexity suit bonds with embedded options (Curve-Based Interest Rate Risk Measures, pp.325–329).
2. Estimate the percent change in a bond's full price for a benchmark-curve move using effective duration and convexity (pp.330–332).
3. Define key rate duration and describe how it measures sensitivity at specific maturities (pp.333–336).
4. Distinguish empirical duration from analytical duration (pp.337–341).

## Core principles

- **Embedded options break yield-based measures.** A call or put makes cash flows depend on future rates, so no single yield describes the bond and Macaulay/modified duration do not fit. Reprice with an option model after shifting the benchmark curve up and down, holding call terms, spread and volatility fixed. Only as good as the model (p.325, PDF 335).
- **Callable bond: investor is short the option.** Callable price sits below the non-callable price; the gap is the option value. At high rates the two behave alike. As rates fall, gains are capped (price compression), effective duration shortens, and convexity turns negative past an inflection point (pp.325–326).
- **Putable bond: investor owns the option.** Price is always above the non-putable price. When rates rise the put cushions the loss, so duration falls; convexity stays positive (p.326).
- **Effective vs modified duration on a plain bond.** Close but not equal, because a par-curve shift moves spot rates non-parallel. The gap narrows for flatter curves, shorter maturities and prices near par, and vanishes only for a flat curve (p.327).
- **Duration plus convexity with curve inputs.** The convexity term takes the sign of effective convexity. For a callable with negative convexity, a rate rise hurts more than an equal fall helps. For option bonds a smaller shift does not automatically improve the estimate (pp.330–332).
- **Key rate duration splits risk by maturity.** Effective duration assumes a parallel shift. Key rate (partial) duration moves one maturity point at a time, drawn as a tent fading to zero at neighbouring key rates, exposing shaping risk. A portfolio's key rate duration at a point is each bond's duration times its weight, summed. Compare key rates with a curve forecast (pp.333–336).
- **Empirical vs analytical duration.** Analytical measures treat benchmark yields and spreads as uncorrelated; empirical duration comes from historical prices and captures co-movement. In a flight to quality, government yields fall while spreads widen, so for credit-risky bonds empirical duration is lower than analytical; for high-grade sovereigns they are similar. Estimation needs choices on factors, weights and data window (pp.337–341).

## Vocabulary

- Effective duration — sensitivity to a parallel benchmark-curve shift; works with uncertain cash flows (p.325, PDF 335).
- Effective convexity — curvature response to a parallel shift; can be negative (p.326, PDF 336).
- Curve vs yield duration — shift the benchmark curve vs the bond's own yield (p.325).
- Embedded option — call, put or prepayment right making cash flows rate-dependent (p.325).
- Call option value — non-callable minus callable price (p.325); put option value — putable minus non-putable price (p.326).
- Negative convexity — price rises by less than it falls for equal opposite moves (p.326, PDF 336).
- Key rate (partial) duration — sensitivity to a move at one maturity point (p.333, PDF 343).
- Shaping risk — exposure to slope or shape changes (p.334, PDF 344).
- Analytical vs empirical duration — formula-based vs estimated from historical prices (p.338, PDF 348).
- Flight to quality — selling risky assets for government bonds: benchmark yields fall, spreads widen (p.338).

## Formulas

1. **EffDur = (PV₋ − PV₊) / (2 × ΔCurve × PV₀)** (Eq. 1, p.327, PDF 337). PV₀ = current full price; PV₊ and PV₋ = full prices after the curve moves up and down; ΔCurve = shift as a decimal (25 bp = 0.0025). Unit: years.
2. **EffCon = [(PV₋ + PV₊) − 2 × PV₀] / (ΔCurve² × PV₀)** (Eq. 2, p.327, PDF 337). Same variables; the shift is squared. Can be negative.
3. **%ΔPV_Full ≈ (−EffDur × ΔCurve) + ½ × EffCon × (ΔCurve)²** (Eq. 3, p.331, PDF 341). Result is a decimal. The sign of ΔCurve flips only the duration term; the convexity term uses the square. Full price; approximation.
4. **KeyRateDur_k = −(1/PV) × (ΔPV/Δr_k)** (Eq. 4, p.334, PDF 344). ΔPV = price change when only the kth key rate moves by Δr_k (the text uses 1 bp up and down); the minus sign makes ordinary bonds positive.
5. **Σ KeyRateDur_k = EffDur**, k = 1 to n (Eq. 5, p.334, PDF 344). Parts add to the parallel measure.
6. **ΔPV/PV = −KeyRateDur_k × Δr_k** (Eq. 6, p.335, PDF 345). One key-rate move. For several moves, the separate effects add (inferred).
- Portfolio relation (p.335): portfolio duration is the market-value-weighted average of bond durations; each bond's key rate duration is its duration times its weight.

## Traps

- Saying callable duration rises as rates fall. It shortens, and convexity can go negative (p.326).
- Giving a putable bond negative convexity. It stays positive (p.326).
- Entering 25 bp as 25 instead of 0.0025 in Eq. 1–3 (pp.327, 331).
- Flipping the sign of the convexity term when rates fall. Only the duration term flips (p.331).
- Treating key rate durations as redundant. They add to effective duration but show where on the curve the risk sits (pp.334, 336).
- Assuming empirical duration is always smaller. Lower mainly for credit-risky bonds in stress; about the same for high-grade sovereigns (p.339).
- Believing a smaller shift always gives a better estimate for option bonds (p.331).

## Original practice seeds

All hypothetical.

**H1 (Eq. 1–3).** A callable bond has PV₀ = 98.00. A 50 bp parallel shift gives PV₋ = 99.00 and PV₊ = 96.00. EffDur = 3.00/(2 × 0.005 × 98.00) = 3.061. EffCon = (99 + 96 − 196)/(0.005² × 98) = −408.2. For +100 bp: −0.03061 − 0.02041 = −5.10%. For −100 bp: +0.03061 − 0.02041 = +1.02%. The loss is about five times the gain.

**H3 (key rates).** A portfolio has KRD at 2 years of 1.0 and at 10 years of 4.0 (nothing else), so EffDur = 5.0. The 2-year rate falls 20 bp and the 10-year rises 30 bp. Effects: +0.20% and −1.20%, total about −1.00%. A parallel +25 bp would give −1.25%, so the curve-shape view changes the answer.

**H4 (empirical).** A high-yield bond has analytical duration 6.0. In a stress period the government yield falls 50 bp and the spread widens 20 bp, so the bond's own yield falls 30 bp: price change about +6.0 × 0.0030 = +1.8%. Analytical duration on the benchmark move alone predicts +3.0%. The implied empirical duration against the benchmark move is 1.8/0.50 = 3.6, below 6.0.
