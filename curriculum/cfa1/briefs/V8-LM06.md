2. Describe how exposure is obtained: master-feeder, fund of one, SMA, fund of funds, replication ETFs. Printed pp.160–166 (PDF 168–174).
3. Analyze sources of hedge fund risk, return and diversification, including index biases. Printed pp.167–174 (PDF 175–182).

## Core principles
- **An approach, not an asset class.** A private pool run by a general partner for limited partners, holding ordinary assets but using leverage, shorting, derivatives and flexible mandates. The conclusion fails if you treat it as a return stream with its own fundamental source. pp.147–148 (PDF 155–156).
- **Pay follows performance, with guardrails.** Fixed fee plus a share of profit aligns the manager despite information asymmetry. A high-water mark withholds the incentive fee until value regains its peak; a hurdle until a minimum return is cleared. Order: management fee, then hurdle, then incentive. pp.149–150 (PDF 157–158).
- **Redemption limits exist because positions are hard to unwind.** Concentrated, leveraged holdings need stable capital, so lockups, notice periods and gates exist. A soft lockup exit fee goes to the fund, protecting remaining investors, not to the manager. Investors pay in liquidity risk, and opacity and light regulation add operational and fraud risk even if market risk is low. p.157 (PDF 165).
- **Each strategy has its own payoff and main risk.** Long/short equity is usually net long; short-biased is contrarian; market neutral targets near-zero beta and needs leverage, so a volatility shock hurts. Merger arbitrage buys the target below the deal price: a small spread is the reward, a broken deal the loss, magnified by leverage. Distressed investors buy discounted debt aiming at a claim that converts to equity; activists take large public stakes and push change. Event-driven prospers in strong, deal-rich economies. Convertible arbitrage is long the convertible, short stock by option delta, betting on convergence; credit risk can be hedged. Macro trades top-down; managed futures follow trends and suffer in choppy, mean-reverting markets. pp.152–156 (PDF 160–164).
- **Structures trade cost, control and liquidity.** Master-feeder pools feeders into one trading master for tax and regulatory convenience, but does not remove tax for taxable-jurisdiction investors. A fund of one or SMA gives a large investor control, transparency and cheaper negotiated fees, at the price of operational and governance work and weaker manager alignment. A fund of funds adds diversification, access and due diligence for a second fee layer. Replication ETFs are cheap and liquid but earn less. pp.160–164 (PDF 168–172).
- **Return = market beta + strategy beta + alpha, and indexes flatter it.** Hedge funds limit market beta and seek idiosyncratic return, but fees take part of alpha. Voluntary reporting creates selection, survivorship and backfill bias, and indexes are non-investable and lagged. A fund-of-funds composite is the preferred benchmark. pp.167–169 (PDF 175–177).
- **Diversification depends on the regime.** Market-neutral, relative value and event-driven styles help in downturns and lag in uniform rallies. Equity correlation is date-dependent. Adding hedge funds to a stock/bond mix typically lowers volatility and raises risk-adjusted return. pp.171–174 (PDF 179–182).

## Vocabulary
- Hedge fund — private pool defined by methods, not holdings (p.147).
- GP / LP — manager / capital provider (p.148).
- Side letter — investor-specific terms outside fund documents (pp.148, 161).
- High-water mark — incentive fee only above prior peak (p.150).
- Hurdle rate — minimum return before incentive fee (pp.149–150).
- Two and twenty / one or thirty — 2% management plus 20% of net profit; greater of 1% or 30% of excess return (p.161).
- Lockup, notice period, gate, soft lockup — redemption limits; soft lockup fee paid to fund (p.157).
- Long/short, short-biased, market neutral — equity styles (pp.152–153).
- Merger arbitrage, deal spread — buy target, earn gap to deal price (p.154).
- Distressed, fulcrum security — discounted debt; claim converting to ownership (p.154).
- Activist — large public stake used to force change (p.154).
- Convertible arbitrage — long convertible, short stock by delta (p.155).

## Formulas
None printed. The notes restate the fee problem's logic (printed pp.149–150, PDF 157–158):
- Management fee = rate × end-of-year assets.
- Performance fee = rate × (gain − hurdle rate × starting capital − management fee).
- Net return = (gain − total fees) / starting capital.

Units: currency or percent. Assume management fee on end value, hurdle on starting capital; always follow the stated terms. Intuition: costs come off before the manager shares profit, so a halved gain can cut the incentive fee far more than half.

## Traps
- Calling hedge funds an asset class, or assuming all hedge and use leverage (p.151).
- Assuming relative value is a single asset class.
- Thinking event-driven wants calm markets.
- Wrong fee order, or hurdle before management fee.
- Believing offshore master-feeder removes tax for taxable investors.
- Treating alpha as investor return; fees and layering reduce it.
- Mixing up survivorship (dead funds vanish), backfill (new reporter's history added) and selection (who reports). Societal bias is not one.
- Assuming hedge funds are always low-correlated with stocks.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. **Fee waterfall.** Start 200m, end 230m before fees. Management 2% × 230 = 4.6m. Hurdle 5% × 200 = 10m. Excess = 30 − 10 − 4.6 = 15.4m; incentive 20% = 3.08m. Total 7.68m; net 22.32m = 11.16%. If end is 215m: management 4.3m, excess 0.7m, incentive 0.14m, net 10.56m = 5.28%.
2. **Fee layering.** 100m, 10% gross gain, underlying 2 and 20, no hurdle: management 2m, incentive 20% × 8 = 1.6m, leaving 6.4m. Fund of funds charging 1% (1m): 5.4m = 5.4%. Adding 10% incentive on 5.4m (0.54m): 4.86m = 4.86%.
3. **Merger arbitrage.** Target 47, deal 50, six months: spread 3/47 = 6.38% (about 13.2% compounded annual). At 2x, about 12.8% in six months. If the deal breaks and price falls to 38: −9/47 = −19.1%, about −38% at 2x.
