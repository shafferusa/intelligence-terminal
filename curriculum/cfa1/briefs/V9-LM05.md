## Learning outcomes
1. Compare cognitive errors with emotional biases (Behavioral Bias Categories, p.204; Summary pp.230–231).
2. Discuss the commonly recognized biases and what they mean for financial decisions (Cognitive Errors pp.204–216; Emotional Biases pp.217–225).
3. Describe how biases can produce market patterns that traditional finance does not explain (Behavioral Finance and Market Behavior, pp.226–230).

## Core principles
- **Two families (p.204).** Cognitive errors come from faulty reasoning, so better data and education can moderate (reduce) them. Emotional biases come from feeling and impulse, so the practical answer is to adapt: build the plan around the client's tendency.
- **Belief perseverance (pp.205–209).** Cognitive dissonance, the discomfort of conflicting facts, pushes people to protect prior views. Conservatism overweights the prior and underreacts to new evidence. Confirmation seeks only supportive evidence. Representativeness classifies by resemblance and neglects base rates and sample size. Illusion of control and hindsight complete the group. Risk angle: concentrated holdings and excess trading. Fix: seek disconfirming views, use base rates, write decisions down.
- **Processing errors (pp.210–216).** Anchoring starts from a reference number and adjusts too little. Mental accounting treats money as labelled buckets although money is fungible. Framing changes answers with the wording (gain frame tends toward risk aversion, loss frame toward risk seeking). Availability judges likelihood by ease of recall. Fix: view the whole portfolio as one and focus on future prospects.
- **Loss aversion and the disposition effect (pp.217–218).** Losses hurt more than equal gains please; the value function is steeper below the reference point. The investor is risk-averse in gains (sells winners early) and risk-seeking in losses (holds losers), leaving a riskier, less efficient portfolio. The conclusion fails if the loser truly has better forward prospects, so judge each holding on expectations, not purchase price.
- **Overconfidence (p.219).** Faith in one's skill beyond the evidence; filed as emotional although it has cognitive features. Prediction form: ranges too narrow. Certainty form: probabilities too high. Self-attribution feeds it. Consequences: underestimated risk, heavy trading, weak diversification. Fix: review trading records over a long period and separate skill from a rising market.
- **Inertia family (pp.220–225).** Self-control favors the short-term payoff (hyperbolic discounting) and under-saves. Status quo is passive inertia. Endowment values what you own above what you would pay for it. Regret aversion avoids action because a failed action stings more than a failed inaction, and it encourages herding. All can leave a portfolio unchanged, but the motive and the fix differ.
- **Anomalies are not automatically mispricing (pp.226–227).** An apparent abnormal return may vanish under a different pricing model (risk compensation), may be a statistical artifact (small samples, survivorship, data mining), or may be temporary and arbitraged away once known. Only survivors need behavioral explanations.
- **Market patterns (pp.227–230).** Momentum is linked to availability/recency, hindsight and regret. Bubbles have rational parts (unknown crash timing, limited arbitrage, career risk of standing aside) and behavioral parts (overconfidence, overtrading, confirmation, self-attribution, regret pulling people in; anchoring and dissonance slow the exit). Value stocks historically beat growth stocks, but a multi-factor model can remove the effect, so it may be distress-risk compensation; behavioral alternatives are the halo effect, overconfident growth forecasts, familiarity and home bias.

## Vocabulary
- Cognitive error — reasoning flaw; correctable with information (p.204).
- Emotional bias — feeling-driven pattern; adapt to it (p.204).
- Moderate vs adapt — reduce a bias vs build the plan around it (p.231 / PDF 241).
- Cognitive dissonance — discomfort when new facts clash with held beliefs (p.205).
- Conservatism — underweighting new evidence relative to the prior (p.205).
- Confirmation bias — favoring information that supports the view (p.205).
- Representativeness — classifying by resemblance; base-rate and sample-size neglect (p.207).
- Illusion of control — thinking you can steer chance outcomes (p.208).
- Hindsight bias — outcomes seem predictable all along (p.209).
- Anchoring and adjustment — insufficient move from an initial figure (p.210).
- Mental accounting — separate buckets for fungible money (p.211).
- Framing / narrow framing — the answer depends on how the choice is posed (p.212).
- Availability — judging by ease of recall: retrievability, categorization, narrow experience, resonance (p.214).
- Disposition effect — selling winners too soon, holding losers too long (p.217).
- Prediction vs certainty overconfidence; self-attribution (p.219).
- Herding — copying the crowd to avoid regret (p.223).
- Halo effect — one good trait colours the overall judgment; a form of representativeness (p.230).
- Home bias — favoring domestic or nearby firms (p.230).

## Traps
- Overconfidence is classified as emotional even though it looks cognitive (p.219).
- Moderate (cognitive) is not the same as adapt (emotional) (pp.204, 231).
- Conservatism is about updating on new evidence; anchoring is about adjusting from a starting number (pp.205, 210).
- Representativeness (resemblance, base rates) is not availability (ease of recall) (pp.207, 214).
- Status quo, endowment and regret aversion all produce "keep holding" but differ in motive: inertia, valuing ownership, fear of regret (p.221).
- The disposition effect is a result of loss aversion, not a separate bias (p.217).
- A value or momentum premium is not proof of mispricing; a multi-factor model can absorb value (p.230).
- An anomaly needs persistence and a predictable sign (pp.226–227).
- Bubbles can be partly rational: limited arbitrage and career risk (p.228).

## Original practice seeds
1. Hypothetical disposition effect. Stock W bought at 50, now 70, expected return 8% over one year; Stock L bought at 50, now 35, expected return 2%. Selling W gives up 70 x 1.08 = 75.60 expected value (an expected gain of 5.60 per share) while keeping the weaker asset (L worth 35 x 1.02 = 35.70). If L's expected return were -10%, L would be worth 31.50, so holding costs more.
2. Hypothetical base rate. In a sector, 3 of 100 firms fail in a downturn. A distressed firm resembles past failures and an analyst says 80%. A sound estimate starts near 3% and moves up with firm-specific evidence (say, to 15%). Bias: representativeness (base-rate neglect). If the base rate were 40%, the sensible starting point changes.
3. Hypothetical mental accounting. 100,000 in bonds for "safe goals" and 20,000 in one stock as "fun money": total 120,000, so the stock is 20,000/120,000 = 16.7% of wealth. If it halves, total wealth falls 10,000/120,000 = 8.3%, a real loss, not a trivial one.
