## Core principles
- **Two axes organize the module.** Political cooperation (agreement on rules and standards, mostly between governments) differs from globalization (flows of goods, capital, people and information, mostly driven by private firms). They are correlated but separate: flows can continue without government support. Failure case: assuming cooperation guarantees open flows (pp.145, 152–155).
- **Interests are ranked.** Each country ranks its national interests, with survival at the top. When two cooperation tactics conflict, the higher-ranked need wins, e.g. military needs override tariff harmonization in wartime. Rankings vary by country and shift with leaders and events; short political cycles favor short-term goals. Risk arises when two partners' rankings diverge (pp.149–150).
- **Institutions make cooperation durable.** Strong institutions (rule of law, property rights, accountability) make a country credible and embed cooperation across society, so defecting costs more (p.148).
- **Globalization pays, but unevenly.** Firms gain from profit (more customers, lower costs, scale), access to resources and markets, and non-financial gains. Costs: unequal gains (an aggregate gain is not a gain for everyone), weaker standards if firms follow lax local rules, political backlash, and interdependence. Firms facing rollback can bring essentials home, rebuild supply chains with duplication, or concentrate on key markets locally. Full reversal is unlikely (pp.152–159).
- **Archetypes combine both axes.** Multilateralism (cooperative, globalized), hegemony (non-cooperative, globalized), bilateralism (cooperative, nationalist), autarky (non-cooperative, nationalist). Interdependence lowers the chance a partner attacks you but raises exposure to shocks elsewhere. Movement within and between quadrants matters, and a change in tools signals a change in archetype (pp.167–171).
- **Tools: national security, economic, financial.** Cooperative versions increase flows between countries; conflict-escalating ones cut flows (investment limits, sanctions, nationalization). Dominance of one currency helps cooperation but leaves others exposed to that country's policy tightening. Stable places attract capital and labor; risky ones lose them and must offer higher returns (pp.172–177).
- **Risk is assessed on three dimensions.** Likelihood, velocity (speed of onset and market reaction) and size and nature of impact. High velocity means short-term price volatility and tactical response; medium means sector effects; low means slow shifts for asset allocation and ESG. High likelihood with low impact needs little; high impact with low likelihood warrants a scenario but not constant monitoring. Because risks are non-linear, use scenarios and signposts, not point forecasts (pp.181–184).
- **Premium logic.** Persistent geopolitical risk raises discount rates and required returns, lowering valuations with no change in cash flows. This is why riskier markets trade at a discount (pp.177, 185–186).

## Vocabulary
- Geopolitics — how geography shapes politics and relations among nations (p.144).
- State actor — government or leader that controls national security and resources (p.144).
- Non-state actor — participant such as an NGO or multinational that takes part without that control (pp.144–145).
- National interest — the aims a country pursues, whether military, economic or cultural (p.149).
- Hierarchy of interests — ranking of national interests, survival first (p.149).
- Political cooperation — degree of agreement on rules and standardization (p.145).
- Standardization — common procedures for making, selling or moving things across borders (p.147).
- Soft power — influence by attraction, such as culture and exchanges, not force (p.148).
- Institution — established formal or informal organization or practice (p.148).
- Globalization — integration of people, firms and governments across borders (p.152).
- Nationalism (antiglobalization) — favoring one's own economic interests over others' (p.154).
- Portfolio flows vs FDI — short-term securities vs long-term productive capacity (p.156).
- Reshoring, reglobalizing, doubling down — rollback coping tactics (p.159).
- IMF / World Bank (IBRD, IDA) / WTO — the three institutions of LO3 (pp.159–165).
- Autarky, hegemony, multilateralism, bilateralism — the archetypes (pp.167–171).
- Cabotage — restricting domestic transport to national operators; a multifaceted tool (p.176).
- Event, exogenous, thematic risk — known-date, sudden, known-and-evolving (pp.178–180).
- Velocity — how fast a risk reaches markets (p.181).
- Signpost — indicator that a risk is becoming more or less likely (p.184).
- GPR — Geopolitical Risk Index, built from news-article counts (p.186).

## Traps
- Cooperation is not globalization: rules among states vs flows among mostly private actors (p.155).
- Interdependence is double-edged: fewer attacks from partners, more spillover vulnerability (pp.167, 181).
- Predictability of an event risk lets you prepare but does not change likelihood, speed or size (p.179).
- Separate event (dated), exogenous (sudden, unanticipated) and thematic (known, slowly evolving) risk (pp.178–180).
- High velocity does not mean lasting damage: fast risks move prices quickly; slow ones erode inputs and valuations over time (p.185).
- Follow policy, not political noise (p.184).
- Aggregate gain from globalization does not mean everyone gains (p.157).
- Sanctions and nationalization are non-cooperative tools; armed conflict is the extreme national security tool (pp.172–175).

## Original practice seeds
1. **Scenario test (hypothetical).** A USD 100m portfolio holds USD 60m equities and USD 40m government bonds. Downside scenario over a month: equities -15%, bonds +3%. Loss 60 x 0.15 = 9.0m; gain 40 x 0.03 = 1.2m; net -7.8m, or -7.8%. If equities fall 25%, net is 15.0 - 1.2 = -13.8m (-13.8%).
2. **Risk premium and value (hypothetical).** A share pays USD 5 at each year end forever. At 8%, value is 5 / 0.08 = USD 62.50. A persistent 3-point geopolitical premium makes it 11%: 5 / 0.11 = USD 45.45, down 27.3%, with cash flows unchanged.
3. **Archetype drift (hypothetical).** Country Z signs a multilateral trade pact, then adds export restraints, domestic content rules and investment limits. It drifts from multilateralism toward hegemony or autarky; raise likelihood and velocity estimates and move signposts from green toward amber.
