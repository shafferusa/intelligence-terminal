## Core principles

- **Alternatives are a residual label** (p.6, PDF 14). Anything that is not public equity, public fixed income or cash. Investors want diversification and/or higher expected return, and pay with illiquidity, long horizons, large outlays and a need for specialist skill.
- **Three categories: private capital, real assets, hedge funds** (pp.7–11, PDF 15–19). Private capital is equity or debt raised outside public markets. Real assets are mostly physical assets that produce cash flows or hold value. Hedge funds are identified by how they invest (leverage, derivatives, short selling), not by what they hold, so their risk can differ sharply from owning the same shares outright.
- **Commodities produce no cash flow** (p.9, PDF 17). Return is price change only, unlike farmland or timberland, which also earn crop, lease or timber cash flows.
- **Control rises and fee burden falls from fund to co-investment to direct** (pp.12–18, PDF 20–26). In a fund the investor only decides in or out and pays management plus performance fees. Co-investment is a fund stake plus the right to add money to one deal at a lower fee, at the cost of more oversight work. Direct investing needs in-house skill and monitoring. Manager's reasons for co-invest (p.16): speed, deals beyond fund limits, diversification.
- **The limited partnership is the typical wrapper** (pp.19–21, PDF 27–29). The GP runs the fund, has unlimited liability in theory, and may commit its own money. LPs are passive, liability is capped at their investment, and commitments are drawn over time. The LPA sets the rules. Side letters give particular LPs bespoke terms (for example an excusal right); a most-favoured-nation clause lets others claim better terms.
- **Fees align a dependent investor with a skilled manager** (pp.22–28, PDF 30–36). Management fee roughly 1–2% plus a performance fee. Hedge funds and REITs charge on AUM; PE funds on committed capital, which reduces (does not remove) the incentive to rush capital out.
- **Hurdle, catch-up, high-water mark, clawback, waterfall** (pp.23–25, PDF 31–33). Hurdle: minimum return before a performance fee. Hard: fee only on the excess. Soft: once cleared, fee on the whole return. Catch-up: LPs take everything until the hurdle is met, then the GP takes everything until it holds its agreed share of total profit, then the agreed split applies. High-water mark (hedge funds): no new performance fee until value recovers above the prior peak. Clawback: LPs reclaim fees already paid if later losses erase earlier gains. Deal-by-deal (American) waterfall favours the GP; whole-of-fund (European) pays the GP only after LPs receive capital plus hurdle on the whole fund and favours LPs.

## Vocabulary

- Alternative investment — anything other than public equity, public fixed income or cash (p.6).
- Private equity / private debt; venture capital = PE for early-stage firms (p.7).
- Leveraged buyout — debt-financed PE purchase (named only) (p.7).
- Venture debt / distressed debt — loans to early-stage firms / debt of issuers near bankruptcy (p.8).
- Real assets — physical, and some intangible, assets that yield cash flows or store value (p.8).
- Infrastructure, PPP, concession agreement — long-lived public-use assets; public plus private financing; contract giving the private party build, operate and fee rights for a period (p.8).
- Natural resources (less-developed land) vs commodities (standardized goods) (p.9).
- Digital asset, cryptocurrency, token — electronically held asset with rights; coin on its own blockchain; asset built on an existing blockchain (p.10).
- Hedge fund; fund of funds (p.11).
- Co-investment rights — invest directly beside the fund in a deal (p.16).
- Committed capital — total LPs promise, drawn over time (p.22).

- MLP, special purpose entity, joint venture (p.22); REIT (p.8); term sheet (p.15).

## Formulas

1. **Hard hurdle, no catch-up:** r_GP = max[0, p × (r − r_h)] (p.23, PDF 31). r = fund return, r_h = hard hurdle, p = GP performance-fee share, r_GP = GP fee as a return on fund capital; all % per period. One period.
2. **With catch-up:** r_GP = max[0, r_cu + p × (r − r_h − r_cu)] (p.23, PDF 31). r_cu = catch-up return paid straight to the GP after the hurdle. Caveat (notes' own derivation, not the book's): the catch-up is complete when r_cu = p × r_h / (1 − p), and the formula holds only if r ≥ r_h + r_cu; between r_h and that point the GP takes all of the excess.

## Traps

- Calling a hedge fund an exotic asset; holdings can be ordinary (pp.4, 11).
- Treating VC as separate from PE. VC is the early-stage subset; PE mostly targets mature or declining firms (pp.7–8).
- Expecting commodities to pay income (p.9).
- Confusing commitment with cash paid. PE fees run on committed capital; hedge-fund and REIT fees on AUM (pp.20, 22).
- High-water mark vs clawback: HWM blocks future fees until the old peak is regained; clawback returns fees already paid (p.24; solutions p.31).
- Thinking co-investment replaces the fund; the investor stays in the fund and adds a direct stake (p.16).
- An excusal right exists only through a side letter (solutions).

## Original practice seeds

A. (Hypothetical) Hard hurdle. One-year return 14%, hurdle 6%, fee 20% of the excess, management fee ignored. r_GP = 20% × (14% − 6%) = 1.6%. If the return were 5%: max[0, 20% × (−1%)] = 0, so no performance fee.

B. (Hypothetical) Catch-up. Return 20%, hard hurdle 5%, p = 20%. Catch-up needed: 20% × 5% / 80% = 1.25%. r_GP = 1.25% + 20% × (20% − 5% − 1.25%) = 1.25% + 2.75% = 4.0%, which is 20% of the whole 20% return. Without catch-up: 20% × 15% = 3.0%.

C. (Hypothetical) Clawback. Two deals of USD50m each. Deal 1 earns USD20m profit, so a deal-by-deal 20% carry pays the GP USD4m. Deal 2 loses USD20m, so fund-level profit is zero and the USD4m is clawed back. A whole-of-fund waterfall would never have paid it.
