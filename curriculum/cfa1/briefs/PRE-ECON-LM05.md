## Core principles
- **Why policy exists.** Only governments can move a whole economy. Monetary policy is the central bank steering money and credit; fiscal policy is government taxing and spending. Both aim to smooth the cycle and keep inflation stable; fiscal policy can also redistribute. Shocks are hard to read and policy can itself feed booms (p.171–172).
- **Money solves barter.** Barter needs a double coincidence of wants and a price for every pair of goods. Good money is accepted, of known value, divisible, valuable per unit weight and hard to counterfeit. A currency that stops holding value tends to lose its other roles too (p.173–174).
- **Banks create deposits by lending.** A bank keeps a fraction of deposits as reserves and lends the rest; the borrower spends, the seller deposits at another bank, which repeats the step. Total deposits = new deposit / reserve requirement, a maximum: excess reserves or cash not redeposited shrink it. The central bank can start expansion by buying securities or lending reserves (p.174–176).
- **Quantity theory.** M x V = P x Y is an identity. Add steady velocity and neutral money, and more money means a higher price level. Monetarists say money growth drives inflation; causation may instead run from real activity to money (p.178).
- **Money demand has three motives.** Transactions and precautionary demand grow with activity; speculative demand falls when other assets' returns rise (higher opportunity cost) and rises when their perceived risk rises (p.178–179).
- **Money market clears through bond prices.** The nominal interest rate acts as the cost of holding money. The money supply line is vertical; demand slopes down. Above equilibrium there is excess money supply: holders buy bonds, prices rise, yields fall. Below equilibrium the reverse. This assumes fixed stocks of money and bonds. A supply increase shifts the line right: rates fall, and in the long run the price level rises (p.180–181).
- **Fisher effect.** With neutral money the real rate is stable, so nominal rate changes track expected inflation: nominal = real + expected inflation (p.182).
- **Inflation costs depend on expectations.** Indexed or perfectly predicted inflation can be adjusted for. Surprises move wealth between lenders and borrowers, raise lenders' premium and blur price signals (p.183–184).

## Vocabulary
- Monetary policy — central bank action on the quantity of money and credit (p.172; PDF 180).
- Fiscal policy — government decisions on taxation and spending (p.172).
- Medium of exchange — asset accepted to buy goods or settle debts (p.173).
- Store of wealth (carries value through time) and unit of account (common yardstick for prices and debts) (p.174).
- Fractional reserve banking — lending out part of deposits while keeping a fraction in reserve (p.175).
- Reserve requirement — share of deposits held back; voluntary or set by the central bank (p.175).
- Money multiplier — maximum expansion per unit of reserves, 1 / reserve requirement (p.176).
- Narrow money — notes, coins and very liquid deposits (p.177).
- Broad money — narrow money plus a wider set of liquid assets (p.177).
- Velocity of circulation — average number of times a unit of currency changes hands per period (p.178).
- Money neutrality — money changes move the price level, not real output or velocity; monetarists hold that money growth drives inflation (p.178).
- Transactions, precautionary, speculative balances — the three motives for holding money (p.178–179).
- Fisher effect — nominal rate equals real rate plus expected inflation (p.182).
- Expected vs unexpected inflation — the anticipated level vs the surprise (p.183); inflation uncertainty — how hard it is to forecast (p.184).

## Formulas
1. **Total deposits created** = new deposit / reserve requirement. Reserve requirement as a decimal. The result includes the original deposit. Assumes full lending, full redepositing, no excess reserves; it is a maximum. (p.175; PDF 183)
2. **Money multiplier** = 1 / reserve requirement (decimal, unitless). Inverse to the requirement; same maximum caveat. (p.176; PDF 184; restated p.185)
3. **Equation of exchange: M x V = P x Y.** M money (currency units), V velocity (turnovers per period), P price level, Y real output (units per period). An identity; the quantity theory adds constant V and neutral Y. The full version uses T (all transactions including financial) instead of Y (footnote, p.178). (p.178; PDF 186)
4. **Fisher effect: R_nom = R_real + expected inflation.** Rates over the same horizon (per year for a one-year loan). Assumes a stable real rate. The Summary adds a risk premium that Equation 3 omits. (p.182; PDF 190)

## Traps
- Treating the multiplier as a guaranteed result; it is a ceiling, cut by excess reserves and cash held outside banks.
- Moving reserve requirement and multiplier in the same direction; they are inverse.
- Drawing money supply as upward sloping here; it is vertical.
- Mixing up excess money supply (rate above equilibrium) and excess demand (below).
- Saying speculative demand rises when other assets' returns rise; it falls.
- Reading M x V = P x Y as proof money causes inflation; it is an identity, causation is the monetarist assumption.
- Saying the Fisher effect makes nominal rates stable; the real rate is stable.
- Calling credit cards money like debit cards; credit cards defer payment, debit draws on existing balances (p.177).
- Assuming inflation is costly regardless of type; expected and unexpected inflation differ.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A customer deposits USD 2,000 in a system with a 20% reserve requirement. Multiplier = 1/0.20 = 5; maximum total deposits = 2,000 x 5 = USD 10,000 (check 2,000/0.20). First bank keeps 400, lends 1,600. If the requirement falls to 10%, maximum deposits = 2,000/0.10 = USD 20,000, double.
2. Fisher: real required rate 2%, expected inflation 3%, so nominal 5%. If expected inflation rises to 6% with a stable real rate, nominal = 8%. If inflation then turns out 9%, the lender's realised real return is about -1%, and the borrower gains.
3. Quantity equation: M = 500, V = 4 per year, Y = 1,000 units. P = (500 x 4)/1,000 = 2.0. If M rises to 600 with V and Y fixed: P = 2,400/1,000 = 2.4, a 20% rise matching the money increase.
