1. Describe key features of business models: who, what, where, how much, how; value proposition; value chain; pricing and revenue models; organization and capabilities. Taught in Defining the Business Model, p.215–229 (PDF 223–237).
2. Describe types of business models: conventional models, variations, innovation, network-effect and platform models. Taught in Business Model Types, p.230–238 (PDF 238–246).

## Core principles
- **A business model is a story of how the firm makes money.** Five elements: who the customers are, what is offered and why they want it, where (channels) it is delivered, how much it is priced, and how it is organized and executed. It is not a business plan; it needs just enough detail to show how the parts connect to revenue, cost, assets and profit. The analyst builds an independent view because management descriptions can overstate market size or differentiation (p.215–219).
- **Segment and need decide competitors and forecasts.** The customer's underlying need (not the product category) shows who competes. Narrow segments bring entry barriers, shifts in the segment, and rivalry as risks. Business buyers are profit-driven; consumer needs are diverse. A missing element can be inferred, for example a firm moving to a broader market informs unit and price forecasts (p.217–219).
- **Channels change cost behavior.** Traditional channels pass goods through wholesalers and retailers. Direct sales keeps the firm close to customers but carries large, partly fixed costs (salaries). Omnichannel mixes both. Differing channels reduce comparability between competitors (p.219–221).
- **Pricing power vs price taker.** Many rivals and undifferentiated goods make the firm a price taker, so it must win on cost leadership and cannot pass on cost increases. Few rivals or real differentiation give pricing power. A premium may be justified by lower total cost of ownership, or simply by brand. Management may overstate differentiation (p.221–222).
- **Pricing models are about who pays, when and how much.** Price discrimination charges by willingness to pay; net price is after discounts. Multi-product ideas: bundling, razor/razorblade (cheap device, profitable proprietary consumables; fails if rivals sell compatible consumables), add-on pricing (overuse harms goodwill). Growth ideas: penetration pricing (a temporary discount that acts as marketing spend, which only pays if customers stay), freemium, hidden revenue (a third party pays, such as advertisers). Alternatives to ownership: subscription, leasing, licensing, franchising (p.222–224).
- **Execution and unit economics.** The value chain is the set of activities inside one firm that create value; the supply chain spans all steps and firms needed to deliver a physical product. Vertical integration brings control and proprietary technology but needs capital and fixed cost; outsourcing brings flexibility but supplier dependence (single-source risk, a supplier who is also a competitor). A model must show how it profits: per-unit revenue and cost, margin, break-even, and how costs behave as volume rises. High fixed costs mean scale lowers unit cost and a new entrant matching price may lose money (p.225–229).
- **Conventional models and variations.** Eight standard types (resource producer, manufacturer, distributor, retailer, broker, bank, service producer, software), each with its typical customers and revenue source, plus variations: contract manufacturer, value-added reseller, licensing, franchising (p.230–232).
- **Innovation and network effects.** Innovators (often entrants using technology) cut cost or change the payment pattern, then become conventional. Digital technology cuts the cost of communication and transactions, so location matters less and network effects strengthen. In a network, each user's benefit rises as users join; multi-sided platforms connect different user groups that attract each other. This is a barrier to entry and an advantage, so the key metrics are users and user growth. (p.233–238).

## Vocabulary
- Business model — how a firm works: who, what, where, how much, how (p.216).
- Customer segment — group of customers sharing attributes (p.217–218).
- B2B / B2C — customers are firms / households (p.217).
- Channel — route by which the offer reaches customers (p.219).
- Direct sales — selling without intermediaries (p.220).
- Omnichannel — blended digital and physical channels (p.220).
- Price taker — accepts the market price (p.221).
- Pricing power — ability to hold or raise price without losing demand (p.221).
- Cost leadership — competing through the lowest cost base (p.221).
- Total cost of ownership — all costs a buyer bears over an asset's life (p.221).
- Net price — price after discounts and promotions (p.222).
- Penetration pricing — discounting to win share (p.223).
- Hidden revenue model — user pays nothing; another party pays (p.223).
- Value proposition — what makes customers choose this offer at its price (p.224).
- Value chain vs supply chain — one firm's value-adding activities vs the end-to-end delivery network (p.226–227).
- Vertical integration — performing key upstream steps in house (p.226).
- Unit economics — revenue and cost per unit (p.227).
- Network effect; one-sided vs multi-sided; crowdsourcing — value grows with users; one vs several user types; users supply content (p.234).
- Disintermediation — platform displaces an intermediary (p.235–236).

## Traps
- Confusing value chain (inside one firm) with supply chain (across firms).
- Assuming a premium price must be justified by cost savings; brand can be enough.
- Treating penetration pricing as a plain price cut; it is a marketing-like cost that depends on retention.
- Thinking "free" means no revenue; check who pays (hidden revenue).
- Using list price when net price is what the firm earns.
- Calling direct sales always better; it adds large fixed cost.
- Assuming network effects arrive automatically; they need users and connections.
- Mixing up pricing labels: subscription (recurring access fee) is not a product sale; franchising adds territory and support to licensing.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. Razor/razorblade. A device sells for 80 and costs 100. Cartridges sell for 30, cost 10; a customer buys 18 over three years. Revenue 80 + 18×30 = 620; cost 100 + 18×10 = 280; profit 340. If generics take half the cartridge volume (9 bought): revenue 350, cost 190, profit 160. The proprietary-consumable assumption drives the result.
2. Penetration pricing. An app costs 4 per user-month to serve; list 10, promo 6 for the first 3 months. Margin 2 then 6. For 1,000 users over 12 months: 3×1,000×2 + 9×1,000×6 = 60,000. If half leave after the promo: 6,000 + 9×500×6 = 33,000. Retention decides whether the discount pays.
3. Network effect (my illustration; the book gives no formula). Connections among n users = n(n−1)/2: 10 users give 45; 20 users give 190, about 4.2 times as many. Doubling users more than doubles possible connections.
