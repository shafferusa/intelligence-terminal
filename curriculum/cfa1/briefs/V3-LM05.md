## Learning outcomes
1. Describe the types of capital investment: going concern, regulatory/compliance, expansion, new lines/other (Capital Investments, p.139–145).
2. Describe the capital allocation process; calculate NPV, IRR and ROIC and contrast their uses (Capital Allocation, p.146–157).
3. Describe capital allocation principles and common pitfalls, split into cognitive errors and behavioral biases (p.158–162).
4. Describe the kinds of real options relevant to capital investments (p.163–168).

## Core principles
- **Capex is cash now, expense later.** A long-lived asset is recorded at cost; cash leaves when paid, while the income statement charges non-cash depreciation or amortization over the years of benefit. Depreciation matters to project cash flows only through its tax effect (p.139–140, p.159).
- **Projects sort by purpose and risk.** Maintain the business (going concern, regulatory) versus grow it (expansion, new lines). Risk rises from going concern (lowest) to new lines (often highest). Compliance spending is forced by a third party and may be done even at negative NPV (p.141–145).
- **Match financing to asset life.** Short debt on a long asset creates rollover risk. Outsiders usually proxy maintenance capex with D&A and treat the rest of capex as expansion (p.141).
- **NPV measures value added to shareholders.** Discount after-tax incremental cash flows at the return available on equally risky alternatives. NPV >= 0 is necessary, not sufficient (competing projects, constraints, strategy). A zero-NPV project has no cushion for forecast error (p.147–149).
- **IRR is NPV's percentage cousin, with flaws.** It is the rate that sets NPV to zero; accept if IRR >= r (the hurdle). It implicitly assumes reinvestment at the IRR, while NPV assumes reinvestment at r. Several sign changes can give multiple IRRs, and IRR ignores size and duration, so for mutually exclusive projects pick the higher NPV (p.150–153).
- **ROIC is the outsider's company-level scorecard.** After-tax operating profit over average long-term liabilities plus equity; compare with the blended debt-and-equity required return. It is accounting-based, backward-looking, mixes all projects, and has no standard definition (p.153–157). NPV and IRR need inside forecasts; ROIC does not.
- **Internal cash is not free, and inflation must be consistent.** Retained operating cash is shareholders' capital, so the same risk-adjusted r applies. Nominal flows go with a nominal rate, real with real (p.159–160).
- **Real options are rights, not obligations.** They add value through later adaptation. If NPV is positive without them they only add upside; if negative, their value may flip the decision, but it rests on unobservable inputs. They are not a commodity hedge (p.163–168).

## Vocabulary
- Capital investment — asset lasting a year or more, recorded at cost (p.139).
- Going concern project — spending to keep current operations running (p.141).
- Rollover risk — refinancing danger when debt matures before the asset's life ends (p.141).
- Regulatory/compliance project — spending required by an outside authority (p.142).
- New line of business — project or acquisition outside the core (p.143).
- Capital allocation — how management and the board choose projects and return surplus cash (p.146).
- Required rate of return / opportunity cost (r) — return on similarly risky alternatives (p.147).
- Hurdle rate — the r used as the IRR cutoff (p.151).
- Invested capital — long-term liabilities plus equity (p.153).
- Capital turnover — sales divided by invested capital (p.155).
- Incremental cash flow — change in the firm's cash flows caused by the project, including cannibalization (p.159).
- Sunk cost — already spent, unaffected by the decision; ignored (p.159).
- Cognitive error vs behavioral bias — flawed analysis/inputs versus distorted judgment such as inertia (p.159–161).
- Real option families — timing, sizing (abandon/grow), flexibility (price-setting, production), fundamental (p.163–164).
- Decision tree — probability-weighted branches of outcomes over time (p.165–166).

## Formulas
1. **NPV = Σ (t=0..T) CF_t / (1+r)^t** (Equation 1, p.148). CF_t after-tax cash flow, outflows negative; r required return per period; t in periods (may be fractional). Spreadsheet: NPV(rate, values) treats the first value as t=1, so add the t=0 flow outside; XNPV(rate, values, dates) handles uneven dates (p.148, p.150).
2. **IRR: Σ (t=0..T) CF_t / (1+IRR)^t = 0** (Equation 2, p.150). Solved numerically; assumes reinvestment at IRR; unreliable with multiple sign changes. IRR(values, guess) includes t=0 and assumes even spacing; XIRR(values, dates, guess) uses dates (p.151–152).
3. **ROIC = (1 − tax rate) × operating profit_t / average (LT liabilities + equity) over t−1 and t** (Equation 3, p.153). Annual; working capital and short-term liabilities excluded.
4. **ROIC = (after-tax operating profit / sales) × (sales / average invested capital)** (p.154). Margin × turnover; sales cancel. A high-margin, slow-turnover firm and a low-margin, fast-turnover firm can match.
5. **After-tax cash flow_t = gross cash flow_t × (1 − tax rate)** (p.163, used when depreciation is stated as non-deductible). The t=0 outlay is not taxed this way; if depreciation is deductible add tax rate × depreciation as a shield.
6. **Project NPV = NPV without options − option cost + option value** (Equation 4, p.164). All terms at t=0.
7. **Decision-tree NPV** (p.165–166): discount each period's probability-weighted expected cash flow; a conditional-branch probability is the product of branch probabilities along the path.

## Traps
- Treating positive NPV as "always accept"; it is necessary, not sufficient (p.149).
- Choosing the higher IRR between mutually exclusive projects; use higher NPV (p.153).
- Reading IRR as the realised return; true only if reinvestment is at the IRR (p.151).
- Trusting one software IRR when signs change more than once; use NPV (p.152).
- Spreadsheet NPV() including t=0; it does not. IRR() does (p.148, p.151).
- Counting depreciation as cash, or including sunk costs; include cannibalized sales, exclude money already spent (p.159).
- Treating retained earnings as free, or using only the equity return as ROIC's benchmark (p.159, p.154).
- Using a total-asset or working-capital-inclusive denominator for ROIC, or one year-end instead of the average (p.153).
- Mixing real flows with nominal rates, or assuming real discounting protects against unexpected inflation (p.160).
- Believing real options hedge price risk; only financial derivatives do (p.166).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
A. Invest 100 at t=0, receive 40 at t=1, 2, 3. At r = 10%, PV = 99.47, NPV = −0.53, reject. At r = 8%, PV = 103.08, NPV = +3.08, accept. IRR is about 9.70%, so the decision flips when r crosses it.
B. Firm X: after-tax operating profit 120, sales 1,500, average invested capital 1,000. Margin 8%, turnover 1.5, ROIC 12%. Firm Y: sales 3,000, same profit and capital: margin 4%, turnover 3.0, ROIC 12%. If Y's turnover falls to 2.5 at the same margin, ROIC = 4% × 2.5 = 10%.
C. Base NPV without options −10, abandonment option costs 2, option value 15: Project NPV = −10 − 2 + 15 = +3, accept. If option value is 8, NPV = −4, reject.
