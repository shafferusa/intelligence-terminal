## Core principles
- **Judge assets by what they add to the portfolio.** Ask how a new holding moves relative to what you already own, not how risky it is alone (pp.122, 130). Portfolio expected return is the weighted average of the parts; risk is not, because holdings do not move in lockstep (p.126).
- **Concentration doubles up exposures.** An employee whose pay and savings both depend on one firm holds the same risk twice (pp.122–124).
- **Diversification reduces volatility, not necessarily losses.** In a crisis co-movement rises, so the benefit shrinks when wanted. It is risk reduction, not risk elimination or downside protection (pp.127–130).
- **MPT.** Markowitz: focus on how securities relate to each other. Later work (Sharpe, Lintner, Treynor): only systematic (non-diversifiable) risk earns a premium, the base of CAPM (p.130).
- **The process is a loop.** Planning (needs, objectives, constraints, written IPS, possibly a benchmark), execution (asset allocation, security analysis, portfolio construction and trading), feedback (monitor, rebalance, evaluate against objectives and benchmark), which may change the IPS (pp.131–134). Asset allocation is commonly seen as the most influential decision (pp.132–133).
- **Investor needs follow liabilities.** DC: the employee bears investment and inflation risk. DB: the sponsor promises the benefit and bears funding risk (pp.135–136). Banks and insurers owe cash on demand or on claims: high liquidity need, low risk tolerance. Endowments and foundations are perpetual with spending rules: long horizon, high risk tolerance (pp.136–140). Mature DB plans need income; growing ones do not.
- **Fees explain industry economics.** Passive earns a revenue share well below its asset share because fees are low; alternative managers (management plus performance fees) hold few assets but earn a large revenue share (pp.142–143).
- **Pooling.** Open-end fund investors buy and redeem at NAV with the fund, so others' trades do not change anyone's per-share value; closed-end funds and ETFs trade between investors on an exchange and can sit at a premium or discount to NAV (pp.146–147, 150).

## Vocabulary
- Portfolio approach — judging each asset by its contribution to total portfolio risk and return (p.122).
- Diversification ratio — portfolio SD over average single-asset SD; lower means more benefit (p.126, PDF 136).
- Systematic risk — the non-diversifiable part, the part rewarded with a premium (p.130).
- Investment policy statement (IPS) — written record of client objectives, constraints, benchmark (p.131).
- Asset allocation — weights across asset classes (p.132). Top-down / bottom-up — macro-first vs company-first (p.132).
- Rebalancing — restoring target weights after drift (p.134).
- DC plan — contributions defined; employee carries investment risk (p.135). DB plan — benefit defined; sponsor carries funding risk (p.136).
- Endowment / foundation — long-lived pools funding non-profit operations / grants (p.137).
- General account / surplus account — insurer's conservative premium pool / higher-risk residual (p.139). Sovereign wealth fund — state-owned pool (p.139).
- Buy-side / sell-side — asset managers vs broker-dealers (p.141).
- NAV — fund assets less liabilities, per share, set daily from closing prices (pp.146–147). Open-end / closed-end; load / no-load (pp.146–148).
- SMA, ETF, hedge fund, high-water mark, GP/LP, carried interest (pp.150–152).

## Formulas
1. **Diversification ratio = SD(equal-weighted portfolio) / SD(a randomly selected single asset, i.e., the average single-asset SD).** SDs in % per year. Assumes comparable mean returns; simple and sample-dependent; equal weights are not optimal. p.126 (PDF 136).
2. **NAV per share = (fund assets − liabilities) / shares outstanding; new shares issued = money invested / NAV per share.** "Minus liabilities" is the standard definition. pp.146–147 (PDF 156–157).
3. **Annualizing quarterly data (footnote 6, p.125):** annualized mean = quarterly mean × 4; annualized SD = quarterly SD × 2 (square root of 4, valid for independent periods).

## Traps
- Diversification eliminates risk or guarantees downside protection. It reduces volatility; correlations rise in crises.
- Best past performer assumed to win again; averaging SDs for the portfolio.
- Putting asset allocation in planning. Planning produces the IPS; allocation is in execution.
- Passive funds earn little revenue because they are small. It is low fees, not small size.
- ETFs trade intraday at a market price that can differ from NAV; open-end funds deal once a day at NAV.
- "No-load" is not "no fees"; money market funds are not bank-insured.

## Original practice seeds
(All HYPOTHETICAL.)
1. Two stocks, equal weights, each expected return 10% and SD 20%, correlation 0.5. Variance = 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.03, SD = 17.3%, return 10%, ratio = 17.3/20 = 86.6%. With correlation 0.9: variance 0.038, SD 19.5%, ratio 97.5%. With correlation 0: variance 0.02, SD 14.1%, ratio 70.7%. (The correlation formula is from a later reading; used only to illustrate.)
2. A fund has NAV of 50m and 2,000,000 shares, so NAV/share = 25. An investor adds 1.0m: 40,000 new shares; 2,040,000 shares; NAV/share still 25. If assets then fall to 45.9m, NAV/share = 22.50 (down 10%). Redeeming 10,000 shares pays 225,000.
3. A 200,000 account earns 5% before fees; adviser fee 1.00% vs 0.50%. Net 4% for 10 years: 200,000 × 1.04^10 = 296,049. Net 4.5%: 200,000 × 1.045^10 = 310,594. Lower fee adds about 14,500.
