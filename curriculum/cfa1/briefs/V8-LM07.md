## Learning outcomes

1. Describe how distributed ledger technology is used in finance. Taught in Distributed Ledger Technology (pp.183–189).
2. Explain what makes digital assets distinctive as investments and contrast them with other asset classes. Taught in Digital Asset Investment Features (pp.190–196).
3. Describe the forms and vehicles for investing in digital assets, direct and indirect. Taught in Digital Asset Investment Forms (pp.197–204).
4. Analyze the sources of risk, return and diversification in digital asset investments. Taught in Digital Asset Investment Risk, Return, and Diversification (pp.205–208).

## Core principles

- **Shared ledger replaces the central record keeper.** Every node holds a matching copy. A new entry is validated, then nodes agree through a consensus mechanism. Entries are grouped into blocks, each linked by hash to the previous one, so rewriting history is very hard. Gains: accuracy, transparency, faster ownership transfer, streamlined post-trade work. Limits: heavy energy use, and privacy or data breaches remain possible (pp.183–185).
- **Smart contracts automate agreed terms.** Code executes pre-agreed conditions, such as a derivative payoff or a collateral transfer on default. The risk is rigid code with possibly flawed logic (p.184).
- **PoW versus PoS: who pays to be trusted.** In PoW, miners spend computing power and energy in a costly lottery; the winner adds the block and earns new coins. Rewriting history needs more than half of network compute, which is ruinously expensive on a large chain, and only the period of control can be altered. In PoS, validators stake capital and propose blocks, and other staked validators attest. Security rests on honest stakers holding the majority. Both pay validators in new assets (pp.185–186).
- **Permissionless means open and slow; permissioned means gated and fast.** Permissionless: fully decentralized, unlimited membership, governed by members, costlier, less scalable. Permissioned: tiered access, a controlling organization, partial decentralization, faster and cheaper (pp.186–187).
- **No cash flows, so price rests on perceived scarcity and future ability to move value.** A return needs a buyer at a higher price, which explains volatility and missing valuation consensus. Four contrasts with traditional assets: inherent value, validation, medium of exchange, legal protection (pp.191–192).
- **Direct holding means you carry operational risk.** Holdings sit in a wallet controlled by keys; a lost passkey means lost assets. Other risks: exchange fraud, theft, phishing, whales in thin markets. Centralized exchanges face hack and outage risk; decentralized exchanges are hard to attack but hard to regulate (pp.197–200).
- **Indirect vehicles trade wallet risk for other risks.** A coin trust holds coins but can trade at a premium or discount to NAV and charges fees. Futures are typically cash settled and inherently leveraged. A crypto ETF in the book's term sheet holds cash and derivatives, not coins, charges an expense ratio, and is non-diversified (pp.201–203).
- **Diversification is historical, not guaranteed.** Return drivers differ from those of equities and bonds, so correlations were low, but they have risen, especially in stressed markets (pp.206–208).

## Vocabulary

- Digital asset — electronic-only asset with ownership or use rights, secured by cryptography (p.179).
- Distributed ledger / DLT — shared database, matching copy at each node (p.183).
- Consensus mechanism — how nodes agree on ledger state and the next block (pp.183, 185).
- Blockchain / block / hash — blocks chained by hashes (pp.184–185).
- Smart contract — self-executing code with pre-agreed terms (p.184).
- Proof of work / miner (p.185); proof of stake / validator (p.186).
- 51% attack — majority of compute used to rewrite recent history (p.186).
- Permissioned / permissionless network (p.186).
- Cryptocurrency / altcoin — privately issued coin on its own ledger; altcoin is any besides Bitcoin (pp.188, 193).
- Stablecoin — aims at steady value via a reserve basket or algorithmic supply control (p.194).
- CBDC — central bank's tokenized fiat (p.188).
- Token / tokenization; NFT, security, utility, governance tokens (pp.188–189).
- ICO — unregulated token sale, fraud-prone (p.189).
- Wallet / passkey (pp.197, 200); pump and dump, Ponzi scheme, whale (pp.198–200).
- Coin trust / crypto ETF / crypto futures (pp.201–202).
- Asset-backed token / dApp / DeFi (p.203).

## Formulas

None defined in this module. The module reports mean, standard deviation and coefficient of variation (CV = SD / mean; risk per unit of return; a standard Quant measure, not stated as a formula here, p.206 / PDF 214). A lower CV means less dispersion per unit of average return. Printed CVs do not all recompute from rounded inputs, so do not rely on recomputation.

## Traps

- Coins have their own blockchain; tokens ride on an existing one.
- A supply cap does not stabilize the price; missing valuation consensus drives volatility.
- Stablecoins are not risk-free: opaque reserves and algorithmic pegs have failed.
- PoW and PoS both reward validators with new assets. Do not say only PoW pays.
- Decentralized exchanges resist hacking but are lightly regulated.
- A crypto ETF in the term sheet holds futures and cash; a coin trust holds coins but can trade away from NAV.
- Direct: tokens, NFTs, exchange-traded tokens. Indirect: trusts, futures, ETFs, hedge funds, crypto stocks (p.204).
- Low correlation is a historical observation that rose under stress. High mean return with high SD is no bargain without the CV.

## Original practice seeds

All three are hypothetical.

1. **Coin trust premium.** A trust holds 1,000 coins at USD 50 and has 10,000 shares, so NAV per share is USD 5.00. At USD 5.40 the premium is 5.40/5.00 − 1 = 8.0%. At a 6% discount with unchanged coin price, the share price is 5.00 × 0.94 = USD 4.70.
2. **Fee drag.** An ETF with 1.50% annual expenses on USD 100,000 costs USD 1,500 a year. With a 10% gross return the net is about 8.5%. With 0% gross the investor still loses USD 1,500.
3. **Correlation matters.** A portfolio is 95% equities (SD 15%) and 5% crypto (SD 60%). At correlation 0.2, variance = 0.020306 + 0.0009 + 0.001710 = 0.022916, so SD = 15.14%. At correlation 0.8, the cross term is 0.006840, variance = 0.028046, SD = 16.75%. Same weights, risk up about 1.6 points.

## Book slips and uncertainties

- Exhibit 9's title and its note disagree on the end date of the sample period. Treat the dates as unreliable.
- Printed CVs in Exhibit 9 do not all recompute from the rounded inputs (the bond one is clearly off). Use it for ranking, not arithmetic.
- Stablecoins: p.193 says they cannot be exchanged for fiat, while the Terra case (p.194) says reserve-backed coins are redeemable from issuers. Trust the nuance: reserve-backed designs promise redemption without legal backing; algorithmic ones have no reserve.
- The book lists ICO under security tokens, yet the module's exercises file it under tokens generally.
- One solution point (Bitcoin return skew) is not stated in the reading text. Learn it as tested.
