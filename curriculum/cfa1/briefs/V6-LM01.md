## Core principles
- A bond is a standardized, tradable loan. The issuer receives principal at the start, pays coupons periodically, and returns principal (plus the last coupon) at maturity. Private loans are bilateral; bonds are standardized so they trade (pp.3–7). Features define who pays whom and when, so the first analyst task is to model the promised cash flows.
- Coupon depends on par, not price. Periodic coupon = annual rate x par, divided by payments per year. Only the price changes what you earn per unit invested (pp.7, 9–10). It fails if you mix a quoted annual rate with a per-period payment.
- Floating-rate notes reset with a market reference rate; the issuer spread is fixed at issuance. The investor's cash flow moves with the reference rate, not with the issuer's later credit changes. The reference rate can be negative (p.9). Credit risk stays with the investor; only the rate-reset part of the coupon floats.
- Zero-coupon bonds still pay interest, but all of it arrives at maturity as the gap between purchase price and par (p.9). The interest is earned only if the issuer survives to maturity.
- Price and yield move in opposite directions. A higher price gives a lower current yield and a lower YTM, so a premium bond yields below its coupon rate (pp.4, 11). Credit risk (non-payment) is the main risk, so lower credit quality demands higher yield (p.4).
- Repayment source and ranking drive credit risk. Sovereigns repay from taxation, so usually carry the lowest credit risk in their own region (pp.5, 8, 14). Unsecured corporates rely on operating cash flow; secured ones add pledged collateral as a backstop. ABS repay from pooled asset cash flows split into tranches in priority order (pp.14–15). In bankruptcy senior ranks before junior (p.10). Weakness: inflation still erodes sovereign nominal payments (p.5).
- Bondholders have no votes, so covenants are their protection. Affirmative covenants say what the issuer must do (usually cheap, administrative); negative covenants say what it must not do (limit debt, dividends, liens, asset sales) (pp.16–18). Violation can bring changed terms, acceleration or termination (pp.18–19). Trade-off: protection lowers borrowing cost but cuts flexibility, and over-tight covenants can force an avoidable default (p.18).

## Vocabulary
- Par / face / principal — amount repaid at maturity and the base for coupons (pp.7, 9).
- Tenor — remaining time to maturity (p.8).
- Money market vs capital market — original maturity up to one year vs over one year (p.8).
- Perpetual bond — no maturity, contractual cash flows, no votes, ranks above equity (p.8).
- Floating-rate note (FRN) — coupon = market reference rate + credit spread (p.9).
- Credit spread — issuer-specific add-on in bps (p.9); also the yield gap over a comparable sovereign (p.12).
- Senior / junior (subordinated) — repayment priority in bankruptcy (p.10).
- Embedded option — call, put or conversion right that cannot trade separately (p.10).
- Current yield — annual coupon divided by price (p.11).
- Yield to maturity — IRR on price and promised cash flows (p.11).
- Yield curve — YTMs against maturity for one issuer's comparable bonds (p.11).
- Indenture — the bond contract (p.14).
- Pari passu — equal ranking with similar debt (p.16).
- Cross-default — default on other debt triggers default on this bond (p.16).
- Incurrence test — ratio hurdle that must be met before certain actions (p.17).
- Negative pledge — bars pledging assets to new creditors (p.18).

## Formulas
1. Coupon per period = (coupon rate x par) / m. m = payments per year; currency units; fixed-rate, level coupon. Intuition: annual interest split across the year (pp.10–11).
2. FRN coupon rate = MRR + credit spread; periodic payment = par x FRN coupon / m. Spread in bps (100 bps = 1%); spread fixed; MRR may be negative. Intuition: the reference rate floats, the issuer's credit premium does not (pp.9, 13).
3. Current yield = annual coupon / price. Price per 100 of par; ignores principal gain or loss and time value. Intuition: like a dividend yield (p.11).
4. YTM: price = sum over t=1..N of CF_t / (1+r)^t, with the final CF including principal. r is per period; annualize by multiplying by periods per year (simple doubling for semiannual, not compounding). Assumes no default, hold to maturity, reinvestment at YTM. Intuition: the discount rate that equates price to promised cash flows. The module sets it up but does not solve it (p.11).
5. Credit spread = issuer YTM − comparable sovereign YTM, in bps; compare the same maturity (p.12).

## Traps
- Computing the coupon from price instead of par, or forgetting to divide by frequency (pp.7, 9).
- Thinking the FRN spread moves with later credit changes. Only the MRR resets (p.9).
- Final-period payment: one period's coupon plus principal, not a full year's coupon (pp.10–11).
- Treating YTM as the realized return without the three assumptions (p.11).
- Calling a perpetual equity. It has contractual cash flows, no votes, and ranks above equity (p.8).
- Classifying cross-default as negative. The book treats it as affirmative (pp.16, 18).
- Confusing secured bonds with asset-backed securities. ABS come from special purpose entities and repay from pooled asset cash flows (pp.14–15).
- Misreading pari passu as ranking above or below similar debt; it means equal ranking (p.16).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
- A. EUR1,000 par, 4% fixed, semiannual. Coupon = 20 per payment (40 per year). Current yield at price 980 is 40/980 = 4.082%; at 1,020 it is 40/1,020 = 3.922%. Price up, yield down.
- B. USD50m FRN, quarterly, coupon = MRR + 80 bps. At MRR 3.00%: coupon 3.80%, payment = 50m x 0.038 / 4 = 475,000. If MRR resets to 3.40%: 4.20%, payment 525,000, so +50,000 per quarter. If the issuer's credit worsens later, the spread stays at 80 bps.
- C. Maintenance: net debt/EBITDA <= 6.00x and coverage > 2.00x. Incurrence for a dividend: <= 4.00x and coverage > 3.50x. A firm at 3.6x and 3.8x passes both. A firm at 4.4x and 3.8x passes maintenance but fails incurrence, so no dividend.
