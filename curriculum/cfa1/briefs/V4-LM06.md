## Learning outcomes
1. Describe measuring inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV) and the effect on statements and ratios. Inventory Valuation, p.175–181 (PDF 187–193); Introduction p.174.
2. Calculate and explain how rising or falling inventory costs affect statements and ratios under different cost-flow methods. Inflation and Deflation, p.182–183 (PDF 194–195); reinforced by Inventory Ratios (method-switch example, p.190–191 / PDF 202–203) and practice.
3. Describe inventory presentation and disclosure and what an analyst should weigh in disclosures and other sources. Presentation and Disclosure and Inventory Ratios, p.184–191 (PDF 196–203).

## Core principles
- **Cost flow splits one pot.** Goods available for sale divide between cost of sales and ending inventory; the cost formula decides the split (p.174, p.182). Only reported profit, and so tax, changes.
- **Rising costs: FIFO vs LIFO.** FIFO gives lower cost of sales, higher gross profit, inventory, current ratio and taxable income than LIFO or weighted average, and lower turnover. Falling costs reverse this (p.182–183).- **LIFO is half-stale.** FIFO inventory is near replacement cost; LIFO cost of sales is near current cost, but LIFO inventory sits at old prices (p.182–183).- **LIFO liquidation.** Selling more than is bought while costs have been rising pushes old low-cost layers into cost of sales, inflating gross profit with a non-recurring gain (p.183). A fall in the LIFO reserve is the clue (solutions, p.205–207).
- **Write-downs are non-cash and early.** When NRV (expected selling price less costs to complete and sell) falls below carrying amount, inventory is written down and expensed (p.175). Profitability, liquidity and solvency ratios worsen; activity ratios look better. Managers may delay write-downs, mainly under US GAAP. Risk is highest with fast obsolescence or ratio-based debt covenants (p.175–181).
- **IFRS reverses, US GAAP does not.** Under IFRS a later NRV recovery must be reversed, capped at the original write-down, credited to cost of sales; US GAAP prohibits reversal (p.175–176).
- **Turnover needs a story.** High turnover may mean efficiency, too little stock or write-downs; low turnover may mean slow or obsolete stock. Compare with industry and sales growth (p.185). Gross margins tend to be higher for luxury goods and in less competitive industries (p.185).
- **Indirect effects.** Method changes also move current ratio, ROA and debt-to-equity (via retained earnings) (p.184, p.190–191).

## Vocabulary
- Cost formula — rule assigning costs to cost of sales vs ending inventory: FIFO, LIFO, weighted average, specific identification (p.174).
- Net realisable value (NRV) — expected selling price minus costs to complete and sell (p.175).
- Write-down / reversal — expensed cut to carrying amount; IFRS-only recovery, capped at the original write-down (p.175).
- Market value (US GAAP, LIFO and retail only) — replacement cost, capped at NRV, floored at NRV minus a normal margin (p.175).
- Valuation allowance — contra account netted against inventory (footnote 2, p.175).
- Carried at NRV — exception for agricultural, forest and mineral producers and commodity broker-traders; inventory can exceed cost (p.175).
- LIFO liquidation — sales exceed purchases, so old layers are expensed (p.183).
- LIFO reserve — FIFO inventory minus LIFO inventory; used only in the solutions (p.205–207).
- Perpetual vs periodic — records updated per transaction vs at period end; matters for LIFO (p.205).

## Formulas
1. Inventory turnover = Cost of sales / Average inventory; average = (beginning + ending) / 2. Text p.185 (PDF 197). One worked example divides by ending inventory (p.184 / PDF 196); check what the question asks.
2. Days of inventory on hand = Days in period / Inventory turnover (365 used). Days may be based on ending inventory (p.185).
3. Gross profit margin = Gross profit / Net sales (p.181, p.190).
4. Net profit margin = Profit / Net sales (p.181).
5. Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities; Debt-to-equity = Total debt / Total shareholders' equity; ROA = Net income / Average total assets (p.190 / PDF 202).
6. Gross (historical cost) inventory = Net inventory + valuation allowance (p.186).
7. IFRS reversal = lesser of the NRV recovery and the original write-down, on units still held (solutions, p.205).
8. FIFO inventory = LIFO inventory + LIFO reserve. Adjusted cost of sales (FIFO) = LIFO cost of sales − write-down charges in cost of sales − change in LIFO reserve (a fall in the reserve raises FIFO cost of sales). Adjusted net income = net income + write-down charges × (1 − tax rate). Adjusted turnover uses average FIFO inventory (solutions, p.205–206). Assumes a stable tax rate.

## Traps
- "High turnover is always good": it can come from write-downs or under-stocking (p.185).
- "Write-downs hurt every ratio": activity ratios improve (p.175).
- "LIFO is more efficient because turnover is higher": a method artefact (p.184).
- "FIFO means more cash": in rising prices FIFO shows higher profit and taxes (p.205); write-downs are non-cash (p.204).
- LIFO-to-FIFO: start from gross inventory plus the reserve, not net; mind the sign of the reserve change (p.205–206).
- Perpetual vs periodic changes inventory values only under LIFO (p.205).

## Original practice seeds
1. (Hypothetical, USD.) 100 units at cost USD 10; year-end NRV USD 8, so write-down = 1,000 − 800 = USD 200. Next year NRV is USD 12, units unsold. IFRS: reversal capped at 200, carrying back to 1,000. US GAAP: stays 800. If NRV recovers only to USD 9, IFRS reversal = 100 × (9 − 8) = USD 100.
2. (Hypothetical, USD.) Opening 10 units at USD 5; buy 10 at USD 7; sell 10 at USD 12 (revenue 120). FIFO: cost of sales 50, gross profit 70 (58.3%), inventory 70. LIFO: cost of sales 70, profit 50 (41.7%), inventory 50. At a USD 9 purchase price: FIFO profit still 70, inventory 90; LIFO cost of sales 90, profit 30 (25.0%), inventory 50.
3. (Hypothetical, USD thousand, 365 days.) Cost of sales 600, average inventory 100: turnover 6.0, days 60.8. A write-down of 20 lifts cost of sales to 620; average inventory 90 gives turnover 6.89, days 53.0. The ratio improves although profit fell by 20.
