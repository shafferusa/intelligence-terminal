1. Describe the benefits and costs of international trade. Benefits and Costs of Trade (printed 195–197; overview pp.193–194).
2. Compare tariffs, quotas, export subsidies (and VERs) and their economic effects. Trade Restrictions (printed pp.197–202).
3. Explain why countries form trading blocs, common markets and economic unions, and the advantages. Trading Blocs and Regional Integration (printed pp.203–208).

## Core principles
- **Trade raises the total pie but not everyone's slice (p.195).** Each country sells what it makes relatively cheaply at a better price abroad and buys the rest cheaper. Resources move to export sectors and out of import-competing ones. Gains are aggregate: winners could compensate losers, but usually nobody pays. Fails for workers and firms in contracting industries.
- **Sources of gain (p.195).** Old: comparative advantage from technology (Ricardian) or factor endowments (Heckscher-Ohlin). New: scale economies, variety and intra-industry trade (monopolistic competition: differentiated products, free entry, zero long-run profit), competition eroding monopoly power, learning by doing, knowledge spillovers.
- **Costs are adjustment costs (pp.195–197).** Import-competing firms shrink or exit, and workers with industry-specific skills face unemployment and retraining, some permanently worse off.
- **Tariff (pp.198–199).** A small country (a price taker, not a matter of size) cannot move the world price, so a per-unit tariff lifts the domestic price one-for-one. Domestic producers gain A, consumers lose A+B+C+D, government collects C on the imports that remain, and the net loss is B+D (inefficient extra production plus lost consumption). Revenue C is a transfer inside the country, not a loss. A large country can gain through a better terms of trade (the exporter cuts its price), but only if the partner does not retaliate and the terms-of-trade gain exceeds the deadweight loss. World welfare still falls.
- **Quota and VER (pp.200–201).** A quota caps import quantity through licenses. Same price and quantity effect as the equivalent tariff, but area C becomes quota rent, and who gets it matters. Government auctions the licenses: loss B+D, as with a tariff. Foreigners hold the licenses: loss B+C+D. A VER is the exporter's own cap, so the exporter keeps the rent and the importer loses B+C+D. A local importer may share quota rent under an import quota but not under a VER.
- **Export subsidy (pp.201–202).** The government pays per unit exported. Exporters shift sales abroad and the domestic price rises by the subsidy. The government's budget falls, since it pays out. In the large-country case the world price falls, so foreign buyers capture part of the subsidy and the welfare loss is bigger. Importers may respond with countervailing duties. Exhibit 3 (pp.201–202): producers gain and consumers lose under all four policies.
- **Why restrict (pp.197–198).** Motives: infant industries, jobs, security, tariff revenue (mostly developing countries), retaliation.
- **Blocs and static effects (pp.203–208).** Blocs beat global talks for speed and ease. Trade creation: cheaper member imports replace costlier domestic output. Trade diversion: costlier member imports replace cheaper non-member imports that are still tariffed. Net welfare is positive if creation outweighs diversion. Costs: adjustment, sovereignty, and no currency adjustment in a monetary union, so problems spread.

## Vocabulary
- Intra-industry trade — a country both exports and imports goods in one category (p.195).
- Tariff and quota — a tax on imports; a cap on imported quantity (pp.197, 200).
- Quota rent — extra profit earned because supply is limited and price higher (p.200).
- VER — exporter-imposed cap on exports (pp.197, 201).
- Countervailing duty — importer's duty on subsidized imports (p.201).
- Small country — price taker in the world market; large country — can influence the world price (p.198).
- Deadweight loss — net national welfare loss, B+D for a tariff (p.199).
- Regional trading bloc / RTA — countries cutting barriers among themselves (p.203).
- Free trade area, customs union, common market, economic union, monetary union — the integration ladder (p.204).
- Trade creation / trade diversion — static effects of a bloc (p.205).

## Formulas
No formal numbered formulas. Relations used (areas confirmed on Exhibit 2, p.199; notation on p.198). Q1 free-trade supply, Q2 supply with tariff, Q3 demand with tariff, Q4 free-trade demand.
- Domestic price with tariff: P_t = P* + t (p.198). P* is the world price; t is the per-unit tariff. Small country, world price unaffected. A percentage (ad valorem) tariff must first be converted to a per-unit amount.
- Imports = quantity demanded − quantity supplied (p.198): Q4 − Q1 under free trade, Q3 − Q2 with the tariff.
- Consumer surplus change = −(A+B+C+D); producer surplus change = +A; revenue (or quota rent) C = t × (Q3 − Q2); national welfare = −(B+D) (p.199).
- Triangles with linear curves (derived shortcut, not a book formula, checked against Example 1, p.200): B = ½ × t × (Q2 − Q1); D = ½ × t × (Q4 − Q3).
- Quota: national welfare = −(B+C+D) if rent goes abroad, −(B+D) if the government auctions licenses (pp.200–201).
- Assumes partial equilibrium, linear curves, small country, no retaliation.

## Traps
- "Small country" means price taker, not small size (p.198).
- Deadweight loss of a tariff is B+D only; revenue C is a transfer, not a loss (p.199).
- A quota is not identical to a tariff: same price and quantity, but rent may leave the country, so the loss can be B+C+D (p.200).
- A VER is imposed by the exporter, who keeps the rent (p.201).
- Export subsidy: government revenue falls, national welfare falls, and in the large-country case foreign buyers capture part of it (pp.201–202).
- Customs union versus common market differs by factor (labor and capital) mobility; an FTA has no common external policy (p.204).
- Trade diversion arises because the lowest-cost non-member is still tariffed (p.205).

## Original practice seeds
**A. Small-country tariff (hypothetical).** World price 10; at 10, domestic supply 50 and demand 150 (imports 100). A tariff of 2 makes the price 12: supply 60, demand 130 (imports 70), all linear. Consumer loss = 2×130 + ½×2×20 = 280. Producer gain = 2×50 + ½×2×10 = 110. Revenue = 2×70 = 140. Deadweight loss = 10 + 20 = 30; check 280 − 110 − 140 = 30.

**B. Who gets the rent (hypothetical).** Same market, but a quota of 70 units gives price 12 and rent 2×70 = 140. If the government auctions the licenses, the national loss is 30. If foreign exporters keep the rent, it is 30 + 140 = 170.

**C. Creation versus diversion (hypothetical).** Importer M, domestic cost 14 per widget. Non-member Yon costs 8, member Zed costs 9; both face a 25% tariff before the union (landed 10 and 11.25), so M imports from Yon. After M and Zed form a customs union, Zed lands at 9 and Yon at 10, so M switches to Zed: diversion, resource cost rises 8 to 9, and M also loses 2 per widget of tariff revenue. If instead M made widgets at 14 behind prohibitive tariffs, buying from Zed at 9 is creation, saving 5.
