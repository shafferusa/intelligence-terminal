2. Explain private debt and its investment characteristics: categories, risk and return. Section 3, printed pp.79–83 (PDF 87–91).
3. Describe how private capital can diversify a portfolio: vintage years, position on the risk ladder, correlation with public indexes. Section 4, printed pp.84–87 (PDF 92–95).

## Core principles
- **LBOs run on leverage.** A buyout fund buys a company with mostly borrowed money; the debt becomes the target's own, secured on its assets and repaid from its cash flows, so the target's operating cash pays the lenders and the fund keeps the residual. Value comes from cash flow growth (mostly organic revenue growth, then cost cutting, then acquisitions). Fails when debt is scarce or costly or cash flow disappoints (pp.68–69, PDF 76–77).
- **Earlier stage means higher required return.** Venture capital targets seed to early growth (little or negative cash flow, very high business risk); PE targets growth through maturity and decline; growth is where they overlap. Investors in riskier stages are paid by equity upside, since no cash flow services debt (pp.69–71, PDF 77–79).
- **Each exit trades price, speed, control and cost.** A trade sale is fast, private and cash-in-hand, but few buyers and possible staff resistance. An IPO may fetch the best price but brings fees, disclosure, market risk and lock-up. A SPAC fixes valuation early but warrants and fees dilute. Recap, secondary sale and write-off complete the list; a recap returns cash but the sponsor keeps control (pp.73–77, PDF 81–85).
- **PE risk and return exceed listed equity.** Illiquidity, leverage and control stakes raise required return. Index data mislead: self-reporting, survivorship and backfill biases overstate return, and infrequent, lagged valuations understate volatility and correlation (pp.77–78, PDF 85–86).
- **Private debt pays more than public bonds because lenders accept illiquidity and take opportunistic or complex positions.** Coupons are usually floating (reference rate plus spread), loans are typically secured with covenants. Ranking sets risk: senior secured is steadier; mezzanine is junior and usually unsecured, so it demands higher pay, often with warrants or conversion rights. Fails on default or collateral decline (pp.79–83, PDF 87–91).
- **Venture debt avoids dilution but lenders face thin collateral**, so they often get equity-purchase rights as compensation. Distressed investors need skill with default probability, recovery and restructuring; DIP financing funds firms in bankruptcy (pp.79–82, PDF 87–90).
- **Vintage year is the year of a fund's first investment.** Funds live about 10–12 years: an investment period of about five years, then harvesting. Funds started at low valuations in recoveries tend to do better than those started near peaks, so an investor spreads commitments across vintages. This is the investor's policy, not the fund's (pp.84, 87, PDF 92, 95).
- **Diversification benefit is only moderate.** Correlations with public indexes are well above zero; the exhibit ranks the categories. Excess return is possible with skilled managers, paid for with leverage, market and liquidity risk (pp.86–87, PDF 94–95).

## Vocabulary
- Private capital — funding from outside public markets and traditional lenders, equity plus debt (p.68).
- Leveraged buyout (LBO) — acquisition financed mostly by debt that the target itself services (p.68).
- MBO / MBI — current managers join the buyer / managers are replaced by the buyer's team (p.69).
- Portfolio company — a company held by a PE fund (p.69).
- Co-investment — direct stake alongside the lead sponsor; fund-of-funds is the indirect route (p.69).
- Seed, early-stage, later-stage — VC financing steps by company maturity; pre-seed money comes from founders, family and angels (pp.70–71).
- Mezzanine-stage financing vs mezzanine financing — a timing label vs an equity-debt hybrid instrument (p.71).
- Convertible preferred — senior to common in liquidation, convertible into common; typical for VC (p.71).
- Growth (minority) equity — non-controlling stake in a mature company (p.72).
- PIPE — private placement of shares in an already-listed company at a fixed price, fast but dilutive (p.72).
- Trade sale, IPO, direct listing, SPAC — exit routes (pp.74–76); lock-up and stockholder overhang (p.76).
- Recapitalization, secondary sale, write-off — other exits (p.77).
- Vintage year, investment period, harvesting period (p.84).
- Survivorship bias, backfill bias, marking to market — index data problems (p.77).
- Direct lending, leveraged loan, venture debt, mezzanine debt, distressed debt, DIP financing, unitranche, specialty (litigation) loan — private debt types (pp.80–82).
- Loan-to-value (LTV) — loan outstanding over collateral value (p.81).
- Illiquidity premium — extra return for being unable to sell quickly (p.82).

## Formulas
The book has no formal equations; three arithmetic ideas appear.
- LTV = loan outstanding / collateral value. Unitless ratio or %. Assumes collateral valued at current market. Falls with amortisation, rises if collateral falls (p.81, PDF 89).
- Redemption cost of preferred = [redemption price (multiple of par) + dividend rate x years unpaid] x principal. Currency units. Assumes redemption at a set year, no dividends paid, ignores warrant dilution and time value. (p.73, PDF 81).
- Floating coupon = reference rate + spread (1bp = 0.01%; the conversion is standard, not stated in the book). Resets as the reference rate moves (p.82, PDF 90).

## Traps
- Mezzanine-stage financing (timing) is not mezzanine financing (instrument) (p.71).
- A recap is not a true exit: the PE firm keeps control (p.77).
- A SPAC's fixed valuation does not make it cheap; warrants and fees dilute (p.75).
- Low reported PE volatility reflects smoothing and lagged marks, not safer assets (p.77).
- Correlations to public indexes are not low; the benefit is moderate and differs by category (p.86).
- Vintage diversification is an investor policy across funds, and the advantage belongs to low-valuation starts (pp.68, 87).
- Match funding source to life-cycle stage (pp.70–71).
- Basis points are not percent; LTV moves with both amortisation and collateral value (pp.81–82).
- PIPE buyers get a discount, which hurts existing holders (p.72).

## Original practice seeds
All HYPOTHETICAL.
1. A firm issues USD10m of 8% preferred, redeemable at 102% in year 3, no dividends paid. Cost = 10 x (1.02 + 0.08 x 3) = USD12.6m. At 10%: 10 x (1.02 + 0.30) = USD13.2m, so +USD0.6m.
2. A USD60m loan on a USD100m property has a maximum LTV covenant of 0.60. After USD10m of amortisation the loan is USD50m; at USD100m value LTV = 0.50. If value falls to USD80m, LTV = 50/80 = 0.625, a breach. A paydown of 50 - 0.60 x 80 = USD2.0m restores 0.60; alternatively collateral worth 50/0.60 = USD83.3m is needed, about USD3.3m more.
3. A fund making its first investment in 2021 has vintage 2021; with a five-year investment period it invests about 2021–2025 and harvests until about 2031–2033 (10–12 year life). Committing USD3m to each of three vintages rather than USD9m to one reduces vintage-timing risk.
