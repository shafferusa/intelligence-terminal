## Core principles

- **Bank lines rank by how firmly the bank is bound.** An uncommitted line carries no written promise, so the bank can refuse a draw just when the borrower is weak. A committed line is a written promise, uses more bank capital and carries a commitment fee. A revolver is a multi-year committed facility with covenants and is the most dependable. Order: uncommitted < committed < revolver (pp.82–83, PDF 92–93).
- **Secured funding trades assets for access.** Weaker credits pledge collateral. With assignment of receivables the firm borrows against them and still collects. With factoring it sells them at a discount and the factor takes collection and credit risk (p.84).
- **Commercial paper is for strong names and is rolled over.** Investors fear the next sale fails, so a committed backup line is expected (p.84).
- **Banks fund from sources of different reliability.** Operational deposits are the stickiest. Interbank and central bank funds are short-term and rate-linked. The discount window is a last resort at a higher cost and more oversight. In ABCP a bank moves loans to a special purpose entity (SPE), which issues secured paper and gets a liquidity line from the bank. The danger is long assets funded by short paper that cannot be rolled (pp.85–87).
- **A repo is a collateralised loan written as a sale.** The seller (cash borrower) delivers a security and promises to buy back the same or a similar one at a fixed price on a fixed date, and keeps the economic benefits such as coupons. The buyer (cash lender) earns the repo rate and holds the collateral. Collateral worth more than the cash lent is the cushion. Weaker collateral, longer terms and undercollateralisation raise the rate. Strong demand for one specific security lowers its rate, even below zero (pp.88–91, 93–94).
- **Repos have three uses.** (1) Dealers finance bond inventory, funding only the cushion elsewhere. (2) Cash lenders earn short-term secured income (the reverse repo from their side). (3) Hedge funds borrow a bond to short it: take it under repo, sell it, buy it back to return it. The short seller profits only if the price fall exceeds the repo interest (pp.91–93).
- **Repo risk is mainly rollover and liquidity risk.** Repos are secure but mostly overnight and uncommitted, so counterparties can refuse to roll after bad news. Other risks: default, collateral, margining, legal, netting and settlement. Triparty repo uses an agent to value and hold collateral, which lowers cost but leaves credit risk where it was (pp.94–96).
- **IG and HY issuers are analysed differently.** IG yield is mostly the government benchmark, cash flows are bond-like, covenants are few, maturities run up to about 30 years and issues are standardised and unsecured. HY yield is mostly an issuer-specific spread, cash flows are more equity-like, so analysts weigh default probability and loss given default; covenants are tighter, maturities typically 10 years or less, and market access swings with the cycle (pp.98–103).
- **Callables and fallen angels.** HY issuers use prepayable leveraged loans and callable bonds to refinance cheaply if credit improves. Investor price gains are capped at the call price. A fallen angel has HY credit risk but IG-style bonds (long, few covenants, usually non-callable), and forced selling by IG-mandate holders hits a small HY market (p.101).

## Vocabulary

- Uncommitted line — bank limit the bank may refuse to honour; no commitment fee (p.83, PDF 93).
- Commercial paper (CP) — short-term unsecured notes of strong issuers; Eurocommercial paper is the smaller, less liquid version (p.84).
- ABCP and SPE — secured paper issued by an entity holding a bank's loans (p.86).
- Repo rate — interest earned by the cash lender (pp.88–89).
- General collateral vs special — a basket of acceptable securities vs one in-demand security (pp.89, 93).
- Initial margin and haircut — the cushion as collateral over cash lent, and as a fraction of collateral value (pp.89–90).
- Variation margin — collateral top-up or release as prices move (p.90).
- Reverse repo — the same trade from the cash lender's side (p.93).
- Fallen angel — issuer once IG, now HY (p.101).

## Formulas

1. Repurchase price = Purchase price × [1 + repo rate × days/360]. Purchase price is the cash lent; repo rate is annual; simple interest; the book's examples use 360. p.89 (PDF 99).
2. Initial margin = Security price₀ / Purchase price₀. 100% or more; above 100% means overcollateralised. p.89 (PDF 99). Rearranged: Purchase price₀ = Security price₀ / Initial margin.
3. Haircut = (Security price₀ − Purchase price₀) / Security price₀. Equals 1 − 1/initial margin, so linked but not equal. p.90 (PDF 100).
4. Variation margin = (Initial margin × Purchase price_t) − Security price_t. Purchase price_t is the loan plus accrued interest at t. Positive means a shortfall, so the borrower posts more; negative means excess, so it may reclaim. p.90 (PDF 100).

## Traps

- Thinking the cash lender gets the coupons. The seller keeps them (p.89).
- Treating haircut and initial margin as the same number. A 5% haircut is not a 105% margin; 1/(1 − haircut) matches it (pp.89–90).
- Dropping the day fraction or using the wrong term in the repurchase price. Interest runs on the cash lent, not collateral value.
- Reversing the variation margin sign. A price fall means the borrower posts more (p.90).
- Believing "secured" means safe. Repos carry default, margin, legal and rollover risk (pp.94–96).
- Trusting uncommitted lines in stress, or applying commitment fees to them (p.83).
- Assuming a good rating removes rollover risk. CP relies on a committed backup line (p.84).
- Saying the bank issues ABCP. The SPE does (p.86).
- Assuming popular collateral earns a higher repo rate. Scarcity lowers it (pp.93–94).
- Assuming callable debt helps investors. Their price upside is capped at the call price (p.101).

## Original practice seeds

All three are hypothetical.

**A. Repo cost.** A dealer sells a bond worth 50,000,000 under a repo with a 2% haircut for 10 days at a 1.20% repo rate, actual/360. Loan = 50,000,000 × 0.98 = 49,000,000. Interest = 49,000,000 × 0.012 × 10/360 = 16,333.33. Repurchase price = 49,016,333. Initial margin = 50,000,000/49,000,000 = 102.04%. At a 2.40% rate, interest doubles to 32,666.67.

**B. Margin call.** A repo has an initial margin of 105% and a loan of 20,000,000 (accrued interest ignored). Collateral falls from 21,000,000 to 20,400,000. Required = 1.05 × 20,000,000 = 21,000,000. Variation margin = 21,000,000 − 20,400,000 = +600,000, so the seller posts 600,000 more. At 21,300,000 the result is −300,000: reclaim 300,000.

**C. Short via repo.** A hedge fund takes 10,000,000 of a bond under repo at 0.80% for 15 days and sells it (margins and costs ignored). Repurchase price = 10,000,000 × (1 + 0.008 × 15/360) = 10,003,333. Buying back at 9,850,000 gives profit 153,333. Had the price risen to 10,100,000, the result is −96,667.
