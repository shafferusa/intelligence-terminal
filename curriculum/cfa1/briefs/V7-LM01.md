## Learning outcomes

1. Define a derivative and describe its basic features. Taught in Derivative Features (pp.5–7, PDF 13–15) and Derivative Underlyings (pp.8–13, PDF 16–21).
2. Describe basic features of derivative markets and contrast OTC with exchange-traded markets. Taught in Derivative Markets (pp.14–18, PDF 22–26).

## Core principles

- **A deal struck now for a payoff later.** Two counterparties fix terms today; what changes hands at maturity depends on an underlying. Someone exposed to a price can lock it in and pass the price risk to a party willing to hold it. Each side then carries counterparty credit risk: the other may fail to perform. Spot trading swaps the asset immediately (pp.3, 5–6).
- **It transforms exposure; it does not pass the underlying's returns through** (p.6; PDF 14). Ask: which exposure am I creating or removing?
- **Firm commitment vs contingent claim.** Forwards, futures and swaps bind both sides to exchange. An option is the main contingent claim: one party chooses whether and when to settle. A call or put right inside a bond is an embedded contingent claim; a separate contract is stand-alone (pp.6–7; PDF 14–15).
- **Reasons to use:** take a short position, diversify, hedge, get large exposure for a small cash outlay, and often lower costs and better liquidity than spot. The small outlay is why exposure can be big relative to cash put up (the book frames it as a benefit) (pp.4, 7).
- **Match the underlying to the exposure.** Underlyings include equities, bonds, interest rates (not an asset), currencies, commodities, credit and unusual ones; a contract may reference several. A borrower paying floating can swap to fixed: the swap's received floating rate cancels the loan's floating leg, leaving fixed plus the loan spread. In any hedge, name the risk factor (a rate, a currency, a price) as the underlying, not the goods or loan itself (pp.8–13).
- **OTC vs ETD.** OTC: customized, bilateral, dealers make markets and offset risk among themselves; confidential but less transparent, usually more counterparty risk, possibly less liquid. ETD: exchange sets size, quality, location and maturity; transparent, liquid, cheaper; collateral posted at the start and during the trade, with the exchange guaranteeing against default; flexibility is lost (pp.14–15; PDF 22–23).
- **Central clearing moves credit risk.** After 2008 most OTC derivatives had to be cleared centrally. A trade executed on a swap execution facility goes to the central counterparty, which novates it into two identical trades facing itself. Users keep customization and gain transparency and lower bilateral risk, but risk concentrates in the CCP, a systemic worry (p.16; PDF 24–25).

## Vocabulary

- Cash/spot market — immediate exchange at today's price (p.3).
- Derivative — contract whose value is driven by an underlying (p.5).
- Underlying — asset or variable, even a rate or index, driving value (p.5).
- Forward contract — agreement to trade at a fixed price on a future date (pp.5–6).
- Counterparty credit risk — risk the other party cannot perform (p.6).
- Notional / contract size — amount payoffs are computed on (p.6).
- Long / short — gains when the underlying rises / falls (p.6).
- Stand-alone vs embedded — separate contract vs one inside a security (p.6).
- Firm commitment — pre-determined exchange both must make (p.7).
- Contingent claim — one party decides whether or when to settle (p.7).
- Hedging — offsetting an existing or expected exposure (p.7).
- Warrant — company-issued option; shares come from the issuer (p.9).
- Market reference rate (MRR) — benchmark short rate such as SOFR, €STR, SONIA, CORRA; replaced Libor (pp.9, 11).
- Soft vs hard commodities — agricultural vs energy and metals (p.10).
- Credit default swap — contract on an issuer's or index's default risk (p.10).
- Dealer / market maker — intermediary quoting prices and laying off risk (p.14).
- Clearing vs settlement — verifying and recording trades vs final payment or delivery (p.15).
- Central counterparty (CCP), swap execution facility (SEF) — entity between the parties; multi-dealer swap platform (p.16).
- Novation — replacing the original trade with identical trades facing the CCP (p.16).

## Formulas

No formal formulas. One implicit relationship (pp.6, 8; PDF 14, 16): buyer's cash settlement per unit at maturity = S_T − F_0, where S_T is spot at maturity and F_0 the agreed forward price (currency per unit); multiply by contract size; the seller receives the opposite sign. Assumes cash settlement; ignores default, margin and discounting. Intuition: the buyer gains if the asset ends worth more than the agreed price. The book states it in words only.

## Traps

- Calling a derivative a pass-through of the underlying's returns (p.6).
- Reversing the forward settlement sign: the seller gains when spot ends below the forward price (p.6).
- Treating only options as contingent claims; an embedded bond call also is. Forwards, futures, swaps are firm commitments (pp.7–8).
- Calling an ETF purchase a derivative; it is spot (p.8).
- Thinking an underlying must be an asset; a rate counts (p.9).
- Confusing a warrant (company-issued) with an exchange option (p.9).
- Confusing clearing with settlement (p.15).
- Thinking a CCP eliminates credit risk; it concentrates it (p.16).
- Assuming a large exposure needs a similarly large cash outlay; it does not (pp.4, 7).

## Original practice seeds

1. **Forward settlement (hypothetical).** A farmer sells a 3-month cash-settled forward on 100 tonnes of wheat at 200 per tonne. Spot at maturity is 230: the farmer pays (230 − 200) × 100 = 3,000 on the forward and sells wheat for 23,000, net 20,000. If spot is 180: receives 2,000, sells for 18,000, net 20,000. The price effect is gone.
2. **Swap net cost (hypothetical, annual, ignoring day count).** Floating loan at MRR + 1.00%, swap pays fixed 3.00% and receives MRR. Net = (MRR + 1.00%) − MRR + 3.00% = 4.00%. If MRR moves from 2% to 5%, loan cost goes 3% to 6% but the swap receipt rises 3 points; net stays 4.00%.
3. **Novation (hypothetical).** Dealers A and B hold one swap (1 contract). After novation: 2 contracts, A–CCP and B–CCP. A now relies on the CCP, not on B.
