## Core principles

- **Primary issuance moves cash to the issuer only when new shares are sold.** An IPO, a SPAC merger, a direct listing and a back-door listing are different routes to public status. Underwritten IPOs use a prospectus and book building. A direct listing skips the underwriter and lets the market set the price. A SPAC is a cash shell that must find a private target within roughly two years or return the money. If existing owners are the sellers, the firm receives nothing (pp.48–51, PDF 58–61).
- **Dilution depends on who sells.** A follow-on of newly created shares is dilutive. A follow-on of existing holders' shares is non-dilutive and pays the sellers, not the firm. Offerings can mix both (p.51, PDF 61).
- **Underpricing is an incentive story.** A first-day jump above the offer price means the issuer collected less than the market later paid. Underwriters can favour institutional clients with allocations, who in turn pay commissions. Managers may care more about the first-day gain on their own holdings than about proceeds. Both are principal-agent problems (pp.49–50, PDF 59–60).
- **Secondary markets give liquidity, price discovery and feedback.** Prices guide management on timing and value share-based pay. They also supply return and risk data. Prices can drift from value. Share-based pay with new shares dilutes owners (pp.52–54, PDF 62–64).
- **Venues differ in who sets the price.** An exchange is order-driven: investors use member broker-dealers as agents, orders meet in one central matching system, and transparency is high. An OTC market is quote-driven: dealers post bid and ask quotes and fill from inventory. Execution is very likely because dealers must honour quotes, but transparency is lower, best price is not assured, and dealers may withdraw in stress (pp.56, 60–61, PDF 66, 70–71).
- **Trading cost is explicit plus implicit.** Explicit costs are commissions and fees. Implicit costs are the bid-ask spread, market impact, delay cost (slippage) and opportunity cost. Only the spread can be read from the order book. A large market order walks the book, buying at rising asks, so the average fill is worse than the best quote. Block trades (roughly 10,000 shares or more) and very small or illiquid trades go off-exchange to a broker-dealer who can slice or cross the order (pp.56–58, 60, PDF 66–68, 70).
- **Liquidity is cost, speed and size.** Turnover compares daily volume with the float. Depth is order size near the best prices. Breadth is how many orders sit across nearby prices. Resiliency is how fast price returns to its market-clearing level after a shock (pp.62–64, PDF 72–74).
- **An index is a rule, not a market.** It is a fixed list of securities with a weighting scheme. Cap weighting tilts toward large, previously risen stocks; equal weighting removes that tilt. Choose the index that matches the mandate as a benchmark (pp.65–69, PDF 75–79).

## Formulas

1. Bid-ask spread = lowest ask − highest bid (currency per share). p.56 (PDF 66).
2. Bid-ask spread (%) = (lowest ask − highest bid) / lowest ask. Divides by the ask, not the mid. p.56 (PDF 66).
3. Mid-market price = (lowest ask + highest bid) / 2. p.57 (PDF 67).
4. VWAP = Σ(Price_i × Volume_i) / Σ(Volume_i), i = 1..n trades. Large trades count more, so it is not a simple average. p.58 (PDF 68).
5. Market float = shares outstanding − restricted or otherwise unavailable shares. p.62 (PDF 72).
6. Average daily volume = Σ(shares traded on day i) / n. p.62 (PDF 72).
7. Turnover ratio = ADV / market float (per day). Averages hide spikes and ignore depth and spread. p.62 (PDF 72).

Also used, unnumbered: days to liquidate = position / (allowed fraction × ADV), rounded up to whole days; p.63 (PDF 73).

## Traps

- Percentage spread uses the ask in the denominator (p.56).
- Turnover uses float, not shares outstanding; shares outstanding divided by ADV gives a number of days (pp.47, 64).
- Only the spread is observable in the order book (pp.47, 60).
- Block trades are split or crossed off-exchange to reduce impact, not to shrink the spread (p.60).
- A follow-on is primary issuance, and dilutive, only when new shares are sold (p.51).
- Underpricing means a first-day jump relative to the offer price (p.49).
- The book is loose on direct listing: the text says existing shares are listed, but one solution calls it a way to issue shares. Treat it as a primary-market route with no underwriter (pp.48, 55).
- Resiliency is speed of price recovery, not avoidance of shocks (p.64). A composite index is defined by covering over 90% of market value (p.69).
- Compute days to liquidate and round up, not to nearest (p.63).

## Original practice seeds

All hypothetical.

1. **Spread and mid.** Best bid 49.95, best ask 50.05. Spread = 0.10. Spread % = 0.10 / 50.05 = 0.1998%. Mid = 50.00. If the ask widens to 50.15, spread = 0.20, spread % = 0.20 / 50.15 = 0.3988%, and mid = 50.05.
2. **Float, ADV, turnover, days.** 800m shares outstanding, 200m restricted, so float = 600m. Five days of volume in millions: 1.0, 1.2, 0.8, 1.1, 0.9. ADV = 5.0 / 5 = 1.0m. Turnover = 1.0 / 600 = 0.1667% per day. If restricted shares rose to 400m, float = 400m and turnover = 0.25%. A holder of 150,000 shares allowed 10% of ADV (100,000 per day) needs 1.5 days, so 2 trading days.
3. **VWAP.** Trades: 100 at 20.00, 400 at 20.50, 500 at 20.20. Numerator = 2,000 + 8,200 + 10,100 = 20,300. Volume = 1,000. VWAP = 20.30. The simple average of prices is 20.233. If the first trade were 1,000 shares at 20.00: (20,000 + 8,200 + 10,100) / 1,900 = 20.158, because the big low-priced trade pulls VWAP down.
