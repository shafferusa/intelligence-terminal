## Core principles

- **Exposure exists before any exchange happens.** Anyone who will pay or receive foreign currency later carries the risk that the future spot rate differs from today's; the forward rate is known today. pp.218–219 (PDF 226–227).
- **Instruments differ in who bears default and who pays when.** Spot settles about two business days out. An outright forward is over the counter, so counterparty risk is real. Futures are exchange-traded and marked to market daily, which lowers default risk. An FX swap is a spot leg plus an opposite forward leg. Options cost a premium. Liquidity falls with longer term and larger size. pp.219–222 (PDF 227–230).
- **Rolling a forward settles only the rate difference.** Offsetting the expiring forward with a spot trade nets the foreign-currency amounts to zero; what is paid is notional times the gap between the originally contracted forward rate and the roll-date spot, in the quote currency, to whoever gained. A new forward carries the exposure on. p.220 (PDF 228).
- **A weaker currency helps the trade balance only if quantities respond enough (elasticities view).** Depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad (volume up) and imports dearer at home (price up, volume down). The import bill falls only if import demand is elastic; if inelastic, the balance can worsen. pp.223–225 (PDF 231–233).
- **The J-curve is a timing risk.** Signed contracts, delivery lags and low short-run elasticities mean the balance can worsen first and improve later even when the long-run condition holds. pp.225–226 (PDF 233–234).
- **The trade balance is a saving-investment story (absorption view).** Balance equals income minus domestic spending, equivalently saving minus investment. Depreciation helps only if it raises income relative to spending. With spare capacity, demand switching lifts output and some extra income is saved. At full employment, domestic prices climb and the gain fades unless spending falls. pp.226–227 (PDF 234–235).
- **Wealth effect: spending falls, but only for a while.** A weaker currency erodes what domestic-currency wealth buys; households save to rebuild it, then stop, so the improvement is likely temporary. Lasting change needs shifts in saving, investment or the fiscal balance. p.227 (PDF 235).
- **Trade and capital accounts move together.** A trade surplus is matched by a capital account deficit. The trade balance cannot shift alone; asset prices must change so foreign assets look more attractive, and domestic assets less, to both foreign and domestic investors. Capital controls force the trade balance to zero. pp.227–230 (PDF 235–238), p.232 (PDF 240).

## Vocabulary

- Exchange rate — price of one currency in terms of another (Summary, p.231). FX market — where currencies trade; largest by turnover (p.217).
- Spot — immediate delivery, usually settled two business days later (p.219). Outright forward — fixed rate agreed today for a later date (p.219).
- Futures — standardized, exchange-traded, daily margin (p.220). FX swap — spot plus opposite forward; not a currency swap (p.220).
- Swap funding — foreign borrowing plus an FX swap instead of domestic borrowing (p.221). FX option — right, not obligation, bought for a premium (p.221).
- Hedge / speculate — reduce / take on exchange-rate risk (p.219).
- Devaluation vs depreciation — official cut under a fixed rate vs market-driven fall under a floating one (p.223).
- Marshall–Lerner condition — elasticity combination under which depreciation improves the balance (p.223).
- Price elasticity of demand — %ΔQ per %ΔP, quoted positive; elastic above 1, inelastic below 1 (p.224).
- J-curve — balance worsens first, then improves, after depreciation (p.225).
- Elasticities vs absorption approach — micro relative-price view vs macro spending-versus-income view (p.226). Absorption — total domestic expenditure; expenditure switching — shifting demand between domestic and foreign goods (p.226).
- Wealth effect — lower real wealth leads to lower spending (p.227).

## Formulas

1. **Price elasticity:** ε = −%ΔQ / %ΔP. Unitless, positive by convention. p.224 (PDF 232).
2. **Expenditure change:** %ΔR = %ΔP + %ΔQ = (1 − ε) × %ΔP. R = expenditure (P × Q). Additive form is an approximation, accurate for small changes. A price rise lowers spending only if ε > 1. p.224 (PDF 232).
3. **Generalized Marshall–Lerner:** wX·εX + wM·(εM − 1) > 0. wX, wM = export and import shares of total trade (sum to 1); εX = foreign demand elasticity for exports; εM = domestic import demand elasticity. Assumes export prices fixed in domestic currency, import prices in foreign currency; ignores the J-curve. If true, depreciation moves the balance toward surplus. p.224 (PDF 232).
4. **Balanced-trade case:** εX + εM > 1 (wX = wM = 0.5). The classic Marshall–Lerner condition. With a deficit, wM > wX; εM > 1 alone guarantees improvement. p.224 (PDF 232).
5. **Trade balance identity:** X − M = (S − I) + (T − G). S = private saving, I = private investment, T = tax receipts, G = government spending. p.229 (PDF 237).
6. **Forward roll cash flow:** notional × (originally contracted forward rate − spot rate at roll date), in the quote currency; sign depends on whether the base currency was bought or sold forward. p.220 (PDF 228).
7. **Hedged foreign-bond return (domestic per foreign quote):** (1/spot) × (1 + r_foreign) × forward − 1. Stepwise in the book, not displayed. Assumes zero-coupon, no default, hedge matches the maturity. pp.221–223 (PDF 229–231).

## Traps

- Spot is not same-day; settlement is usually T+2 (p.219).
- Unhedged risk comes from the unknown future spot rate, not the known forward rate (p.222).
- Elasticity is positive by convention; a price rise cuts spending only if elasticity exceeds 1 (p.224).
- Export revenue in domestic currency rises through volume only; the import bill rises through price unless εM > 1; with a deficit, import elasticity weighs more (p.224).
- Depreciation does not guarantee a better balance: the condition can fail, or the J-curve can delay the gain (pp.225–226).
- An FX swap is not a currency swap; the roll payment is in the quote currency (p.220).
- The trade balance cannot move alone; at full employment the gain fades, and the wealth-effect gain is temporary (pp.227–230).

## Original practice seeds

All hypothetical.

1. **Hedge.** A UK firm will receive EUR 10,000,000 in 60 days. The 60-day forward is GBP 0.8500 per EUR, locking GBP 8,500,000. Unhedged, spot at 0.8200 gives GBP 8,200,000; at 0.8800, GBP 8,800,000. The forward removes a GBP 600,000 range.
2. **Marshall–Lerner.** Exports 300, imports 500 (domestic currency millions; prices fixed as in formula 3). wX = 0.375, wM = 0.625. With εX = 0.5, εM = 0.8: 0.1875 + 0.625 × (−0.2) = +0.0625, so depreciation improves the balance. If εM falls to 0.5: 0.1875 − 0.3125 = −0.125, so it worsens it.
3. **Expenditure.** A 5% import price rise with ε = 0.4: %ΔR ≈ +3% (exact: 1.05 × 0.98 = 1.029, about +2.9%). With ε = 1.5: ≈ −2.5% (exact: 1.05 × 0.925 = 0.971, about −2.9%), so spending falls.
