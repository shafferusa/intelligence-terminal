## Core principles
- **What backs repayment.** A sovereign can tax, so its repayment capacity is the economy's future tax take, plus fees and state-enterprise cash flows. Other public borrowers rely on local taxes, user fees, project income or sponsor support. Credit quality, and so how far out the maturity ladder the issuer can borrow, depends on how stable that source is; a narrow or cyclical base weakens it (pp.109–110, PDF 119–120; p.120, PDF 130).
- **Economic vs accounting view of a government balance sheet.** Public accounts are often cash-based and omit items such as unfunded pension accruals and depreciation. The economic view sets expected future taxes (a claim) against promised future spending (an obligation). Ignoring the unfunded promises overstates strength (p.110, PDF 120).
- **DM vs EM sovereigns.** DM issuers have diversified economies, a broad tax base, stable policy and often a reserve currency, so domestic-currency debt is close to default-free across all maturities. EM issuers are more cyclical and concentrated, lean on state enterprises, may have restricted currencies (limiting long local-currency debt) and often borrow externally. Default risk is real for EM external debt (pp.110–111, PDF 120–121).
- **Domestic vs external debt, and currency.** External debt is owed to foreign creditors, usually in foreign currency but sometimes in the issuer's own. A foreign-currency lender avoids direct currency risk, yet bears indirect risk because the issuer must earn that currency to pay. If the issuer's currency falls or reserves shrink, debt service gets harder (p.111, PDF 121).
- **Level vs composition.** Fiscal policy (spending plus debt service vs revenue) sets how much debt exists; debt management sets its mix (bills, notes and bonds; fixed, floating, inflation-linked, foreign currency). Bills are 1–12 months, usually zero-coupon, sold at a discount (p.112, PDF 122).
- **Ricardian equivalence and rollover risk.** It parallels Modigliani–Miller. If households smooth consumption, expect tax cuts to be reversed, face perfect capital markets and care about heirs, then the debt-versus-tax choice and the maturity choice do not change the present value of taxes. Under that theory the shortest maturity is cheapest (no term premium). In practice the assumptions fail, and constant refinancing exposes the budget to rollover risk (uncertain future rates), so governments spread maturities and issue predictably (pp.112–113, PDF 122–123).
- **Why issue long-dated bonds.** They give risk-free benchmarks at each maturity (credit spread = issuer yield minus government yield), let investors hedge rate risk apart from credit risk, serve as repo and derivatives collateral, and support monetary operations and reserves. Bills carry a liquidity premium, so they yield less (p.113, PDF 123).
- **Auctions vs underwriting.** Sovereigns sell on a published schedule by auction, through primary dealers. Corporates time issues opportunistically through underwriters. Bids are ranked best price (lowest yield) first until filled. Single-price: all winners pay the stop-yield price. Multiple-price: winners pay what they bid. Non-competitive bidders name only a quantity and always get filled; a competitive bid at a worse yield than the stop yield gets nothing (pp.116–118, PDF 126–128).
- **Public-sector tiers below the sovereign.** Agencies, local governments and supranationals differ in repayment source and in how much state support stands behind them. Agency yields sit near the guarantor's but without its full liquidity premium. Supranationals rank highest in credit quality among these because member governments back them (pp.120–124, PDF 130–134).

## Vocabulary
- Economic balance sheet — financial assets and liabilities plus expected future taxes against promised spending (p.110, PDF 120).
- DM / EM sovereign — developed-market issuers, near default-free in domestic currency; emerging-market issuers with higher credit and currency risk (pp.110–111, PDF 120–121).
- Restricted currency — poorly convertible currency, which limits long local-currency issuance (p.111, PDF 121).
- External debt — owed to foreign creditors; creates indirect currency exposure (p.111, PDF 121).
- Fiscal policy vs debt management — level of debt vs its composition (p.112, PDF 122).
- Ricardian equivalence — debt vs tax financing irrelevant under strict assumptions (pp.112–113, PDF 122–123).
- Rollover risk — uncertain cost of refinancing maturing short-term debt (p.113, PDF 123).
- Competitive / non-competitive bid — price-and-quantity bid vs quantity-only bid (p.117, PDF 127).
- Single-price vs multiple-price auction — all winners pay the stop price vs each pays own bid (p.117, PDF 127).
- Stop (cut-off) yield — highest yield needed to fill the offering (p.117, PDF 127).
- Primary dealer — designated intermediary expected to bid in every auction (p.118, PDF 128).
- On-the-run / off-the-run — most recently issued benchmark vs older, thinly traded issues (p.119, PDF 129).
- Government agency (quasi-government) — entity funding a specific public purpose under sovereign or local law (p.121, PDF 131).
- General obligation (GO) bond — unsecured, repaid from general local taxes (p.121, PDF 131).
- Revenue bond — repaid from the income of one specific project (p.122, PDF 132).

## Traps
- Mixing up level and composition: fiscal policy sets the amount, debt management the mix (p.112).
- Treating all sovereign debt as risk-free. That holds only for DM domestic-currency debt; EM external debt can default (p.111).
- Equating external debt with foreign-currency debt. It can be in the issuer's own currency (p.111).
- Thinking hard-currency lenders have no currency risk. The risk is indirect, through the issuer's ability to earn that currency (p.111).
- Believing Ricardian equivalence works in practice; its assumptions fail, which is why rollover risk matters (p.113).
- Confusing price with yield in auctions: the best bid is the highest price and lowest yield; bids with yields above the stop yield lose (p.117).
- Thinking non-competitive bidders pay their own price or risk being shut out; they pay the auction result and are always filled (p.117).
- Assuming agency yield equals the sovereign's or carries its full liquidity premium; it is near, not equal (p.121).
- Reversing on-the-run and off-the-run: on-the-run is the newest issue (p.119).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. **Single-price auction.** Offer 100 units. Competitive bids: A 40 units at 3.00%, B 40 at 3.05%, C 40 at 3.10%; non-competitive 10 units. Non-competitive fills first (10), leaving 90. Rank by lowest yield: A 40, B 40 (cumulative 80), C gets 10 of its 40. Stop yield 3.10%; all pay the 3.10% price. Under multiple-price, A pays its own 3.00% price and B its 3.05% price. Check: 10+40+40+10 = 100.
2. **Marginal bidder changes.** Same auction, but C bids 3.20% and D bids 30 units at 3.12%. Ranking: A 40, B 40, D takes 10 of 30. Stop yield 3.12%; C gets nothing, because 3.20% is above the stop yield.
3. **Rollover.** Fund 1,000 for 10 years with ten successive 1-year bills starting at 2%, or one 10-year bond at 3%. If the bill rate jumps to 5% from year 6, interest is 20 a year for years 1–5 and 50 for years 6–10: 5×20 + 5×50 = 350 for bills vs 10×30 = 300 for the bond. Bills start cheaper but their cost swings: rollover risk.
