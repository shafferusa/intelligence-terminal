## Core principles
- **Revenue follows earning, not cash (pp.40–41).** A sale belongs to the period when the seller has delivered what the customer bought. A credit sale creates a receivable; cash received in advance creates a liability (unearned revenue) released as delivery happens.
- **Five-step revenue model (pp.41–44).** IFRS 15 and ASC 606 are close to identical: identify the contract, identify distinct performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it, recognize revenue as each obligation is satisfied. Steps 3–4 set the amount; step 5 sets the timing. Uncertain variable amounts are held back until reversal is highly unlikely. The collectability threshold wording differs between IFRS and US GAAP.
- **Contract balances (pp.43–44).** Revenue recognized before the right to payment is unconditional appears as a contract asset; a receivable arises once only the passage of time remains; cash received before delivery gives a contract liability. An agent reports only its commission; a principal reports gross.
- **Matching expenses (pp.45–46, p.58).** An expense is matched to the revenue it helped earn, to the period of spending, or across the periods that benefit. Bad debts and warranty costs are estimated and expensed in the sale period, not at default or claim. They are expenses, not revenue reductions. Estimates are management judgments that can smooth income.
- **Depreciation spreads a cost already paid (pp.46–50).** Method, life and residual value only change when the expense shows up. Accelerated methods front-load expense, then reverse. Longer lives and higher residuals reduce annual expense. If analysts cannot adjust, they judge policies more or less conservative.
- **Common-size removes scale (pp.51–53).** Dividing every line by revenue makes different-sized firms and years comparable and exposes strategy (high gross margin plus heavy spending suggests a differentiated product; low margin suggests price competition). Aggregated benchmarks hide detail.
- **Comprehensive income captures equity changes that skip net income (pp.54–57).** Comprehensive income = net income + OCI. Four common OCI types: foreign-currency translation, effective hedge derivatives, unrealized gains/losses on securities at fair value through OCI, and certain pension items. EPS-based ratios can omit large OCI losses.

## Vocabulary
- Revenue — amount billed for normal goods/services, after returns and discounts (p.35).
- Net income — revenue less expenses, adjusted for gains and losses (pp.34, 37–38).
- Noncontrolling interest — outside owners' share of a consolidated subsidiary's profit (p.38).
- Expense by nature vs function — grouped by type (wages) vs by purpose (cost of goods sold) (p.38).
- Gross profit — revenue less cost of sales; multi-step shows it, single-step does not (p.38).
- Operating profit — gross profit less operating expenses; not always identical to EBIT (p.39).
- Performance obligation, transaction price, variable consideration (pp.41–44).
- Contract asset / contract liability — revenue booked ahead of billing / cash taken ahead of delivery (pp.43–44).
- Input vs output method — progress by cost spent vs units delivered (p.43).
- Principal vs agent — gross vs net revenue (p.44).
- Matching principle (pp.45–46).
- Residual value, net book value, impairment (annual test for goodwill and indefinite-life intangibles) (pp.46–50).
- Common-size analysis — each line as a percent of revenue (pp.52–53).
- Other comprehensive income — equity changes bypassing net income (pp.54–56).
- FVTPL / FVOCI — IFRS fair-value categories, gains to income / to OCI (pp.54–56).

## Formulas
1. Net income = Revenue − Expenses (+ gains − losses). Stated in words (p.34).
2. Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales; Operating profit = Gross profit − operating expenses (before tax, and for non-financial firms before interest). Definitions vary by company (pp.38–39).
3. Straight-line depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) / Useful life. Very sensitive to both estimates (p.47).
4. Diminishing balance depreciation = (Acceleration factor × 1/Life) × Opening net book value. Factor commonly 1.5 or 2.0 (2.0 = double-declining). Residual is not in the base but depreciation stops at residual; with zero or small residual it never fully depreciates, so firms switch to straight-line (pp.48–49).
5. Cost-to-cost revenue to date = (Costs incurred / Total expected costs) × Transaction price; period revenue = that less revenue already recognized. A revised estimate causes a catch-up in the period of change (pp.43–44).
6. Common-size % = Line item / Revenue × 100; taxes divided by pretax income give the effective tax rate (pp.52–53).
7. OCI = Ending equity − (Beginning equity + Net income − Dividends), with no share issuance or repurchase; Comprehensive income = Net income + OCI (p.54; worked on p.56).
8. Comprehensive EPS = EPS + OCI per share (OCI / shares; negative for a loss); then Price / Comprehensive EPS (p.57).

## Traps
- Treating revenue as cash received (p.41).
- Netting bad debts against revenue; they are an expense booked on estimate (p.45).
- Assuming operating profit equals EBIT (p.39).
- Believing accelerated depreciation changes cash; it only shifts timing (p.50).
- Putting residual value into the declining-balance base (p.48).
- Reporting gross sales when the firm is an agent (p.44).
- Calling OCI part of net income or non-operating income (p.54).
- Scaling taxes by revenue when the effective tax rate needs pretax income (p.53).
- Assuming IFRS and US GAAP revenue rules match in every respect (p.41), or that income-statement and cash-flow classification of interest always agree (p.50).
- Comparing labelled lines without checking definitions (pp.35–39).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. **Cost-to-cost.** A firm builds a machine for $500,000 under an over-time contract; total expected cost $400,000; cost to date $100,000. Progress = 25%; revenue = $125,000. If expected total cost is revised to $500,000, progress is 20%, cumulative revenue $100,000, so the period of revision books a catch-up of −$25,000.
2. **Depreciation.** A truck costs $60,000, residual $10,000, life 5 years, bought at the start of year 1. Straight-line = $10,000 a year. Double-declining: 40% × $60,000 = $24,000 in year 1; 40% × $36,000 = $14,400 in year 2. Year-1 pretax income is $14,000 lower under double-declining; cash paid is the same.
3. **OCI plug.** Opening equity $300m, net income $40m, dividends $10m, no issuance, closing equity $335m. Without OCI, equity would be $330m, so OCI = +$5m and comprehensive income = $45m.
