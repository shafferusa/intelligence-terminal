## Learning outcomes
1. Explain the special considerations in judging the credit of sovereign and non-sovereign government issuers and their issues (stated printed p.383, PDF 393; taught in Sovereign Credit Analysis, printed 386–398, and Non-Sovereign Credit Risk, printed 399–405).

## Core principles
- **Governments borrow for policy, not profit.** Debt service comes from taxes, fees and tariffs (sometimes state-enterprise profits), not operating cash flow. Ask what the repayment source is and how stable it is (pp.383–386).
- **Ability and willingness to pay are separate.** A large tax base does not prevent a restructuring if institutions or politics are weak. Sovereign immunity means creditors cannot force bankruptcy or seize assets, so trouble usually ends in negotiated restructuring. An exchange that under-compensates creditors can count as default in substance (pp.385, 387–388).
- **Sovereign debt is usually a country's safest.** The sovereign can tax all activity in its territory, and an independent central bank is less likely to print money to repay. Advanced-economy sovereigns are often treated as nearly default risk-free (pp.383–385, 388).
- **Five qualitative factors (pp.387–390).** Institutions and policy (home of willingness to pay), fiscal flexibility, monetary effectiveness, economic flexibility (diversification, shock absorption), and external status. Single-commodity economies have volatile tax receipts; informal economies collect less. The factors reinforce each other.
- **Reserve currency (pp.389, 396).** A reserve-currency government can borrow abroad in its own currency. A non-reserve issuer must earn foreign currency (exports, remittances, tourism) to repay foreign-currency debt, so credit tracks those inflows and commodity prices. The conclusion fails if inflows drop, reserves shrink, or external debt outgrows reserves.
- **Quantitative analysis is top-down (pp.391–398).** Government data vary in quality and timing, so analysts use macro ratios over GDP or revenue in three groups: fiscal strength (debt burden and debt affordability; higher is weaker for both), growth and stability (size, per capita income, average growth, volatility), and external stability (reserves, external debt, balance of payments). Ratios are weighed with qualitative factors, never alone (p.393).
- **Non-sovereign issuers (pp.399–401).** Agencies are statutory, mission-driven bodies with explicit or implicit sovereign support, usually rated like the sovereign. Government banks and development institutions behave similarly. Supranationals are owned by several sovereigns; their rating reflects members and support.
- **Regional governments: GO vs revenue (pp.401–405).** A GO bond is unsecured, relying on general revenue and taxing power, so analysis resembles sovereign analysis, but the issuer has limited jurisdiction, no monetary or national fiscal control, and exposure to local industry decline and population change. A revenue bond is repaid from one project's cash flow, is riskier than GO, is analysed like a corporate credit, and is judged on DSCR, often with a minimum-coverage covenant. The government may be an implicit backstop. Some countries rate regions equal to the sovereign; the US rates them individually. Regional credit is usually equal to or below the sovereign's (p.404).

## Vocabulary
- Ability / willingness to pay — capacity vs intent to service debt (pp.385, 387).
- Sovereign immunity — legal limits on forcing a sovereign into bankruptcy or asset sale (p.387).
- Reserve currency — freely exchangeable currency held in foreign reserves (p.389).
- Monetization of debt — financing government via the central bank, risking inflation (p.388).
- Default in substance — voluntary exchange treated as default because creditors lost value (p.388).
- Debt burden — leverage-type ratios such as debt to GDP (p.392).
- Debt affordability — coverage-type ratios such as interest to revenue; higher is worse (p.392).
- External liquidity and solvency — ability to generate stable foreign-currency inflows (p.396).
- Agency — statutory, mission-driven public body (p.400).
- Government sector bank / development financing institution — created or supported by government for public goals (p.400).
- Supranational — entity owned by several sovereigns (p.401).
- Regional (sub-sovereign) issuer — state, province or local government; municipal bond is the US term (p.401).
- GO bond — unsecured, backed by general revenues and taxing power (p.402).
- Revenue bond — repaid from one project's cash flow (p.402).
- DSCR — project revenue left after operating costs relative to principal plus interest due (pp.402–403).

## Formulas
The module has no numbered formulas. Ratio definitions come from Exhibits 4, 6 and 7.
- Debt to GDP = general government debt / GDP (p.392; PDF 402). Higher is weaker.
- Debt to revenue = general government debt / government revenue (p.392). Higher is weaker.
- Interest to GDP = government interest payments / GDP (p.392). Higher is weaker.
- Interest to revenue = government interest payments / revenue (p.392). Higher is weaker.
- Annual fiscal deficit or surplus as % of GDP (p.392): shows discipline and debt-burden direction.
- Average real GDP growth = (GDP t − GDP t−1) / GDP t−1. Exhibit 6 (p.394; PDF 404) prints the subtraction reversed, apparently a typo. Growth volatility = standard deviation of annual growth; per capita GDP = GDP / population.
- FX reserves to GDP = FX reserves / GDP; reserve ratio = FX reserves / external debt; external debt burden = long-term external debt / GDP; external debt due = external debt due within 12 months / GDP (Exhibit 7, p.396; PDF 406).
- DSCR = (revenue − operating expenses) / (principal + interest). The book gives only a verbal definition (pp.402–403); this form is a study aid. Higher is stronger, the opposite direction from the sovereign affordability ratios.

## Traps
- Believing a sovereign can be forced into bankruptcy (p.387).
- Reading every "higher ratio" as better: debt burden and affordability are worse when higher; reserve ratio and DSCR are better when higher (pp.392, 396, 402–403).
- Treating "debt affordability" as a coverage ratio where higher is good (p.398).
- Mixing qualitative "economic flexibility" with the quantitative "growth and stability" group (p.385).
- Equating capital controls or a fixed exchange rate with external strength; stable foreign-currency inflows matter (p.410).
- Letting one ratio decide a rating; reserves, competitiveness and foreign-currency debt also matter (p.397).
- Assuming local governments are safer than the sovereign (p.404).
- Classifying GO vs revenue by use of proceeds; the repayment source decides. A school financed by general taxes is GO; a bridge repaid by tolls is revenue (pp.404–405).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. Country A, billions: debt 600, GDP 800, revenue 200, interest 30. Debt/GDP = 75%, debt/revenue = 300%, interest/GDP = 3.75%, interest/revenue = 15%. If revenue falls 20% to 160 with debt and interest unchanged, debt/revenue = 375% and interest/revenue = 18.75%. Both worsen with no new borrowing.
2. Country B, USD billions: FX reserves 40, external debt 100, GDP 400. Reserve ratio = 40%, FX reserves/GDP = 10%. If reserves fall to 20, these become 20% and 5%.
3. Toll-road revenue bond, USD millions: revenue 50, operating expenses 30, principal 10, interest 6. DSCR = 20/16 = 1.25x, meeting a 1.20x covenant. If revenue falls to 46, DSCR = 16/16 = 1.00x and the covenant is breached.
