## Learning outcomes
1. Describe the sequence of steps in industry and competitive analysis (Process, pp.340–344).
2. Describe industry classification schemes and compare ways of grouping companies (Classification, pp.345–350).
3. Determine industry size, growth, profitability and market-share trends (Industry Survey, pp.351–360).
4. Analyze industry structure and external influences with Porter's Five Forces and PESTLE (pp.361–377).

## Core principles
- **Industry work supplies the story behind valuation inputs.** The narrative (growth, margins, share) justifies model numbers and can force forecast revisions. Five steps: define the industry, survey it, evaluate structure, assess external influences, evaluate the firm's strategy (next module) (pp.337, 340–344). A wrong peer group means wrong value drivers.
- **An industry is a peer group sharing value drivers.** Firms selling comparable products from the customer's view face the same forces, so peer multiples are meaningful. Schemes (GICS, ICB, TRBC) are strict hierarchies: one lowest-tier box per firm, by largest revenue source. Limits: business models inside a box differ, a conglomerate hides its rivals, global schemes suit local markets poorly, membership changes (pp.345–348).
- **Alternative groupings suit different questions.** Geography (domicile, listing or HQ) helps when local conditions drive results; cyclical vs defensive vs growth sorts by economic sensitivity. Fixed costs magnify cyclical profit swings (pp.349–350).
- **Size is a denominator choice; share and concentration inherit it.** Top-down uses aggregate statistics; bottom-up sums participants' relevant revenue only. A smaller industry total raises every share and the HHI. Bottom-up fails with many private firms; top-down without granular data (pp.352, 356–360).
- **Profitability is judged against the cost of capital.** ROIC rewards all capital providers, so compare it with WACC; a positive gap is excess return, a negative gap destroys value. Growth industries beat the economy but are not automatically profitable (pp.353–355).
- **Five Forces explains profitability; PESTLE explains growth and share shifts.** Intense forces make ROIC above WACC unlikely. A moat (intangibles, switching costs, network effects, cost advantage, efficient scale) can protect all firms or only one or two. Buyers and suppliers capture profit when concentrated or hard to leave or replace; vertical integration (upstream or downstream) offsets them at the cost of capital and flexibility but does not address substitutes (pp.361–369).
- **Concentration and rivalry move in opposite directions.** Many small players usually means fiercer price competition; consolidation usually softens it, unless the market is segmented or differentiated. Check organic vs acquired share change (pp.356–357).
- **Innovation type decides financial impact.** Basic research has no immediate use; breakthrough innovation creates new products across industries; sustaining is incremental; disruptive attacks with a new value proposition. Incumbents either invest (hastening their own decline) or lag (losing share) (printed pp.372–373; PDF 382–383).

## Vocabulary
- Industry — firms offering comparable products from a customer's view (p.345). GICS / ICB / TRBC are commercial hierarchical schemes; NAICS / NACE are national or regional (p.345).
- BICS — Bloomberg scheme showing segment detail across industries (p.348).
- Strict hierarchical taxonomy — each firm in exactly one lowest group (p.346).
- Cyclical / defensive / growth — grouping by economic sensitivity (pp.349–350).
- Top-down / bottom-up size estimate — aggregate statistic vs sum of participant revenue (p.352).
- Market share — firm's industry-relevant revenue over industry revenue (pp.351, 356).
- Mature vs growth industry — near or below GDP growth vs above-trend regardless of the cycle (pp.353–354).
- NOPAT, ROIC, WACC — operating profit after tax, return on all capital, cost of all capital (pp.354–355).
- HHI — concentration index from squared shares (p.356).
- Organic vs acquired share change — gain from sales vs purchases (p.357).
- Porter's Five Forces — entrants, substitutes, buyers, suppliers, rivalry (pp.341, 361).
- Economic moat — durable competitive advantage (p.361).
- Vertical integration (backward/upstream, forward/downstream) — bringing supply or distribution steps in-house (pp.366–368).
- PESTLE — political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental (pp.343, 369).
- Energy transition — shift to lower-carbon energy affecting producers and heavy users (p.375; PDF 385).

## Formulas
1. **ROIC = NOPAT_t / Average invested capital** (Equation 1, pp.354–355). NOPAT_t = net operating profit after tax for period t; average invested capital = long-term liabilities plus equity, averaged over start and end of the period; result in %. Compare with WACC (difference, or ratio above 1 = excess return).
2. **Operating profit margin = Operating income / Total revenue** (p.354). Avoids non-operating distortions of net margin.
3. **HHI = Σ s_i²**, i = 1..n (Equation 2, p.356). s_i = share as a whole number (50% = 50); maximum 10,000 (monopoly). Depends on the size base and on assumptions for small firms. Some regulators call over 1,800 highly concentrated and scrutinise deals adding over 100 (p.357).
4. **Market share = Firm's industry revenue / Industry revenue** (pp.351, 356). Numerator is only revenue in the industry as defined.

## Traps
- Using total company revenue as the numerator when the firm has other segments; this overstates share.
- Entering HHI shares as decimals instead of whole numbers; HHI is not a firm count or top-four share.
- Treating high concentration as intense rivalry; usually the reverse. Lower concentration means a lower HHI and says nothing definite about differentiation.
- Assuming growth industries are always profitable, or confusing "defensive" (stable in downturns) with "growth".
- Believing classification captures every business of a multi-industry firm; it assigns one group.
- Reading vertical integration as outsourcing or a fix for substitutes; patents are finite barriers.
- Comparing ROIC to the cost of equity instead of WACC; geographic grouping uses domicile or listing, not where revenue sits.

## Original practice seeds
1. **Hypothetical HHI after a merger.** Four firms hold 40, 30, 20 and 10 percent. HHI = 1,600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3,000. If the 20 and 10 firms merge: 1,600 + 900 + 900 = 3,400, an increase of 400 (check 2 × 20 × 10 = 400), well above a 100-point trigger.
2. **Hypothetical market share.** Regional bakery sales in one year total 800 million. Firm A's bakery segment earns 120 million (total company revenue 200 million, including a coffee-machine unit). Share = 120/800 = 15.0%. If the industry total is revised to 600, share = 20.0%. Using total revenue gives 25.0%, which is wrong.
3. **Hypothetical ROIC vs WACC.** NOPAT 90; invested capital 800 at start and 1,000 at end, average 900. ROIC = 90/900 = 10.0%. With WACC 8.0%: spread +2.0 points, ratio 1.25. With WACC 11.0%: spread −1.0 point, ratio 0.91 (below 1).
