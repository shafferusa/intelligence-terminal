## Learning outcomes
The module's three outcomes are generic to the Standards modules (p.157, PDF 171):
1. Apply the Code and Standards to situations involving professional integrity: IV(A), IV(B), IV(C) and their examples (pp.158–175).
2. Recommend practices and procedures that prevent violations: the three Compliance Practices sections (pp.161, 166, 170–171); IV(C) is richest.
3. Distinguish conforming from violating conduct: all examples and practice problems (pp.161–177).

## Core principles
- **Loyalty ends when employment ends (IV(A), pp.158–160).** The employer supplies pay, clients, information and tools; you may not turn them against it. The duty lasts until pay and duties truly stop, not until the resignation letter. After that, only contracts (non-solicit) and confidentiality still bind.
- **Preparing is allowed; soliciting and taking are not.** Arranging a new business or firm on your own time is fine. Soliciting clients (explicit or implicit), using firm records or client lists, self-dealing, and disparaging the employer are violations. A wish to protect clients does not justify early solicitation; if the employer is egregious, resign first (p.159).
- **Firm records belong to the firm.** Files you created still need written permission to take; remove or return firm material on personal devices. Your own skills, experience and the bare names of former clients are not confidential (p.160). Contact with former clients after leaving is fine if details do not come from firm records and no agreement bars it.
- **Duty to employer is not absolute (pp.158–161).** Personal and family obligations need not be subordinated, employer policy yields to law and the Code, and whistle-blowing to protect clients or market integrity can be justified if not for personal gain. IV(A) covers employees; independent contractors are governed by their agreement (p.161).
- **Extra pay needs written consent (IV(B), pp.165–166).** A third party paying you (often a client) can bias you toward the payer and away from the employer and other clients. Consent from all parties, in any retrievable form, is the cure. Indirect and non-cash benefits count. Disclose nature, approximate amount and duration.
- **Supervisors owe reasonable efforts to prevent and detect (IV(C), pp.168–169).** Anyone with authority or influence over others is a supervisor, credentialed or not. "Reasonable" depends on headcount and nature of work; delegation is allowed with instruction. A code of ethics alone is not enough: procedures, review, training and incentives are needed. If a supervisor clearly cannot discharge supervision because the system is missing or inadequate, decline until reasonable procedures exist and report to senior management (p.169).
- **When a violation surfaces (p.169).** Assess the extent promptly; do not rely on the employee's assurances; a warning is not sufficient; limit activities or raise monitoring pending investigation. A supervisor with sound procedures is not automatically in violation when one slips through, but knowing or should-have-known non-compliance can be.
- **Incentives matter (p.171).** Pay linked only to revenue invites a "succeed at all costs" culture, so reward how results are achieved, not only what is earned. A supervisor whose own pay depends on the activity needing scrutiny faces a conflict (IV(C) Ex 2, p.172).

## Vocabulary
- Duty of loyalty — acting for the employer's benefit in work matters: no harming it, withholding your abilities, or revealing its confidences (pp.157–158).
- Self-dealing — redirecting an employer's asset, deal or knowledge to your own gain (p.159).
- Whistle-blowing — acting against employer wishes to protect clients or market integrity (p.160).
- Written consent — any communication leaving a record you can later produce, such as a saved email (p.166).
- Supervisory responsibility — authority or influence over employees, regardless of credentials (p.168).
- Compliance system — policies, procedures, training, review and enforcement beyond a code of ethics (p.168).
- Code of ethics — short, principle-based statement for all employees (p.170).
- Compliance procedures — detailed operational rules supporting the code (p.170).
- Nonsolicitation agreement — contract barring contact with former clients for a period (p.165).
- Independent contractor — not an employee; duties set by the agreement with the client (p.161).
- Additional compensation — pay or benefit from someone other than the employer, cash or not (p.166).
- Compliance officer — person with defined authority and resources to run the procedures (p.170).

## Traps
- "Resigning ends my duty." Loyalty runs until employment actually ends (p.159).
- "Taking my own work product is fine." Any firm records need written permission (p.162).
- "Knowing former clients' names is confidential." It is not (p.160).
- "Interns and unpaid staff owe nothing." Treat them as employees for these purposes (p.162).
- "Only cash needs IV(B) consent." Non-monetary benefits count (p.167); and IV(B) calls for consent, not a ban on extra pay (p.166).
- "IV(A) bans all independent business." It bars only competing services without consent; preparation is allowed (pp.158–160).
- "A code of ethics satisfies IV(C)," or "warning the violator suffices." Neither does (pp.168–169).
- "Supervisors are strictly liable." The standard is reasonable efforts, but should-have-known counts (p.169).

## Original practice seeds
1. (Hypothetical) An analyst gives notice on 1 March; her last paid day is 31 March. On 15 March she emails clients her new firm's phone number. Violation: employment continues to 31 March and she promoted the new firm. On 1 April, using only public information and with no non-solicit agreement, no IV(A) issue.
2. (Hypothetical) A manager receives USD 2,000 a year for a seminar series from a vendor. She emails compliance with nature (speaking fee), amount (about USD 2,000) and duration (12 months) and gets written approval. IV(B) is met. If the fee became USD 20,000 tied to vendor products being used in client portfolios, the conflict is far greater and the new amount must be disclosed.
3. (Hypothetical) A head of trading supervises 8 traders with no written allocation policy. She drafts one, trains the team and reviews allocation logs monthly: 12 x 8 = 96 trader-month reviews a year. If one trader still cherry-picks, she may not have violated IV(C); had she never reviewed logs despite suspicion, she likely did.
