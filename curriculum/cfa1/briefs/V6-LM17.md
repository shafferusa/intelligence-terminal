## Learning outcomes

1. Explain how securitization benefits issuers, investors, and the wider economy and financial markets. Taught in The Benefits of Securitization and its three subsections, pp.446–451 (PDF 456–461).
2. Describe securitization: the parties involved and the role each plays. Taught in The Securitization Process, pp.452–459 (PDF 462–469), especially Parties (p.456, PDF 466) and The Role of the SPE (pp.457–459, PDF 467–469).

## Core principles

- **Pooling turns loans into a bond.** An ABS is a bond-like claim paid from a pool of loans or receivables. ABS credit risk is the risk that borrowers do not pay (p.443, PDF 453).
- **Three rungs of complexity.** A covered bond stays on the bank's balance sheet and the bank itself pays, with the pool only as backup, so it is not a true securitization. A pass-through is a true sale to a separate entity, and investors receive whatever the pool pays, so timing and amounts vary. Structured bonds add a preset payment schedule, subordination and credit enhancement to make timing more predictable (p.447, PDF 457).
- **Tranching reallocates risk, not total cash.** One pool pays several classes. Senior classes are paid first and absorb losses last, so they accept lower yield; junior classes take losses first and earn more. Small pool losses hit only the bottom class; large ones eat upward through the layers (p.448, PDF 458).
- **Issuers separate making loans from funding them.** A bank sells loans to the SPE for cash, gaining fees, lower regulatory capital need, liquidity from illiquid assets, and capacity to lend more than its balance sheet could hold. What it keeps is reputational and legal exposure (representations on asset quality), not the borrowers' payments after a true sale (pp.448–449, PDF 458–459).
- **Investors get access and tailoring; the economy gets liquidity.** Investors reach loan types they could not originate, pick exposure by tranche, and diversify while holding a standard-bond-like security. Tradable securities are more liquid than whole loans, trading produces market prices, and borrowers gain an alternative funding source, often cheaper. Contraction, extension and credit risk remain (pp.449–451, PDF 459–461).
- **The seven-step cash flow.** Loans are made, sold to the SPE, the SPE pays the seller cash, the SPE issues ABS, investors pay the SPE, borrowers pay the SPE, and the SPE passes agreed payments to investors. The cash for the loans comes from investors via the SPE. Servicing (collecting payments) can stay with the originator or go elsewhere; it is separate from securitizing (pp.453–456, PDF 463–466).
- **The SPE decouples seller credit from ABS credit.** The SPE is a separate, bankruptcy-remote owner of the assets, so the seller's bankruptcy does not reach the pool, and courts generally cannot reshuffle seniority. The text says this insulation is why securitization can fund more cheaply than a corporate bond secured on the same assets. It breaks down if the sale is not a true sale, if a transfer is challenged as a fraudulent conveyance, or if local trust law is weak (pp.457–459, PDF 467–469).
- **Information comes through the trustee.** The trustee holds assets and funds and sends periodic cash-flow reports, investors' only way to update their view of credit quality (p.456, PDF 466).

## Vocabulary

- ABS — security whose payments come from a pool of loans or receivables (p.443).
- Securitization — pooling assets and issuing securities paid from their cash flows (p.443).
- SPE (also SPV, special purpose company) — separate legal entity that buys the assets and issues the ABS; called the issuer in the prospectus (pp.444, 454, 456).
- Bankruptcy-remote — unaffected by the seller's insolvency (pp.454, 457).
- True sale — full, irrevocable legal transfer of assets to the SPE, so the seller derecognises them (p.457).
- Covered bond — bank bond backed by a segregated pool that stays on the bank's balance sheet (p.447).
- Pass-through security — passes pool payments to investors through a separate entity (p.447).
- Subordination / tranching — classes with different payment and loss priority (p.448); credit enhancement lowers investors' credit risk (pp.447, 456).
- RMBS, CMBS, CMO (mortgage-backed); CDO, CLO, CBO, CDO-squared (other ABS) — product labels grouped on p.448.
- Servicer — collects payments, chases late payers, recovers collateral (p.453).
- Seller / depositor — originator selling the collateral to the SPE (p.456).
- Purchase agreement — seller's representations and warranties on asset quality (p.456).
- Prospectus — describes structure, payment priority and credit enhancements (p.456).
- Trustee — disinterested institution holding assets and funds, issuing cash-flow reports (p.456).
- Whole-business securitization — ABS backed by an operating business's revenue stream (p.451).

## Formulas

None in this module (verified in the reading notes). The only arithmetic is that tranche sizes add up to the pool, and loss allocation runs from the bottom tranche up.

## Traps

- Calling a covered bond a securitization: the pool stays with the bank, which pays (p.447).
- Thinking investors take the originator's credit risk. After a true sale they take pool (borrower) risk (pp.457, 464).
- Assuming bonds newly issued by the originator rank ahead of or behind the ABS: seniority does not apply across different issuers (p.458).
- Believing ABS behave like equity. They behave like standard bonds (p.449).
- Believing securitization lowers system liquidity; it raises it (p.450).
- When asked why ABS funding costs less, both collateral backing and SPE insulation appear in the text; read what the question asks and rank the reasons (pp.457–459).
- Assuming investors benefit most directly. The solutions stress issuers and markets benefit more directly (pp.452, 463).
- Confusing securitizing with servicing (p.453).

## Original practice seeds

All three are hypothetical.

1. **Fees from recycling capital.** A lender sells a 200m loan pool to an SPE for 200m cash, ignoring costs, and relends the 200m at a 1% origination fee: 200 × 0.01 = 2.0m. At 1.5% it is 3.0m. Recycling twice a year at 1% gives 4.0m. Point: the sale frees balance-sheet room.
2. **Loss order.** A 500m pool is split into senior 330m, mezzanine 110m and equity 60m (sums to 500). A 40m pool loss: equity absorbs all 40, the others lose nothing. A 100m loss: equity loses 60, mezzanine loses 40 (40/110 = 36.4%), senior nothing. A 190m loss: equity 60, mezzanine 110, senior 20, which is 20/330 = 6.06% of the senior tranche (no recoveries assumed).
3. **Bankruptcy-remote.** A manufacturer with 600m of bonds sells 300m of receivables to an SPE that issues 300m of ABS, then goes bankrupt. If every customer pays, ABS holders are paid in full, as the receivables are outside the bankrupt estate. If 10% default, the shortfall is 300 × 0.10 = 30m, borne by ABS holders junior-first, and the manufacturer's bondholders have no claim on the pool. Assumes a valid true sale.
