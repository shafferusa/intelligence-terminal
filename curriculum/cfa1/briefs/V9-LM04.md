## Core principles
- **The IPS comes first.** Fact-finding yields a written plan of objectives and constraints: a shared yardstick and evidence in disputes. It records a plan, not guaranteed outcomes, and needs regular and post-change review (pp.160–161).
- **Risk tolerance has two parts.** Ability is objective (horizon, income stability, wealth versus liabilities); willingness is psychological. When they disagree, adopt the lower and document it; counsel, but do not reshape personality unless it rests on a misperception (pp.163–168).
- **Objectives must be measurable and consistent.** Absolute risk objectives stand alone (probability of loss, standard deviation, VaR); relative ones use a benchmark (tracking risk). Returns: state nominal or real, before or after fees and tax, and prefer an investable benchmark; peer-group ranks are not investable. An unrealistic required return signals conflicting objectives (pp.163–169).
- **Constraints shape the asset menu.** Expected or lumpy withdrawals call for liquid, short-maturity holdings. Short horizons rule out risky or illiquid assets. Taxable investors prefer deferral and favoured assets; tax-exempt ones are indifferent. Record legal limits and trading blackouts. Human capital: avoid the sector that pays your salary (pp.170–174).
- **SAA comes first because systematic risk dominates.** SAA is the long-run policy weights across asset classes (plus any hedge policy) that make the objectives reachable inside the constraints. Inputs: CMEs and the IPS. Asset classes should be internally similar, mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive and diversifying (pp.178–181).
- **Mean-variance logic picks the point.** Utility rises with expected return and falls with variance scaled by risk aversion. The best allocation is where the efficient frontier touches the highest reachable indifference curve. New CMEs move the frontier; new objectives or constraints reshape the curves. Limits: single period; an IPS gives thresholds, not a utility function. Exclusions shrink the opportunity set (pp.182–186).
- **A policy portfolio is not yet a portfolio.** Risk budgeting divides total risk among SAA, tactical allocation (deliberate deviation) and security selection. Selection is zero-sum before costs, so the average active manager trails after costs. Drift is unintended; rebalancing by calendar or corridor restores policy risk (pp.187–190).
- **ESG changes inputs, not just labels.** Screens narrow the universe and call for matching benchmarks; integration changes forecasts and position sizes; engagement needs clarity over who votes. Return evidence is mixed (pp.173–174, 191–194).

## Vocabulary
- Investment policy statement (IPS) — written plan of objectives and constraints (p.160).
- Absolute / relative risk objective — loss limit needing no benchmark / risk limit against a benchmark (p.163).
- Tracking risk — standard deviation of portfolio minus benchmark returns (footnote 3, p.163); LDI — investing to meet known obligations (p.163).
- Ability to bear risk / willingness to take risk — objective capacity / subjective comfort (pp.164–165).
- Six ESG approaches — negative screening, positive screening, ESG integration, thematic, engagement/active ownership, impact (p.173).
- Asset class — group of assets with similar risk-return behaviour (p.178).
- Strategic asset allocation (SAA) — long-run policy weights by asset class (pp.161, 178).
- Capital market expectations (CME) — forecasts of class returns, risks, correlations (p.178).
- Risk budgeting; tactical asset allocation (TAA) — splitting total risk among sources; deliberate short-term deviation from policy weights (p.187).
- Drift, corridor, rebalancing policy — unplanned weight movement; allowed band; restoring rules (p.188).
- Risk parity — weights set by risk contribution instead of capital (p.191).

## Formulas
1. Utility: U_p = E(R_p) − λσ_p² (p.183, PDF 193). Single-period; λ > 0 is risk aversion; the penalty uses variance, not standard deviation.
2. Expected return: E(R_p) = Σ w_i E(R_i), weights sum to 1 (p.183); valid for any correlations.
3. Risk: σ_p = sqrt(ΣΣ w_i w_j Cov(R_i,R_j)), double sum over all pairs including i = j (p.183).
4. Covariance: Cov(R_i,R_j) = ρ_ij σ_i σ_j; ρ from −1 to +1 (p.183).
5. Required return from a real goal (worked example, pp.168–169): FV_nominal = goal in today's money × (1 + inflation)^years; required return = (FV_nominal / capital actually invested)^(1/years) − 1. Use only invested capital, not reserves held apart.
6. TAA contribution (p.190): Σ (starting weight − policy weight) × period return. Return on policy weights = Σ policy weight × class return.
7. Tracking-risk rule of thumb (p.164): about 95% of outcomes within 2 standard deviations if normal, so a "within X%" band means tracking risk of about X/2.

## Traps
- Treating tolerance as willingness alone, or a high-willingness, low-ability client as able to take lots of risk. Take the lower.
- Calling ability subjective. Willingness is the psychological part.
- Forgetting to inflate a goal stated in today's money before solving for the return, or using the wrong capital base.
- Judging asset-class distinctness backwards: lowest correlation means most distinct. Within-class correlations should exceed across-class ones.
- Confusing TAA with SAA or arbitrage. TAA is deliberate; drift is accidental.
- Believing an IPS guarantees outcomes, or that execution guidelines are the most client-specific section; objectives and constraints are.
- Assuming a P&C insurer needs more return; its distinguishing feature is greater liquidity need versus a life insurer.
- Writing σ instead of σ² in the utility penalty.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. Utility, one year, λ = 2. Portfolio X: E = 8%, σ = 12%; Portfolio Y: E = 6%, σ = 7%. U_X = 0.08 − 2(0.0144) = 0.0512; U_Y = 0.06 − 2(0.0049) = 0.0502; X wins. At λ = 4: U_X = 0.0224, U_Y = 0.0404; Y wins.
2. Two-asset risk: 60% equity (E 8%, σ 18%), 40% bonds (E 3%, σ 6%), ρ = 0.10. E(R_p) = 6.0%. Variance = 0.011664 + 0.000576 + 0.0005184 = 0.0127584, so σ_p ≈ 11.3%. At ρ = 0.60 variance = 0.0153504, σ_p ≈ 12.4%: same return, more risk.
3. Drift: policy 60/40 with ±3-point corridor, start 100. Equities +20%, bonds +2%: equities 72.0, bonds 40.8, total 112.8, equity weight 63.8% breaches 63%. Restoring 60/40 means selling 4.32 of equities and buying 4.32 of bonds. Keeping the drift on purpose makes the extra 3.8 points a TAA position.
