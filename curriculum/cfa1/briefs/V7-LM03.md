## Core principles
- **Timing gap creates the benefit.** Decisions and cash-market trades often happen at different dates (a firm commits to costs now and is paid later; an investor has a view but no cash). A forward or option fixes or caps a price today for a later date, moving risk across time to whoever wants it. The hedger and counterparty swap cash flows, and the hedge fails if the exposure does not match the contract. Four benefit groups: risk allocation/transfer/management, price discovery, operational advantages, market efficiency (pp.55–62).
- **Hedge payoff offsets exposure.** A forward to sell a foreign currency gains exactly when the receivable loses value, so the sum is flat around the forward rate. The hedger also gives up the favourable moves (pp.56–57, 68).
- **Covered call.** Long the underlying plus a sold call. The seller receives the premium up front, keeps the stock's gains up to the strike, and pays away gains above it. Profit is capped, loss is cushioned only by the premium, and the view is flat to modestly higher prices. Alone, the short call has open-ended loss; the holding "covers" it (p.58, PDF 66).
- **Price discovery.** Derivative prices reveal expectations of future prices, rate moves and supply/demand. Futures prices are not unbiased forecasts (p.59). Option prices embed implied volatility, a gauge of expected uncertainty (p.60). A futures price can go negative when taking physical delivery is costly (p.59).
- **Low outlay means implicit leverage.** The cash put up is margin or premium, but profit and loss accrue on the full notional. A small adverse move can wipe out a large share of the outlay, and a seller of options can lose without limit. Mitigants: exposure limits, daily mark-to-market, collateral, central counterparties (pp.63–64, 66).
- **Credit exposure follows value.** Unlike a loan, derivative exposure changes daily with prices. A paid-up option buyer is exposed to the seller; the seller has no exposure. In a forward the party currently in the money is at risk. Exchange daily settlement and central clearing sharply cut credit risk; OTC terms range from uncollateralised to margin-like (p.65, PDF 73).
- **Issuers hedge, then account.** Non-financial issuers hedge incidental exposures (inputs, FX, rates) to steady earnings and cash flow. A derivative outside a qualifying hedge is marked to market through earnings. Hedge accounting offsets hedge and hedged item, with MTM held in OCI until the hedged item hits earnings. To qualify, the derivative must mirror the exposure's timing and size, which favours customised OTC contracts (pp.68–69, PDF 76–77).
- **Investors replicate, isolate, modify.** They copy cash strategies more cheaply, isolate exposures (such as hedging FX on foreign holdings), or add exposures (including shorts). Funds mark positions into NAV daily, so hedge accounting matters less and exchange-traded, liquid markets are favoured. The prospectus states what is permitted (pp.70–72).

## Vocabulary
- Risk transfer/allocation — moving price exposure to others, across time, via forwards or options (p.56).
- Forward commitment vs contingent claim — obligation-type contract vs right-type contract (p.56).
- Price discovery — derivative prices revealing expected future prices and risk (p.59).
- Implied volatility — uncertainty backed out of an option's price (p.60).
- Covered call — long underlying plus a sold call (p.58).
- Operational advantages — low transaction cost, liquidity, small upfront cash, easy shorting, no storage (p.60).
- Market efficiency (as a benefit) — mispricing is cheaper to exploit in derivatives (p.61).
- Implicit leverage — large exposure per unit of cash outlay (p.63).
- Structured note — debt with embedded derivative(s) (p.64).
- Basis risk — hedge value diverges from the hedged exposure because the references differ (p.65).
- Liquidity risk — derivative cash flows are timed differently from the underlying's (p.65).
- Counterparty credit risk — the other side may fail to pay what it owes (p.65).
- Systemic risk — market-wide stress from leveraged derivative use (p.66).
- Central counterparty (CCP) — clearing intermediary that imposes margin (p.66).
- Hedge accounting — offsetting hedge and hedged item in the statements (p.68).
- Cash flow hedge — hedges variable cash flows or forecast transactions (p.69).
- Fair value hedge — hedges the fair value of a recognised asset or liability (p.69).
- Net investment hedge — hedges FX risk of equity in a foreign operation (p.69).
- OCI — equity account holding hedge MTM until released (p.69).
- NAV / prospectus — fund's daily marked value / document setting permitted instruments and purposes (p.71).

## Formulas
No numbered formulas. Calculation conventions:
- Interest cost on margin or purchase = amount financed x annual rate x (months/12). Simple interest; ignores compounding, storage, margin calls and any spot-futures price gap. p.61 (PDF 69).
- Return on cash outlay = (value at T − purchase price) / initial cash outlay (cost for spot, initial margin for futures). Ignores interest and margin changes. Intuition: same P&L over a smaller base. p.64 (PDF 72).
- Leverage ratio = underlying exposure / cash cost. p.64 (PDF 72).
- Home-currency proceeds = foreign amount / (foreign per home rate). Profit margin = (proceeds − cost) / proceeds; this margin definition is inferred by the notes, not stated. p.68 (PDF 76).
- Covered call: maximum profit = (strike − purchase level) + premium; breakeven = purchase level − premium. Derived by the notes from the payoff diagram, not stated. p.58 (PDF 66).
- Call payoff at expiry = Max(0, S_T − X) (S_T underlying at expiry, X strike). p.75 (PDF 83).

## Traps
- Margin is not the exposure. Futures P&L runs on the full notional, so percentage returns on margin are huge (p.64).
- A futures price is not an unbiased forecast of the future spot price (p.59).
- Basis risk is a value mismatch; liquidity risk is a cash-flow timing mismatch. Do not swap them (p.65).
- The option seller has no credit exposure once paid; the buyer does (p.65).
- Cash flow hedge: variable cash flow (floating debt swapped to fixed with a pay-fixed swap). Fair value hedge: fixed-rate debt swapped to floating. Check the swap direction (p.69).
- Under hedge accounting, MTM sits in OCI until the hedged item hits earnings; without it, it hits earnings at once. The text states OCI generally, but in standard practice it chiefly applies to cash flow hedges (pp.68–69).
- A covered call is not free protection: only the premium cushions losses, and upside is capped (p.58).
- Investors are less focused on hedge accounting than issuers, not indifferent; they favour standardised exchange-traded markets more than issuers do (p.71).

## Original practice seeds
1. **Covered call (hypothetical; index points, one unit, 6 months).** Own the index at 100, sell a 105-strike call for premium 2. At 103: +3 +2 = +5. At 110: +10 −5 +2 = +7 (the maximum, 5 + 2). At 95: −5 +2 = −3. Breakeven = 98. With premium 4: maximum 9, breakeven 96.
2. **Leverage (hypothetical; held to expiry, interest ignored).** Buy 10 units at 50: cash cost 500, or futures with margin 25. Price falls to 49: loss 10, so −2% on cash vs −40% on margin. At 48: −4% vs −80%. With margin 50 instead: −20% at 49.
3. **Hedge vs none (hypothetical).** Exporter receives JPY 120,000,000 in 60 days; cost EUR 700,000. Forward at 150 JPY/EUR gives EUR 800,000, profit 100,000, margin 12.5%. Unhedged at 160: EUR 750,000, profit 50,000, 6.7%. At 140: EUR 857,143, profit 157,143, 18.3%. The forward fixes 12.5% and forgoes the extra gain.
