## Learning outcomes
1. Show how a sales-driven pro forma model is built: income statement, cash flow, balance sheet, valuation inputs (pp.449–462).
2. Explain how behavioral biases distort analyst forecasts and how to counter each (pp.462–467).
3. Explain how competitive position (Porter's five forces) shapes pricing power and costs (pp.468–477).
4. Forecast industry and company sales and costs under inflation or deflation (pp.478–485).
5. Explain what drives the forecast horizon and the options beyond it: normalization and terminal value (pp.486–491).

## Core principles
- **Forecasts start with sales.** Growth is built from volume and price/mix, with currency and acquisitions kept apart. Costs follow as a percent of sales (variable) or as growth in currency units (fixed), so margins move through operating leverage. A segment forecast is a consistency check on the consolidated one, not a second answer (pp.451, 453).
- **Three statements are linked.** Net income feeds cash flow and equity; capex and depreciation drive fixed assets; days ratios drive receivables, inventory and payables; cash comes from the cash flow statement; unforecast items are held flat so the balance sheet balances. Interest is charged on beginning debt (pp.454, 458–461).
- **FCFF is cash to all capital providers** before financing flows. A rising working-capital need absorbs cash; a release adds cash (p.462).
- **Forecasters are biased in predictable ways.** Overconfidence: ranges too narrow, so use scenarios. Illusion of control: more detail is not more accuracy, so limit to material, checkable drivers. Conservatism: under-updating, so schedule reviews. Representativeness: ignoring class averages, so start from the base rate then adjust. Confirmation: seeking agreeing evidence, so read opposing views (pp.462–467).
- **Industry structure sets pricing power.** Weak forces let a firm pass on costs and earn higher margins and returns; strong forces squeeze them.  Government is not a sixth force; ask how a policy changes the five (pp.468–477).
- **Inflation is a price, volume and margin trade-off.** The company pays higher input costs first; recovery depends on elasticity and rivalry. Full pass-through protects margin percentage, but a smaller rise can lift or cut profit depending on volume. (pp.478–485).
- **Long-term forecasts need normalizing.** Match the horizon to holding period, cyclicality and company events. Terminal value is usually a growth perpetuity, so the final year must be mid-cycle. If disruption is expected beyond the horizon, adjust the terminal assumptions, not only near-term cash flows (pp.486–491).

## Vocabulary
- Organic growth — volume and price/mix only, excluding currency and acquisitions (p.451).
- Price/mix — price changes plus shifts between higher- and lower-priced products (p.451).
- Days inventory, DSO, DPO — days ratios used to project working-capital balances (p.459).
- Basic vs diluted shares — shares outstanding vs including in-the-money instruments net of assumed repurchase (p.455).
- FCFF — operating cash flow available to all capital providers (p.462).
- Overconfidence (p.462); illusion of control (p.464); conservatism, i.e. anchoring and adjustment (p.465); representativeness / base-rate neglect (p.466); confirmation bias (p.467).
- Inside vs outside view — company specifics vs class averages; use both (p.466).
- Porter's five forces — substitutes, rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, new entrants (p.468).
- ROIC — after-tax operating profit over operating assets less operating liabilities (pp.448–449, PDF 460–461).
- Price elasticity of demand — responsiveness of quantity demanded to price (p.480, PDF 492).
- Normalized earnings / FCF — expected mid-cycle level without temporary effects (p.487).
- Terminal value (p.488); inflection point, a sharp break from the past (p.488).

## Formulas
No formal equation blocks; these appear in prose and solutions.
1. Organic growth = (1 + volume growth) x (1 + price/mix) − 1. Decimals per year; compound, do not add (p.451).
2. Growth under inflation = (1 + price change) x (1 + volume change) − 1; same form for COGS with input-cost inflation (p.483, p.500).
3. Volume change ≈ −(price elasticity) x (retail price change). Linear shortcut (p.474).
4. Interest expense = rate x beginning gross debt; interest income = forecast cash x yield; floating rate = reference rate + spread (p.454).
5. Inventory = COGS x days inventory / 365; receivables = sales x DSO / 365; payables = COGS x DPO / 365. Restatement of the days ratios (p.459).
6. FCFF = EBIT(1 − t) + D&A − increase in working capital − capex (p.462).
7. Equity roll-forward = prior equity + net income + share-based compensation − dividends (p.461). Fixed assets roll as prior balance + capex − D&A (implied by the exhibit, not stated).
8. Cost of sales = fixed + variable cost per unit x volume; lower volume spreads fixed cost thinner, so margin falls (pp.474–475).
9. Average holding period = 1 / annual portfolio turnover (p.486).
10. ROIC is formally after-tax; the text sometimes uses a pre-tax version. Know which is meant (pp.448, 477).
11. Percent above or below trend = actual / trend − 1; normalized revenue from a least-squares linear fit on time (p.487).
12. Gross-margin hit from an input shock = (cost of sales x input share x price rise) / sales; divide by sales (p.501).

## Traps
- Adding volume and price percentages instead of compounding.
- Treating a margin that holds as profit that holds; highest margin and highest profit can be different strategies.
- Applying interest to ending rather than beginning debt.
- Getting the working-capital sign wrong: a fall in net working capital releases cash.
- Treating conservatism as only pessimism, or inside or outside view as always superior.
- Mixing up biases: anchoring to the old forecast is conservatism; asking only supporters is confirmation; trusting extra granularity is illusion of control; tight ranges are overconfidence; ignoring peer averages is representativeness.
- Treating government as a sixth Porter force.
- Using a peak or trough year as the terminal year, or mixing after-tax and pre-tax ROIC.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A beverage segment has revenue of 200m. Volume +5%, price/mix +3%, no FX. Growth = 1.05 x 1.03 − 1 = 8.15%, revenue 216.3m. With price/mix +1%, growth = 6.05%, revenue 212.1m.
2. Revenue 100, COGS 60. Input costs +5%, volume −1%. (a) Price +2%: revenue 100.98, COGS 62.37, gross profit 38.61, margin 38.24%. (b) Price +5%: revenue 103.95, gross profit 41.58, margin 40.0%.
3. COGS = 400; days inventory 120 gives 400 x 120/365 = 131.5. At 100 days, inventory = 109.6, a cash inflow of 21.9.
