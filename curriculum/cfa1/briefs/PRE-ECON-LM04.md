2. Describe types of unemployment and compare unemployment measures (p.145–148).
3. Explain inflation, hyperinflation, disinflation and deflation (p.149–150).
4. Explain how inflation indexes are constructed (p.150–151).
5. Compare inflation measures, uses and limitations (p.151–154).
6. Contrast cost-push and demand-pull inflation (p.155–159).

## Core principles
- Households drive the cycle through spending. Income, job prospects and confidence set willingness to spend. Durables are most cyclical (long-lived, purchase easily postponed), services in between, non-durables least; all less volatile than business investment. Baseline spending follows permanent income, durables follow current disposable income. Failure case: a one-off windfall mistaken for lasting income (p.138–140).
- A rising saving rate signals caution now but stores spending power for recovery (p.140).
- Housing is small but swings hard because buyers borrow: low loan rates cut payments and lift sales and construction. Late in expansions prices and rates outrun incomes, unsold inventory builds, construction falls. Buyers chasing expected gains can extend upswings and deepen corrections. Household formation (mostly ages 25–40) sets the long-run trend (p.141–142).
- Imports follow domestic growth, exports follow trading partners' growth. Appreciation makes imports cheaper and exports dearer to foreigners, so imports rise and exports fall; depreciation reverses it. Only persistent moves matter; growth differentials act faster than currency (p.142–144).
- Unemployment lags the cycle. Firms hesitate to lay off, and in recovery first use overtime, temps and slack in kept staff. Discouraged workers leave in bad times and return in recovery, so the rate can fall in a weak economy and rise early in a recovery. Hours, temps and productivity turn earlier (p.145–148). A very tight labor market with wage escalators can start a price-wage spiral, forcing central bank tightening and risking deep recession (p.145).
- Deflation hurts because debts are fixed in money terms: real debt burden rises while revenue shrinks, so firms cut spending and jobs. Hence developed-economy central banks prefer low positive inflation, around 2% (p.150). Hyperinflation typically follows money-financed government spending; cash is dumped, velocity rises, fix is controlling money supply (p.150).
- Cost-push (rising costs, mainly wages, force prices up) and demand-pull (demand pushes prices, wages follow) share a root: demand exceeding capacity, showing first at labor, commodity or output bottlenecks. Cost-push gauges: unemployment vs NAIRU, wages, unit labor cost. Demand-pull gauges: actual vs potential GDP, capacity utilization, commodity prices. Monetarism is a special case of demand-pull. Embedded expectations sustain inflation after its cause is gone, producing stagflation as in the 1970s (p.155–159).
- Fixed-basket indexes overstate inflation (substitution, quality, new-product bias); the index should fit the purpose: policy, indexed payments, real GDP, industry costs (p.151–154).

## Vocabulary
- Permanent income — income households judge sustainable; anchors baseline spending (p.139).
- Precautionary saving — extra saving because income looks uncertain (p.140).
- Household formation — new households forming; key long-run housing driver (p.141).
- Labor force — employed plus those actively seeking work (p.145–146).
- Frictional vs long-term unemployed — between jobs, short-term vs out of work for months yet searching (p.146).
- Underemployed / discouraged worker — working below capacity, counted employed / stopped searching, outside labor force (p.146).
- Hidden unemployment — discouraged plus underemployed (p.146).
- Price-wage spiral — wages and prices pushing each other up (p.145).
- Disinflation — inflation falls but stays positive (p.150).
- Stagflation — high inflation with weak growth and high unemployment (p.149).
- Laspeyres index — fixed base-period basket (p.151).
- Hedonic pricing / chained index — quality adjustment / updated basket to reduce bias (p.151).
- Headline vs core — all items vs excluding food and energy (p.153–155).
- GDP deflator — price index of domestic output; deflates nominal GDP (p.155).
- NAIRU / NARU — unemployment rate below which wage pressure builds; unobservable (p.156).
- Breakeven inflation — nominal minus inflation-linked yield (p.159).

## Formulas
- Laspeyres index = [Σ q0·pt / Σ q0·p0] × 100. q0 base quantities, p prices (currency/unit); unitless, base 100. Fixed basket assumed. Intuition: reprice the old basket. The book shows only the arithmetic; the sum form generalises it (p.151, PDF 159).
- Inflation rate = Index_t / Index_(t−1) − 1 (p.151, PDF 159).
- Unemployment rate = unemployed / labor force (p.146, PDF 154).
- Participation rate = labor force / working-age population (p.146, PDF 154).
- ULC = W / O. W = compensation per hour per worker; O = output per hour per worker; currency per unit. Rises when pay outpaces productivity (p.157, PDF 165).
- Breakeven ≈ nominal yield − inflation-linked yield, same country and maturity. Approximate; ignores risk and liquidity premia and linker-market distortions (p.159, PDF 167).
- Velocity = nominal GDP / money supply (words only) (p.158, PDF 166).
- Paasche = [Σ qt·pt / Σ qt·p0] × 100 (current basket); Fisher = √(Laspeyres × Paasche). Both appear only in practice (p.166–167, PDF 174–175) and solutions (p.169–170, PDF 177–178), not taught in the body.

## Traps
- Disinflation is not deflation: prices still rise (p.150).
- A falling unemployment rate may just be discouraged workers leaving; a rising rate early in recovery may be re-entrants (p.147–148).
- Underemployed count as employed; discouraged workers are outside the labor force (p.146).
- Appreciation hurts exports and raises imports (p.143).
- Fixed-basket bias is upward, including unadjusted quality gains; with substitution Laspeyres exceeds Paasche, Fisher in between.
- High inflation alone is not stagflation (p.153–154). Real GDP needs the GDP deflator, not CPI (p.155). Headline is noisier than core, not biased.
- Falling velocity is ambiguous: money outgrowing GDP implies inflation risk, a cyclical GDP drop implies recovery (p.158). Hyperinflation raises velocity.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. Base basket 10 kg apples at $2.00 and 20 loaves at $1.00 (cost $40); next month apples $2.50, bread $1.10. Laspeyres = 47/40 × 100 = 117.5, inflation 17.5%. If shoppers switch to 6 kg apples and 22 loaves, Paasche = 39.2/34 × 100 = 115.3, below Laspeyres.
2. Working-age population 1,000, employed 570, unemployed 30: rate 5.0%, participation 60%. If 10 unemployed give up: 20/590 = 3.4%. In recovery they re-enter and 2 jobs go to unemployed people: unemployed 28, labor force 600, rate 4.7%, higher though employment rose.
3. Pay $30/hour, output 15 units/hour: ULC $2.00. Pay +4%, output +1%: 31.20/15.15 = $2.059, about +3.0%, a cost-push signal.

## Book slips and uncertainties
- The Summary gives a much lower hyperinflation rate than the body; trust the body (hundreds of percent a year).
- The Summary adds imported and demand (wartime) inflation not in the body, and has a typo in a wage-increase phrase.
- Solution 15 calls the Fisher index "chaining"; it is really a geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche.
- Not visually checked (text only): PDF 145, 148–150, 152–153, 155–158, 161–163, 168–176. Source page anchors drift slightly; notes verified placements.
