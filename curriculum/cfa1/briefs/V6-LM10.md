## Learning outcomes

2. Explain how holding-period return, Macaulay duration and the investment horizon relate, including which risk dominates and the duration gap. Investment Horizon, pp.249–254 (PDF 259–264).
3. Define, compute and interpret Macaulay duration. Concept at p.251 (PDF 261); calculation in Macaulay Duration, pp.255–261 (PDF 265–271).

## Core principles

- **YTM is a promise with three conditions.** An investor realizes the YTM only if every payment arrives (no default), coupons are reinvested at the YTM, and the bond is held to maturity. Break any one and realized return differs. pp.239–242, p.244.
- **Interest rate risk has two opposing halves.** If rates rise, the coupon recipient reinvests at better rates (gain) but a seller receives a lower price (loss). If rates fall, the reverse. The investor bears whichever half the horizon exposes. The analysis assumes one parallel rate move immediately after purchase that then stays put. p.240, pp.243–248.
- **A buy-and-hold investor still has risk.** Price risk disappears (redemption at par); reinvestment risk remains. p.240, p.247.
- **Horizon against Macaulay duration decides the winner.** Horizon longer than duration means reinvestment dominates (falling rates hurt). Equal means the effects roughly offset. Shorter means price risk dominates (rising rates hurt). The price jump from a rate shock fades with time through pull to par, while the reinvestment gain builds from zero; they cross at duration. p.251–253 (Exhibits 2 and 3).
- **Duration gap = Macaulay duration − horizon.** Positive gap: price risk, rising rates hurt. Negative: reinvestment risk, falling rates hurt. Near zero: roughly hedged against a one-time parallel shift. Duration and horizon both shrink with time, so a match drifts. p.253–254, p.258.
- **Macaulay duration is a weighted-average time.** Each cash flow's weight is its present value at the YTM divided by the full price, and the flow's time is when it arrives. It is below maturity for any coupon bond, because earlier coupons pull the average in. A zero-coupon bond has duration equal to maturity when valued on a coupon date. p.243, pp.255–256.
- **Interest income versus capital gain.** Interest income is time-driven (coupons, reinvestment, accretion). Capital gain or loss is sale price against the constant-yield carrying-value path, from a yield change only. pp.244–247.

## Vocabulary

- Reinvestment risk — uncertainty about the rate at which coupons are reinvested; hurts when rates fall (p.240).
- Price risk — sale price moves inversely with yield; hurts when rates rise (p.240).
- Horizon yield — annualized compound return from reinvested coupons plus sale or redemption proceeds, relative to the purchase price (p.244).
- Interest-on-interest — the part of coupon-reinvestment value earned on the coupons themselves (p.244).
- Constant-yield price trajectory — the price path if YTM never changes; selling on it produces no capital gain or loss (p.244).
- Carrying value — purchase price plus accumulated discount amortization (minus premium amortization) (pp.245, 248).
- Macaulay duration — present-value-weighted average time to receive the cash flows; the horizon where reinvestment and price effects offset (pp.251, 255).
- Duration gap — Macaulay duration minus investment horizon (p.253).
- Annualized Macaulay duration — duration in periods divided by coupons per year, expressed in years (p.256).
- Full price — flat price plus accrued interest; the denominator for the weights between coupon dates (p.257).

## Formulas

1. **Horizon yield:** r = [(FV of reinvested coupons + sale or redemption price) / purchase price]^(1/T) − 1. T in years, annual coupons, values per 100 par. Assumes no default, one immediate parallel rate change, a single reinvestment rate. pp.244, 247, 250.
2. **FV of reinvested annual coupons:** FV = C × [(1+i)^n − 1] / i. C coupon per 100, i reinvestment rate, n years (Excel −FV(i, n, C, 0, 0)). p.244.
3. **Sale price at horizon:** price = PV(new yield, years remaining at horizon, coupon, 100) (Excel −PV). Use years remaining, not original term. pp.245–246, p.250.
4. **Eq. 1, duration gap:** gap = Macaulay duration − investment horizon, same time units. Positive: price risk, rising rates hurt. Negative: reinvestment risk, falling rates hurt. Zero: offset for a one-time parallel shift. p.253.
5. **Eq. 2, Macaulay duration:** MacDur = Σ (k − t/T) × [CF_k / (1+r)^(k − t/T)] / PV_full, k = 1..N. CF_k = PMT, with the last flow PMT + FV. t = days since last coupon, T = days in coupon period, r = YTM per period, N = periods to maturity counted from the start of the current period. Result in periods; divide by coupons per year for years. pp.256–257.
6. **Eq. 3, closed form:** MacDur = (1+r)/r − [1 + r + N(c − r)] / [c((1+r)^N − 1) + r] − t/T. r and c per period, N periods, result in periods. p.258.
7. **Excel DURATION(settlement, maturity, coupon, yld, frequency, [basis]).** Basis 0 or omitted = 30/360, 1 = act/act, 2 = act/360, 3 = act/365. p.258.

## Traps

- Assuming YTM is the return you will earn. It requires hold to maturity, no default, and reinvestment at YTM (p.244).
- Saying buy-and-hold has no interest rate risk. Reinvestment risk remains (p.247).
- Thinking rising rates always hurt. They help a long-horizon investor and hurt a short-horizon one (p.253).
- Flipping the gap sign. Gap is duration minus horizon; positive means price risk and rising rates hurt (pp.253–254).
- Setting duration equal to maturity for a coupon bond. That holds only for a zero-coupon bond on a coupon date (pp.243, 256).
- Forgetting units on semiannual bonds. The table sum is in half-years; divide by 2 (p.256).
- Using 1 as the first time to receipt between coupon dates. Use 1 − t/T; weights stay the same but full price rises (p.261).
- Pricing the sale with the original term instead of years remaining at the horizon (pp.245–246).
- Believing a matched duration removes risk. It works approximately, for one parallel shift, and drifts as time passes (pp.251, 258).

## Original practice seeds

All hypothetical.

**A. Duration of a 3-year, 5% annual-coupon bond priced at par (YTM 5%).** PVs are 4.7619, 4.5351 and 90.7029 (total 100), giving weights 0.0476, 0.0454, 0.9070. Time × weight: 0.0476 + 0.0907 + 2.7211 = 2.8594 years, below the 3-year maturity.

**B. Same bond held 3 years; rates jump to 6% right after purchase.** FV of coupons = 5 × (1.06³ − 1)/0.06 = 15.918. Total 115.918, so r = 1.15918^(1/3) − 1 ≈ 5.05%, above 5%. If rates fall to 4%: FV = 15.608, r ≈ 4.95%. Horizon 3 exceeds duration 2.86, so falling rates hurt, slightly.

**C. Same bond, horizon 1 year, rates go to 6%.** Coupon 5 is received with nothing to reinvest. Sale price with 2 years left = 5/1.06 + 105/1.1236 = 98.167. Total 103.167 per 100, so the return is about 3.17%, versus 5.00% if rates stay. Horizon 1 is below 2.86, so price risk dominates and rising rates hurt a lot.
