## Learning outcomes
1. Explain how the cash flow statement connects to the income statement and balance sheet (Linkages, pp.125–130).
2. Describe the steps to build direct and indirect cash flow statements, including computing flows from income statement and balance sheet data (direct pp.131–136; indirect pp.137–139; investing pp.141–143; financing pp.144–145).
3. Show how to convert indirect-method operating cash flow into the direct format (pp.140–141).
4. Contrast IFRS and US GAAP cash flow statements (pp.146–148).

## Core principles
- **Stocks and flows.** The balance sheet is a snapshot; the income, cash flow (CFS) and equity statements cover a period and bridge snapshots. The CFS bridges beginning cash to ending cash (cash, equivalents, restricted cash) (pp.125–126). Cash ultimately pays investors and lenders, so the CFS tests whether profit is real.
- **Accrual-to-cash gap lives in working capital.** Uncollected revenue raises receivables; unpaid expense raises payables; cash received before delivery creates deferred revenue (a liability). The CFS moves only when cash moves (pp.127–130). Growing receivables with weak cash is an earnings-quality warning (p.128).
- **Direct method = gross receipts and payments.** Adjust each accrual line by the related balance-sheet change. A rise in an operating asset leaves cash worse than the accrual line; a rise in an operating liability leaves it better. Supplier payments take two steps: COGS adjusted for inventory gives purchases, then adjust for payables (pp.131–137).
- **Indirect method = reconcile net income to operating cash flow.** Remove non-operating gains and losses, add back non-cash charges, then adjust for working-capital changes. Both methods give the same operating cash flow; investing and financing are identical under either (pp.131, 137–139).
- **Conversion uses published data only.** Split net income into revenue and expenses, strip non-operating and non-cash items, then adjust each remaining line for its working-capital change. Precision is limited to disclosure (p.140).
- **Investing and financing come from roll-forwards.** Cost sold, accumulated depreciation removed and the gain or loss give proceeds. Dividends paid come from retained earnings, not the income statement. Changes in debt and common stock approximate issuance, repayment or buyback when nothing else is disclosed (pp.141–145).
- **Classification is a choice under IFRS.** US GAAP is rigid; IFRS lets firms place interest and dividends in different sections and allocate identifiable taxes to investing or financing. This shifts operating cash flow, not the total change in cash, so adjust before comparing firms (pp.146–148).

## Vocabulary
- Stock vs flow statements: balance sheet at a date; income, cash flow and equity statements over a period (p.125).
- Direct method: operating cash flow as gross receipts and payments (p.124).
- Indirect method: operating cash flow derived from net income by adjustments (p.124).
- Cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash: the balance the CFS reconciles (p.125).
- Deferred (unearned) revenue: cash received before the obligation is met; a liability (p.127).
- Working-capital accounts: current operating assets and liabilities whose changes explain the accrual-cash gap (p.138).
- Non-cash expense: depreciation, amortisation or depletion; cut net income without cash leaving (p.138).
- Book value of an asset sold: cost less accumulated depreciation (p.142).
- Bank overdraft: cash equivalent under IFRS, financing under US GAAP (p.146).

## Formulas
No numbered formulas; these roll-forwards are used. "Increase/decrease" means change over the period.
- Cash collected from customers = revenue − increase in receivables (+ decrease); equals beginning AR + revenue − ending AR. A deferred-revenue increase adds, a decrease subtracts (pp.132–133).
- Purchases = COGS + increase in inventory (− decrease). Cash paid to suppliers = purchases − increase in AP (+ decrease) (p.134).
- Cash paid to employees = salary expense − increase in salaries payable (p.135).
- Cash paid for other operating expense = expense + increase in prepaids (− decrease) − increase in accrued liabilities (+ decrease) (p.136).
- Cash paid for interest = interest expense − increase in interest payable (+ decrease) (pp.136–137).
- Cash paid for taxes = tax expense − increase in taxes payable and deferred tax liabilities + increase in tax receivable and deferred tax assets (p.137).
- CFO (indirect) = net income + non-cash expenses + non-operating losses − non-operating gains + increase in deferred tax liability (− decrease) ± working-capital changes (operating assets: −increase/+decrease; operating liabilities: +increase/−decrease) (pp.138–139).
- Cost of equipment sold = beginning cost + purchases − ending cost. Accumulated depreciation on sold items = beginning AD + depreciation − ending AD. Proceeds = cost − AD + gain (− loss) (p.142). Assumes all AD relates to equipment and no other additions or write-offs.
- Dividends paid = beginning RE + net income − ending RE (p.145). Assumes only net income and dividends moved retained earnings.
- Ending cash = beginning cash + CFO + CFI + CFF (p.126).

## Traps
- Indirect signs: an operating-asset increase is subtracted; an operating-liability increase is added (p.138).
- Depreciation is not a source of cash; it is added back only because it lowered net income (p.138).
- A gain on sale is subtracted from CFO, yet the full proceeds go to investing (pp.138, 142).
- Supplier payments need two adjustments; an inventory decrease lowers purchases, a payables increase lowers cash paid (p.134).
- A fall in a payable (interest, taxes) means cash paid exceeds the expense (p.137).
- Dividends paid are found from retained earnings and sit in financing under US GAAP (p.145).
- IFRS classification choices change CFO, not the net change in cash; interest paid shown in financing gives higher CFO (p.146).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical; USD, one fiscal year.
1. Retailer Zed: revenue 1,000; receivables 200 to 260; COGS 600; inventory 150 to 130; payables 90 to 120. Cash collected = 1,000 − 60 = 940. Purchases = 600 − 20 = 580. Cash paid to suppliers = 580 − 30 = 550. Cash gross margin = 940 − 550 = 390.
2. Same Zed, but receivables rise only 10 (to 210): cash collected = 990, which is 50 more, so CFO is 50 higher, all else equal.
3. Asset sale: equipment cost 500 at start, 100 bought, 450 at end, so cost sold = 150. AD 200 at start, depreciation 60, 220 at end, so AD on sold items = 40. Book value 110. With a loss of 15, proceeds = 95. Indirect CFO adds back 15; investing shows +95.
