## Core principles
- **Forms differ by who bears risk, who is taxed and how much capital is reachable.** A sole proprietor funds everything, keeps all profit and answers personally for all debts, so growth is capped by one person's wealth and risk appetite. p.7 (PDF 15).
- **Partnership liability depends on role.** General partners manage and carry unlimited, shared liability, so one partner's default lands on the others. Limited partners risk only their investment and usually do not manage. An LLP (only some jurisdictions) limits everyone and shares management. pp.7–9 (PDF 15–17).
- **Pass-through versus double taxation.** Proprietors and partners are taxed personally on profit earned, paid out or not. A corporation pays tax on profit, then shareholders pay again on dividends. The second layer only bites on distributed profit, so retaining and reinvesting avoids it. It fails if the jurisdiction gives dividend credits or exemptions. pp.9–10, 18–20 (PDF 17–18, 26–28).
- **Legal identity plus owner–manager separation lets a corporation raise large sums.** The corporation contracts, borrows and pays tax in its own name. Shareholders elect the board; the board appoints executives. Passive investors fund the firm without running it; shareholder discipline works through voting, which is slow. p.15 (PDF 23).
- **Limited liability caps a shareholder's loss at the amount invested.** Creditors cannot pursue shareholders unless they personally guaranteed the debt. Risk and return are shared pro rata to shares. p.16 (PDF 24).
- **Two funding sources.** Equity (share sales, retained profit) holds a residual claim; debt holds contractual interest and repayment on set dates. Economic profit is return above owners' required return, so it can be negative while net income is positive. pp.16–18 (PDF 24–26).
- **"Public" means exchange-listed.** Listing brings visible prices, secondary-market liquidity and broad capital, at the cost of registration and regular audited disclosure. Private shares have no visible price, need a negotiated buyer and may carry transfer limits, but the firm avoids disclosure cost and keeps control. A public limited company need not be listed. pp.21–25 (PDF 29–33).
- **Routes to listing and back.** An IPO sells new shares through an underwriter and raises capital. A direct listing lists existing shares, with no underwriter and no new capital. Acquisition by a listed company (including via a SPAC) also works. A take-private buyer pays a premium, often with debt, and needs value gains to exceed premium plus financing cost. pp.26–29 (PDF 34–37).

## Vocabulary
- Sole proprietorship — one owner, no separate legal identity, unlimited liability (p.7).
- General partnership — all partners manage and share unlimited liability (pp.7–8).
- Limited partnership — GP with unlimited liability plus LPs capped at investment (p.8).
- LLP — all partners limited, shared management; jurisdiction-dependent (p.9).
- Pass-through entity — no entity tax; owners taxed on profit as earned (p.9).
- Private limited company — shares and limited liability, often capped owner count and restricted transfers (pp.9–10).
- Public limited company — limited liability, no legal cap on owners, entity-level tax (p.10).
- Articles of incorporation — filing that creates a corporation (p.15).
- Limited liability — loss cannot exceed amount invested (p.16).
- Double taxation — tax on profit, then again on dividends paid from it (p.18).
- Economic profit — return above owners' required return on equity (pp.17–18).
- Free float — shares actually available to trade, excluding insider and strategic holdings (p.22).
- Private placement — sale to a few qualified investors without a public offering (p.24).
- Accredited investor — one judged able to bear private-offering risk (p.24).
- SEC / EDGAR / ESEF — US regulator, its filing database, EU reporting format (p.24).
- Underwriter — investment bank placing new shares in an IPO (p.27).
- Direct listing — listing existing shares without issuance (p.27).
- SPAC — listed shell holding IPO cash in trust, returned if no deal by a deadline (p.28).
- Take-private — buyout of all public shares, then delisting (p.29).

## Formulas
No formal equations in the book; these calculation patterns are verified in the notes.
- Total tax rate on fully distributed profit = (corporate tax + dividend tax) / pre-tax income; corporate tax = pre-tax income x corporate rate; dividend tax = dividends x personal dividend rate. Currency, then percent. Assumes full payout, one personal rate, no credits. The second tax applies to what the first left. p.19 (PDF 27).
- Pass-through tax = pre-tax income x personal rate, due whether or not distributed. One layer, on all profit. p.20 (PDF 28).
- Effective look-through ownership = upstream ownership x downstream ownership. Percent; assumes a simple chain. Stakes multiply down a chain. p.36 (PDF 44).
- Economic profit: concept only (net income / equity versus required return), no equation. pp.17–18.

## Traps
- "Public" is not "government-owned" (p.5).
- Public limited company is not necessarily listed (p.21).
- Shareholders elect the board; they do not hire the CEO (p.15).
- Double taxation hits distributed profit only; check the payout assumption (p.18, p.20).
- Pass-through means taxed on income earned, not paid out (p.9).
- The GP of a limited partnership keeps unlimited liability; managers shield themselves by making a limited-liability corporation the GP (pp.8, 12–13, 16).
- A direct listing raises no capital and has no underwriter (p.27).
- Private shares are illiquid and opaque; that is not the same as being worth less (p.23).
- Taxable profit is not accounting profit; economic profit is not net income (pp.17–18).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A firm earns 200 currency units pre-tax in a year; corporate rate 25%; all after-tax profit paid out; dividend rate 20%. Tax 50 + 30 = 80, so 40% of pre-tax income. As a pass-through at 30% personal: 60, which is 20 lower.
2. Same firm retains all 150 of after-tax profit: total tax 50, rate 25%. Paying it out lifts the rate to 40%.
3. A family owns 40% of a listed GP corporation, which owns 70% of the LP holding the assets: 0.40 x 0.70 = 28%. At 80% of the LP: 32%.
