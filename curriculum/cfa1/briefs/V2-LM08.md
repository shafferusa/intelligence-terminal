## Learning outcomes
1. Compute and interpret a cross-rate from two quotes that share a third currency. Cross-Rate Calculations, pp.252–255 (PDF 260–263).
2. Explain the no-arbitrage link between spot, forward and the two interest rates; compute a forward rate from points or a percentage; interpret a forward discount or premium. Forward Rate Calculations with its two subsections, pp.256–263 (PDF 264–271).

## Core principles
- **Cross-rates come from chaining.** Dealers quote majors against the USD; a pair that is not quoted directly is built by multiplying two quotes so the shared currency cancels. If the shared currency sits on the same side in both quotes, invert one first. Direction: buy the base from the cheaper dealer, sell to the dearer; profit is in the price currency (p.266). Risk angle: a dealer who quotes a cross-rate out of line with the two underlying quotes hands a trader a riskless profit (triangular arbitrage), and that trading pulls prices back into line. pp.252–253 (PDF 260–261).
- **Percentage change depends on which currency is the base.** In a quote P/B, the base appreciates when the quote rises. For the change in the price currency, invert the quote and recompute. A falling quote means the base is weakening. p.254 (PDF 262).
- **Forward quotes are points added to spot.** Points are F minus S, scaled to the last decimal place of the quote. Positive points mean the base currency is at a forward premium; negative points mean a forward discount. Points grow with maturity and with the interest-rate gap. pp.256–257 (PDF 264–265).
- **The forward is set by arbitrage, not by opinion.** A domestic investor can deposit at the domestic rate, or convert at spot, deposit abroad, and sell the proceeds forward (swap financing). Both are riskless, so they must pay the same. If the forward is off, you borrow in the cheaper route and invest in the dearer one and pocket the gap. This needs riskless deposits, no transaction costs and no capital controls. pp.257–259 (PDF 265–267).
- **Higher-rate currency trades at a forward discount.** The high-rate currency earns more interest, so it must be cheaper forward to stop a free lunch. A forward discount reflects the rate gap; it is not a forecast of depreciation, and forwards are poor predictors of future spot. pp.259–260 (PDF 267–268); p.261–262 (PDF 269–270).
- **Maturity scaling is only approximate.** Points rise roughly, not exactly, in proportion to the horizon, because the denominator contains the domestic rate. Use the actual/360 fraction for Libor-style deposits. pp.260–261 (PDF 268–269).

## Vocabulary
- Cross-rate — exchange rate for two currencies derived from each one's quote against a third (p.252).
- Triangular arbitrage — riskless profit from a cross-rate inconsistent with its two underlying quotes (p.253).
- Forward points / swap points / pips — F minus S scaled to the last decimal of the spot quote (p.256).
- Forward premium — base currency's forward rate is above spot (p.256).
- Forward discount — base currency's forward rate is below spot (p.256).
- Swap financing — convert at spot, invest abroad, then convert back at the forward rate (pp.257–258).
- Arbitrage relationship — hedged foreign return must equal the domestic return, for the same tenor (p.258).
- Domestic (d) and foreign (f) here — tied to the quote: f/d means foreign per domestic, so d is the base and f the price currency (pp.257–258).
- Libor and actual/360 — deposit benchmark and day-count basis used for the horizon fraction (p.260).
- Forward as expected spot — reading the forward as a forecast; the text warns against it (p.259).

## Formulas
1. **Cross-rate:** A/C = A/B × B/C; invert a quote if the common currency is on the same side. Units: price per base. Mid-style single quotes, no spread; assumes no arbitrage. Intuition: the shared currency cancels. p.252 (inversion case p.253).
2. **% change of base in quote P/B:** S_new/S_old − 1. Price-currency change: S_old/S_new − 1 (the inverse quote's change). Intuition: appreciation is measured on the base. p.254; p.256.
3. **Forward points:** (F − S) × 10,000 for four-decimal pairs; × 100 for yen-type pairs. Intuition: express the gap in the last quoted digit. p.256.
4. **Forward from points:** F = S + points/10,000 (or /100). p.257.
5. **Forward from percent:** F = S × (1 + %); % = F/S − 1. p.257.
6. **Arbitrage identity (f/d quote, one period):** 1 + r_d = S_f/d × (1 + r_f) × (1/F_f/d). r = riskless rate for the same tenor; S and F in foreign units per domestic unit. Needs riskless deposits, no costs, no capital controls. Intuition: the hedged foreign route equals the domestic deposit. p.258.
7. **Forward rate:** F_f/d = S_f/d × (1 + r_f)/(1 + r_d). Higher-rate currency at a forward discount. p.258; discount statement p.259.
8. **Forward/spot ratio:** F/S = (1 + r_f)/(1 + r_d). p.259.
9. **Expected-spot reading (F = S_t+1):** %ΔS = (r_f − r_d)/(1 + r_d). Interpret with caution; forwards forecast poorly. p.259.
10. **With horizon:** F = S × (1 + r_f·tau)/(1 + r_d·tau), tau = days/360. p.260.
11. **Swap points:** F − S = S × [(r_f − r_d)/(1 + r_d·tau)] × tau. Proportional to S and the rate gap, approximately to tau. p.260 (numbers p.261).

Notation: d and f follow the quote (d = base, f = price currency), not the investor's home. Always map r_d to the base currency's rate.

## Traps
- Forgetting to invert when the common currency is on the same side (p.252).
- Swapping r_d and r_f: tie r_d to the base and r_f to the price currency of the quote (p.258; Practice Q6 solution, p.266).
- Reading a forward discount as an expected depreciation; it reflects the rate gap (pp.259–260; p.261–262).
- Wrong scaling: 10,000 for four-decimal quotes, 100 for yen-type quotes (p.256; Q7, pp.265–266).
- Dropping the horizon fraction, for example treating a multi-month deposit as though it were a full year (pp.262–263).
- Assuming points are exactly proportional to maturity; multiplying short-horizon points by the day ratio only approximates the longer-horizon points (p.261).
- Wrong arbitrage direction: buy the base where it is cheap, sell where it is dear; profit is in the price currency (solutions, p.266).
- Recovering spot from a forward and a percentage: divide by (1 + %), not subtract (solutions, p.266).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.

**A. Cross-rate with an input change.** Same-moment quotes: JPY/USD 150.00 and CHF/USD 0.9000. JPY/CHF = 150.00 / 0.9000 = 166.67 yen per franc. If CHF/USD falls to 0.8800 with JPY/USD unchanged, JPY/CHF = 150/0.88 = 170.45, so a franc buys more yen (the franc is stronger against the USD).

**B. Forward from rates.** USD/GBP spot 1.2000 (USD per GBP; base GBP = d, price USD = f). 90-day rates: USD 4.00%, GBP 5.00% per year; tau = 0.25. F = 1.2000 × 1.0100/1.0125 = 1.19704; points = −29.6. GBP, the higher-rate currency, is at a forward discount. If the USD rate falls to 3.00%, F = 1.19407 and points = −59.3: the rate gap doubled and the points roughly doubled.

**C. Points and percent.** JPY/EUR spot 150.00, six-month points −85. F = 150.00 − 85/100 = 149.15. Percent = −0.85/150.00 = −0.567%. The euro (base) is at a forward discount, implying euro rates are above yen rates.
