## Learning outcomes
1. Analyze and interpret reported and common-size cash flow statements. Taught in Evaluating Sources and Uses of Cash (pp.156–159) and Ratios and Common-Size Analysis (pp.160–165); listed p.155.
2. Calculate and interpret FCFF, FCFE and the performance and coverage cash flow ratios. Taught in Free Cash Flow Measures (pp.166–168) and Cash Flow Ratios (pp.168–170).

## Core principles
- **Sources and uses first (p.156).** Ask which of operating, investing and financing activities supplies cash and which consumes it. A mature firm should fund itself from operations. Negative CFO must be plugged with new debt or equity, whose providers eventually expect repayment from operating cash, so it is tolerable only briefly (for example a fast-growing firm building inventory and extending credit).
- **Surplus operating cash has two destinations (p.156).** Reinvest it if value-creating projects exist; otherwise return it to capital providers. Healthy pattern: CFO positive and enough to cover capex.
- **CFO versus net income is an earnings-quality test (p.157).** Net income is reduced by non-cash charges like depreciation, so for a mature firm CFO normally exceeds net income. The warning sign is the reverse: strong profit with weak cash, possibly aggressive accounting. Working-capital swings can also flatter CFO.
- **Investing and financing are read line by line (pp.157–159).** Investing: capex, acquisitions, purchases and sales of securities and assets; ask how big outlays are financed and why assets are sold. Financing: raising or repaying, in what form, when debt falls due, and how capital is returned to owners.
- **Common-size cash flow statements (pp.160–165).** Method one divides each inflow by total inflows and each outflow by total outflows. Method two divides every line by net revenue, showing trends and letting an analyst project revenue-linked items once revenue is forecast. Under the indirect method only net CFO appears, so operating receipts and payments cannot be separately common-sized.
- **FCFF versus FCFE (pp.166–168).** FCFF is cash left for all capital providers after operating costs, taxes and required investment, so interest is added back, after tax, because interest paid already lowered taxes inside CFO. FCFE is what remains for common shareholders after servicing debt, so it adds net borrowing (or subtracts net repayment). Positive FCFE is cash available to distribute.
- **Performance versus coverage ratios (pp.168–170).** Performance ratios gauge cash-based profitability; coverage ratios gauge ability to pay obligations from cash. Asset-based ratios need care: a large pile of financial assets built from past cash generation enlarges the denominator without implying weak operations (p.170).

## Vocabulary
- Sources and uses of cash — inflows and outflows across the three activities (p.156).
- Earnings quality (CFO vs net income) — how well profit is backed by cash (p.157).
- Common-size cash flow statement — restated as percentages of totals or of revenue (p.160).
- Free cash flow — generically, CFO minus capex (p.166).
- FCFF — cash available to debt and equity holders after operating needs, taxes and investment (p.166).
- FCFE — cash available to common shareholders after debt service and investment (p.167).
- NCC — non-cash charges such as depreciation and amortization (p.167).
- FCInv — fixed-capital investment (capex); WCInv — investment in working capital (p.167).
- Net borrowing — new debt raised minus debt repaid (p.167).
- Performance ratios — cash-based profitability; coverage ratios — cash-based solvency and payment ability (p.168).

## Formulas
1. **FCFF (from net income)** = NI + NCC + Int(1 − Tax rate) − FCInv − WCInv (pp.166–167; PDF 178–179). NI net income; Int interest expense; currency units.
2. **FCFF (from CFO)** = CFO + Int(1 − Tax rate) − FCInv (p.167; PDF 179). Assumes interest paid is inside CFO. If interest paid is classified in financing (IFRS option), do not add it back. Under IFRS also add back interest and dividends received if shown in investing, and dividends paid if shown in operating. FCInv is net of proceeds from equipment sales.
3. **FCFE** = CFO − FCInv + Net borrowing; with net repayment, CFO − FCInv − Net debt repayment (p.167; PDF 179). This simple version ignores preferred dividends.
4. **Common-size**: line item / total inflows (or outflows) × 100; or line item / net revenue × 100 (pp.160–163).
5. **Cash flow ratios (Exhibit 9, pp.168–169; PDF 180–181).** Averages use beginning and ending balances by convention.
   - Performance: Cash flow to revenue = CFO / net revenue. Cash return on assets = CFO / average total assets. Cash return on equity = CFO / average shareholders' equity. Cash to income = CFO / operating income. Cash flow per share = (CFO − preferred dividends) / common shares outstanding.
   - Coverage: Debt coverage = CFO / total debt. Interest coverage = (CFO + interest paid + taxes paid) / interest paid. Reinvestment = CFO / cash paid for long-term assets. Debt payment = CFO / cash paid for long-term debt repayment. Dividend payment = CFO / dividends paid. Investing and financing = CFO / cash outflows for investing and financing activities.
   - Footnotes: under IFRS with dividends in operating, add them back before subtracting preferred dividends; under IFRS with interest paid in financing, do not add interest back in the interest coverage numerator.

## Traps
- Adding interest back gross rather than after tax (p.167).
- Adding interest back when it already sits in financing (p.167).
- Wrong denominator in common-size work: separate inflow and outflow totals for method one, one revenue figure for method two (p.160).
- Common-sizing gross receipts and payments under the indirect method (p.161).
- Treating CFO above net income as suspicious; the red flag is net income well above CFO (p.157).
- Forgetting that FCFE deducts debt repayment (and adds borrowing) while FCFF does not (p.167).
- Skipping the preferred-dividend deduction in cash flow per share (p.169).
- Reading a falling cash return on assets as weak operations when the asset base swelled with financial holdings (p.170).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
- **A. FCFF and FCFE.** Firm X, one year, USD millions: CFO 500 (interest already deducted), interest paid 40, tax rate 25%, net capex 180, borrowed 100, repaid 60. FCFF = 500 + 40(0.75) − 180 = 350. FCFE = 500 − 180 + (100 − 60) = 360. If the tax rate rises to 40%, the add-back is 24 and FCFF = 344, down 6.
- **B. Common-size forecasting.** Firm Y, revenue 2,000, CFO 300 (15%), capex 120 (6%). With revenue forecast at 2,400 and margins unchanged, CFO = 360, capex = 144, free cash flow = 216 (versus 180 now). If the CFO margin slips to 12%, CFO = 288 and free cash flow = 144.
- **C. Cash interest coverage.** Firm Z: CFO 300, interest paid 50, taxes paid 70, so coverage = (300 + 50 + 70)/50 = 8.4 times. In a stress year with interest paid 100 and CFO 250, coverage = (250 + 100 + 70)/100 = 4.2 times, half as much.
