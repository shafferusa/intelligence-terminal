## Learning outcomes
1. Describe how the Professional Conduct Program is organised and how the Code and Standards are enforced (Preface, pp.37–38, PDF 51–52).
2. Name the six Code of Ethics components and the seven Standards (pp.43–47, PDF 57–61).
3. Explain the responsibilities each Standard imposes, including sub-sections (pp.44–47, PDF 58–61, plus 2023 changes pp.35–37, PDF 49–51).

## Core principles
- Code vs Standards: the Code is a short list of aspirational principles; the Standards are enforceable conduct rules. They are read together. The Handbook interprets them (p.34, PDF 48).
- Why ethics matters: markets work only if savers trust fairness and transparency. If trust falls, investors stay out or demand a higher risk premium, so less capital flows and funding costs rise. Failure case: a firm that meets only the letter of the law and lacks a culture of integrity (pp.39–42, PDF 53–56).
- Ethics goes beyond law: law says what is required, ethics what is right. Law cannot cover everything, regulators have limited resources, and some people dodge rules. So judgment and an ethical decision framework matter, and senior management's culture is probably the biggest single factor (pp.41–42, PDF 55–56).
- Small acts aggregate: individually harmless decisions can sum to a crisis; pay structures must not nudge decent people toward misconduct; clients come before own and employer interests (p.40, PDF 54).
- 2023 changes: competence becomes explicit Standard I(E) (no prescribed continuing-education programme; expectation depends on role); V(B) adds disclosure of service nature and costs, so clients can decide whether to hire a firm; VI(A) becomes "Avoid or Disclose Conflicts": avoid when reasonable, otherwise disclose prominently, plainly and effectively (pp.35–37, PDF 49–51).
- Enforcement: Professional Conduct staff investigate; the Disciplinary Review Committee (volunteer charterholders) decides contested cases and reviews agreed settlements. Inquiries come from the annual self-disclosure, complaints, media/regulator/public sources and exam monitoring. Sanctions: public censure, suspension, revocation; candidates can be suspended or barred (pp.37–38, PDF 51–52).
- Structure of the Standards: I Professionalism, II Integrity of Capital Markets, III Duties to Clients, IV Duties to Employers, V Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions, VI Conflicts of Interest, VII Responsibilities as a Member or Candidate (pp.44–47, PDF 58–61).- Scope: individuals worldwide, regardless of job or local law; members and candidates are personally responsible (p.43, PDF 57).

## Vocabulary
- Code of Ethics — six aspirational principles (p.43, PDF 57).
- Standards of Professional Conduct — seven enforceable rules with sub-sections (pp.44–47).
- Standards of Practice Handbook — interpretation, compliance guidance, fictional applications (p.34, PDF 48).
- Professional Conduct (PC) staff — investigators of alleged misconduct (p.37).
- Disciplinary Review Committee (DRC) — volunteer panels for contested charges and settlement review (pp.37–38).
- Professional Conduct Statement — annual self-disclosure by members and candidates (p.37).
- Standards of Practice Council (SPC) — volunteers who maintain and interpret the Code and Standards (p.39, PDF 53).
- Asset Manager Code — firm-level code in six areas (p.38, PDF 52).
- Ethics — principles guiding conduct that affects others (p.39, PDF 53).
- Culture of integrity — firm-wide environment beyond mere legal compliance (pp.39, 42).
- Market sustainability — long-term health of markets, weighed in decisions (p.40).
- Material nonpublic information — undisclosed information that could move an investment's value (p.44, PDF 58).
- Market manipulation — distorting prices or inflating volume to mislead (p.44).
- Beneficial owner — one with an ownership interest, relevant to Priority of Transactions (p.47; used, not defined here).
- Referral fee — payment for recommending a product or service; disclose it (p.47).

## Traps
- Legal does not mean ethical (p.41, PDF 55).
- Code is not the Standards: aspirational vs enforceable (p.34).
- VI(A): avoid where reasonable, otherwise disclose; it is not "always avoid" (p.37; p.46).
- I(E) sets no required continuing-education programme (p.36).
- Candidates (CFA and CIPM) are bound too, not just members (p.37).
- Compliance claims must state that CFA Institute has not verified them (p.38).
- DRC panels hear only contested cases, plus a reasonableness review of agreed ones (pp.37–38).
- Placement: IV(B) covers extra compensation arrangements (written consent from all parties); independence and gifts sit in I(B); performance presentation is III(D); fact vs opinion is within V(B).
- Know the numbering: I A–E (5), II A–B (2), III A–E (5), IV A–C (3), V A–C (3), VI A–C (3), VII A–B (2) = 23.

## Original practice seeds
1. Hypothetical: an analyst knows of a material, unannounced merger and urges a friend to buy the target first, never trading personally. II(A) is still violated because the analyst caused another to act. If the news were public or immaterial, II(A) would not apply.
2. Hypothetical: an adviser charges 1.00% a year on 500,000 (start-of-year value): fee = 500,000 × 0.0100 = 5,000. At 1.50% it is 500,000 × 0.0150 = 7,500, a 2,500 yearly gap a prospect would weigh. This is why revised V(B) requires cost disclosure.
3. Hypothetical: local law permits gifts up to 200 each, but a professional association's rule that also binds the member bans all gifts. Under I(A) the stricter rule governs, so the member accepts none. If the law were stricter, it would govern.
