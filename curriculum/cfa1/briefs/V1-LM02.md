## Core principles
- **Two sources of return.** Price change (uncertain) plus cash distributions. Distributions are fixed (bond coupons) or variable (dividends, floating coupons). A "fixed" payout is only as good as the issuer's solvency (p.49).
- **Dividends are not free money.** On payment the share price is expected to fall by about the dividend, so the two offset at that moment (p.50).
- **Equity versus debt.** Equity pays discretionary dividends plus price change. Debt pays contractual coupons and principal. Bond prices move with credit quality, rates and maturity: rates up, price down, longer maturity more sensitive. Hybrids sit between (pp.50–52).
- **Indicators are measuring sticks, not assets.** Rates, FX rates and indexes pay nothing themselves. A short-dated government bill proxies the risk-free rate. Annualise by compounding, not simple multiplication; the day-count convention shifts the answer slightly (pp.53–54).
- **Currency adds a second risk.** Local return and exchange-rate move combine multiplicatively; a local gain can become a home-currency loss if the foreign currency weakens enough (pp.54–55).
- **Compounding beats averaging.** The arithmetic mean overstates growth when returns are volatile, so the more volatile asset can win on arithmetic mean yet lose on geometric mean. Compare strategies on the geometric mean (pp.57–58).
- **Long-run ordering and its limits.** Over long US history equities earned most (mainly via price appreciation), bonds mainly via coupons, T-bills about inflation. Realised corporate bond returns can trail Treasuries because defaults erode higher quoted yields. Data are past, before costs, unadjusted for tax and inflation (pp.58–61).
- **Risk and premium.** Risk is the gap between expected and realised return. Systematic (macro, not diversifiable): inflation, interest rate, market. Unsystematic (micro, diversifiable): issuer (business, financial, credit) and sector. A high return for little risk would attract demand, raise price and cut expected return, so investors earn a premium only for more risk. The link is directional, not proportional, in small samples (pp.62–64).
- **Portfolio return is a weighted sum**, with weights applied separately to price and distribution components; single period, start weights (p.65).

## Vocabulary
- Price return — change in market price alone (p.49).
- Capital distribution return — cash paid to holders, e.g. dividends or coupons (p.49).
- Fixed / variable distribution — payout set in advance (bonds) vs changing (dividends, floating coupons) (p.49).
- Cash — notes, coins and deposits; earns nothing until invested (p.50).
- Amortising bond — repays principal in instalments (p.51).
- Hybrid — between debt and equity, e.g. preferred shares, convertibles (p.52).
- Financial instrument — standardised tradable contract on an asset (p.52).
- Derivative — value comes from an underlying asset or indicator, e.g. a call option (p.52).
- Financial indicator — interest rate, FX rate or index; no direct cash flows (p.53).
- Risk-free rate — proxied by a short-term government bill (p.53).
- Foreign exchange rate — price of one currency in another (p.54).
- Market index — summary figure tracking a basket of securities (p.55).
- Small-cap premium — small-cap growth relative to large-cap; can be negative (p.60).
- Share buyback — firm repurchases its own stock instead of paying dividends (p.61).
- Macro (systematic) vs micro (unsystematic) risk (p.62).
- Range — highest minus lowest annual return (p.63).
- Risk premium — extra return expected for bearing more risk (p.63).
- Risk–return trade-off — higher expected return requires more risk (p.64).

## Formulas
1. **Holding period return:** r = (P1 − P0 + Inc) / P0. Result is per period. (p.53, PDF 63)
2. **Annualisation by compounding:** r_annual = (1 + r_period)^c − 1, c = periods per year (e.g. 365 / days held). Assumes reinvestment at the same rate; 365 vs 360 days changes the figure. (pp.53–54, PDF 63–64)
3. **Currency-adjusted return:** r_(invest,USD) = (1 + r_(invest,EUR)) × (1 + r_(1 EUR,USD)) − 1. The second factor is S1/S0 − 1 with S = USD per EUR; if quoted EUR per USD, use the reciprocal. Multiplicative, not additive. (p.55, PDF 65; quote convention p.54, PDF 64)
4. **Arithmetic mean:** r_bar = (r1 + … + rT) / T. Ignores compounding. (p.57, PDF 67)
5. **Geometric mean:** r_G = [(1 + r1)(1 + r2)…(1 + rT)]^(1/T) − 1. (p.57, PDF 67)
6. **Relative performance:** (1 + r_A) / (1 + r_B) − 1. Ratio of growth factors, not a subtraction. (p.60, PDF 70; market risk premium p.68, PDF 78)
7. **Portfolio return:** r_p = Σ w_i × r_i, weights sum to 100%, single period. (p.65, PDF 75)
8. **Range** = highest annual return − lowest annual return. (p.63, PDF 73)

## Traps
- Multiplying a short-period return by periods per year instead of compounding; a 360-day basis gives a slightly lower figure than 365.
- Adding the asset return and the currency move. Multiply growth factors, after checking which currency is the "price" in the quote.
- Treating the arithmetic mean as growth. A volatile series has arithmetic mean above geometric, and rankings can flip.
- Subtracting returns for outperformance when the ratio form is used; answers differ in the second decimal.
- Calling sector risk "market risk". It is unsystematic and diversifiable; only inflation, interest rate and market risk are systematic.
- Reading a wider range as proof of higher return. It signals higher risk; return is only expected to be higher.
- Forgetting that interest rates act on bonds in two ways: through the price of existing bonds and through the coupon income on newly issued ones (p.62).
- Assuming corporate bonds always beat government bonds in realised returns; defaults can reverse the quoted-yield edge (p.60).

## Original practice seeds
1. **Hypothetical (one year, per-100 prices, no reinvestment).** Bond bought at 100, one coupon of 4, ends at 98. Total = (98 − 100 + 4)/100 = 2.0%; price return −2.0%; distribution +4.0%. If it ended at 95: (95 − 100 + 4)/100 = −1.0%.
2. **Hypothetical (one year).** A USD investor holds a EUR asset returning +5% in EUR. EUR falls 4% vs USD: 1.05 × 0.96 − 1 = 0.8%. EUR rises 4%: 1.05 × 1.04 − 1 = 9.2%. Neither is 5 − 4 or 5 + 4.
3. **Hypothetical (two years, no flows).** A fund gains 50% then loses 50%. Arithmetic mean = 0%. Geometric = (1.5 × 0.5)^(1/2) − 1 = 0.75^0.5 − 1 = −13.40%. The investor ends 25% down.

## Book slips and uncertainties
- The text says corporate bonds earned slightly more than government bonds (p.59 and the pre-test), then shows them trailing Treasuries in realised terms (p.60). Trust the later data-based statement.
- The pre-test treats government bonds as the lowest earners, ignoring T-bills.
- The long-term exhibit's appreciation and distribution columns do not always add to total return; unexplained (p.59).
- The text describes T-bill returns as tracking inflation, but its own data show a small negative real return (p.61).
- The FX case exhibit graphic is price-only while the text includes the coupon, so figures differ; trust the text (pp.54–55). A small sign typo in the text does not affect the result (p.55).
- The text's claim about the income share of long government bond returns has no data behind it (p.62).
- One end-of-module question relies on a period the module's exhibits do not cover.
- Several exhibits are charts; the notes did not study them closely. PDF 57–59 and 79–82 were checked from text only.
