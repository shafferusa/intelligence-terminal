## Learning outcomes
1. Compute and interpret a company's WACC (The Cost of Capital, p.176–179, PDF 184–187).
2. Explain what drives capital structure and WACC: business model, life-cycle stage, market and industry conditions, issuer-specific risk (Factors Affecting Capital Structure, p.180–190, PDF 188–198).
3. Explain the MM propositions: irrelevance, leverage raising the cost of equity, the tax shield, distress costs (MM section, p.191–200, PDF 199–208).
4. Describe optimal vs target capital structure, including static trade-off, pecking order and agency ideas (Optimal Capital Structure, p.201–207, PDF 209–215).

## Core principles
- **Investors set the cost of capital, not management** (p.176–179). Debt has a priority, contractual claim and deductible interest, so it is cheaper than equity. WACC is the discount rate for average-risk projects and the IRR hurdle.
- **Market weights for WACC, book weights often used for targets** (p.177, PDF 185; p.203). A higher share price raises the equity weight; if equity is costlier, WACC rises.
- **Debt and equity costs move together** (p.184–190). Both are paid from one cash-flow stream, so cycle, rates, spreads, sales stability, operating and financial leverage and collateral hit both. Leverage raises ROE in good states but widens outcomes.
- **Business model and life cycle set debt capacity** (p.181–183). Capital-intensive firms with pledgeable assets use secured debt or leases; startups with negative free cash flow lean on equity; mature firms carry unsecured debt.
- **MM without taxes: financing does not create value** (p.192–194). Homemade leverage forces arbitrage, and cheaper debt is offset by riskier equity, so WACC is unchanged. It fails with taxes, distress costs, information gaps or agency costs.
- **MM with corporate taxes: debt adds a tax shield** (p.195–197). Levered value exceeds unlevered value and WACC falls with debt. Literally that implies 100% debt, an artifact of the assumptions.
- **Static trade-off sets an optimum** (p.198–202). Direct and indirect distress costs grow with debt; add debt until marginal tax benefit equals marginal expected distress cost. Managers target a range because the optimum cannot be pinned down.
- **Pecking order and agency** (p.204–207). Managers know more than investors, so choices signal: internal funds, then debt, then equity. No optimum exists. Debt also restrains wasteful free-cash-flow spending.

## Formulas
1. WACC = [r_d(1 − t) × w_d] + (r_e × w_e). Costs are annual rates, weights are market or target fractions; add terms for preferred and other claims. Tax adjustment only where interest is deductible. p.177 (PDF 185); the book prints the base form and adds the tax adjustment in the text.
2. Operating leverage = fixed costs / total costs. p.185 (PDF 193).
3. Interest coverage = profit before interest and taxes / interest expense (times). p.187 (PDF 195).
4. r_e = r_0 + (r_0 − r_d)(D/E). r_0 is the all-equity cost; D, E market values; no taxes; WACC stays at r_0. p.193 (PDF 201).
5. V_L = V_U + tD. t marginal corporate tax rate; D debt (permanent debt implied). Corporate taxes only. p.195 (PDF 203).
6. r_e = r_0 + (r_0 − r_d)(1 − t)(D/E). Reduces to formula 4 when t = 0. Equity cost still rises with D/E, but more slowly. p.196 (PDF 204). No separate MM WACC formula; use formula 1 with formula 6.
7. V_L = V_U + tD − PV(costs of financial distress). Static trade-off. p.201 (PDF 209).
8. Supporting: w_d = (D/E)/(1 + D/E) (p.203, PDF 211); perpetuity V = CF/r; unlevered cost with taxes r_0 = CF(1 − t)/V_U; after-tax debt cost r_d(1 − t). Calculation chain with taxes: find V_L, then E = V_L − D, then r_e from formula 6, then WACC with the new market weights.

## Traps
- Assuming more debt always cuts WACC. Without taxes it does not; with taxes it does until distress costs bite.
- Forgetting after-tax debt cost, or wrongly tax-adjusting the cost of equity.
- Using book weights in a WACC calculation when market values are available.
- Using D/E as the debt weight. Convert with D/E ÷ (1 + D/E).
- Writing the tax shield as t × interest; the MM shield is t × D.
- Confusing operating leverage (fixed costs) with financial leverage (debt); a high ROE from leverage is more risk, not less.
- Treating the 100% debt result as advice rather than a consequence of assumptions; and saying pecking order has an optimum (it does not, unlike static trade-off).
- Saying MM assumed bankruptcy cannot happen; they made it costless (p.194, PDF 202).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A firm has 25% debt, 75% equity at market value; pre-tax debt cost 6%, equity cost 11%, tax 30%. WACC = 0.25 × 6% × 0.70 + 0.75 × 11% = 9.30%. At tax 40% the debt term is 0.90%, WACC = 9.15%. Higher tax lowers WACC by 0.15 points.
2. MM without taxes: r_0 = 8%, debt 4%. At D/E = 1, r_e = 8% + 4% = 12%, WACC = 0.5 × 4% + 0.5 × 12% = 8%. At D/E = 0.5, r_e = 10%, weights 1/3 and 2/3, WACC = 1.333% + 6.667% = 8%.
3. Trade-off: V_U = 80 million, t = 25%, debt 20 million. Shield = 5, V_L = 85. If distress PV is 3, V_L = 82. If it rises to 6, V_L = 79, below V_U, so the same debt destroys value.
