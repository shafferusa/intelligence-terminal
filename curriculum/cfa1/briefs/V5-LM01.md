2. Contrast publicly listed with private equity securities: liquidity, disclosure, size, ownership, life-cycle use and forms of private equity. Taught in section 3, pp.15–20 (PDF 25–30).

## Core principles

- **Equity is the residual claim.** Revenue first pays suppliers, employees, lenders and tax. What is left is net income, which the board distributes as dividends or retains to reinvest. Shareholders are paid last, so their payoff is the most uncertain, with the upside if things go well (Exhibit 1, p.7).
- **Two cash-flow sources, no repayment.** The issuer receives cash at t=0 and never has to return it. The investor gets discretionary dividends plus a future price from a market sale, an acquisition or liquidation proceeds (Exhibit 2, p.7, PDF 17). If the firm never distributes, return is price change only.
- **Dividend policy follows the life cycle.** Start-ups burn cash, growth firms reinvest, mature firms have stable cash and few projects so they pay out, declining firms see revenue shrink (Exhibit 3, p.8). Growth investors want appreciation; income investors want mature payers.
- **Issuing common shares trades ownership for capital.** Existing owners keep operating control but sell economic and voting rights. Common holders have six groups of rights: voting (proportional, at meetings or by proxy), dividends, conversion, liquidation, split/subdivision/combination, and preemption. Common has no par-value claim (pp.9–10). An IPO can be an exit for existing owners, so not all proceeds reach the company (p.10).
- **Preference shares are a debt/equity hybrid.** Fixed dividend = rate × par, paid ahead of common but behind all debt; usually no vote; may have no maturity. Skipping a preferred dividend is not a default, yet the payment is expected, unlike a common dividend. Cumulative: missed dividends accrue as arrears payable before any common dividend. Non-cumulative: a missed dividend is gone (pp.11–12).
- **Each contingency moves risk to one side.** Participating (extra dividend above a profit threshold) and convertible (swap into a fixed number of common shares) give the investor upside; investors pay for conversion with a lower dividend, which cuts the issuer's cash cost (useful to early-stage firms lacking collateral). Putable (sell back at a set price) and liquidation preference give downside protection. Callable favours the issuer. (pp.11–13).
- **Seniority and control line up.** Debt: contractual, most senior, no vote, finite maturity. Preferred: no contractual obligation, senior to common, rarely votes. Common: residual, votes, indefinite (Exhibit 6, p.13).
- **Public vs. private is liquidity, disclosure and control.** Listed firms meet exchange standards (size, profit, minimum holders, audited disclosure), trade on open markets, are mostly mature, with owners separate from managers. Private firms are small, early or late stage, closely held, thinly disclosed, with owner-managers and negotiated infrequent sales; no quoted price, so valuation is harder (pp.15–16, Exhibit 7). Private firms far outnumber public ones, but public equity is far larger by value (p.15).
- **Private equity type follows life stage.** Venture capital for start-ups, growth equity for expansion, buyout equity for mature firms, special situations for distressed or event-driven cases. Returns come at exit (IPO or sale) after a multiyear hold (Exhibit 8, p.17). In an LBO investors buy all of a listed firm's shares using heavy debt (MBO if management joins), take it private, restructure and later resell or relist. In a PIPE a listed firm sells new shares privately at a discount: fast and cheap, but dilutive and a source of old-versus-new holder conflict (pp.18–19).

## Vocabulary

- Common shares — residual, voting ownership; also ordinary shares or common stock (p.9, PDF 19).
- Preference shares — senior to common, fixed dividend, rarely votes (p.9, p.11).
- Par value (preferred) — face amount to which the dividend rate applies (p.11, PDF 21).
- Cumulative / participating preferred — arrears owed before common / bonus dividend above a profit level (p.11, PDF 21).
- Callable / putable — issuer may buy back / holder may sell back at a set price (p.12, PDF 22).
- Liquidation preference — preset priority payout to preferred in a wind-up (p.12, PDF 22).
- Convertible preferred — exchangeable into a set number of common shares (p.12, PDF 22).
- IPO — first sale of equity to the public, possibly including shares sold by existing holders (pp.8, 10).
- LBO / MBO / take-private — debt-funded purchase of all shares / with management in the buyer group / resulting delisting (p.18, PDF 28).
- PIPE — private investment in public equity, usually at a discount (p.18, PDF 28).
- Dilution — lower value per share or ownership from issuing shares below current value (p.19, PDF 29).

## Formulas

No named formulas. Two implied calculations:

- Preferred dividend per share = dividend rate × par value (p.11, Exhibit 5, PDF 21; the exhibit labels it X% × P0).
- Post-issue value per share = (existing shares × old price + new shares × issue price) / (existing shares + new shares) (implied by the PIPE case, p.19, PDF 29). Assumes pre-deal market value = shares × price, ignores deal costs and any value the new cash creates. Intuition: a weighted average, so issuing below the current price pulls it down.

## Traps

- Deferring a preferred dividend is not a default, but preferred dividends are expected and fixed, whereas common dividends are discretionary (p.11).
- A put price is the price at which holders may sell back. It is not a minimum dividend and not a funding source (p.12).
- Preferred is senior to common but junior to all debt (p.9, p.13).
- Sort the contingencies: participating and convertible = upside; putable and liquidation preference = downside protection; callable = issuer's benefit (pp.11–12).
- Arrears are owed only in the cumulative case, and only before common dividends.
- Par value belongs to preferred and bonds, not common (p.9).
- Private equity is not only start-ups: buyout equity targets mature firms (pp.17–18, 20).
- Public equity is larger by value, private firms are larger in number; private firms have no public disclosure duty and often owner-manager overlap (pp.15–16).
- A PIPE or other discount issue dilutes per-share value even though cash comes in (p.19).

## Original practice seeds

All hypothetical.

1. Cumulative preferred: 1,000 shares, par USD50, 4% dividend, so USD2 per share, USD2,000 a year. The firm skips years 1 and 2 and wants to pay common in year 3. It must first pay 3 × USD2,000 = USD6,000 to preferred. If non-cumulative, only the current USD2,000 is owed.
2. Dilution: 10 million shares at USD20 (USD200 million). The firm sells 1 million new shares at USD18 in a PIPE. Value per share = (200 + 18)/11 = USD19.82. At an issue price of USD15 it is (200 + 15)/11 = USD19.55: a deeper discount, more dilution.
3. Convertible: par USD100. A plain preferred pays 6% (USD6); a convertible pays 4% (USD4) and converts into 2 common shares. Conversion pays off once common exceeds USD50, ignoring forgone dividends and time value. At common USD70, conversion value is USD140. The investor gave up USD2 a year for this option; the issuer saves USD2 per share of cash cost.
