## Core principles

- **Structure comes from five features** (pp.38–39, PDF 46–47): number and size of sellers, product differentiation, pricing power, barriers to entry, non-price competition. Easy entry plus identical products leaves no pricing power, so long-run profit sinks to the normal return. Barriers plus a unique product allow lasting excess profit.
- **Profit is maximised where MR = MC** (p.49, PDF 57), in every structure. Only a perfect competitor has P = MR (pp.61–62).
- **A competitive firm is a price taker** (pp.47–48, PDF 55–56). The market sets the price; the firm sees a flat demand line, so P = AR = MR and its decision is output only. Price above average cost at the optimum means economic profit; otherwise a loss.
- **Economic profit is lower than accounting profit** (p.46, PDF 54). Economic cost includes the opportunity cost of the owner's labour, land and capital; zero economic profit still pays the owners their normal return.
- **Competition erases profit through entry** (pp.50–52, PDF 58–60). Profit attracts entrants, supply shifts right, price falls until P = MC = minimum AC. Barriers stop this.
- **A monopolist chooses a point on demand** (pp.54–57, PDF 62–65). MR lies below price; it sets MR = MC, then reads price off demand. MC is positive, so MR is positive, so it operates where demand is elastic. The more inelastic the demand, the larger the mark-up. It has no supply curve (pp.55–56).
- **Regulating a natural monopoly is a trade-off** (pp.57–58, PDF 65–66; p.60, PDF 68). Pricing at MC gives the lowest price but falls below long-run average cost, needing a subsidy. Pricing at long-run average cost gives a fair return at a middle price. The regulator must estimate true cost and a fair return. Alternatives: nationalisation, franchise bidding.
- **Price discrimination captures consumer surplus** (pp.58–59, PDF 66–67). It needs the ability to separate customers and block resale. Some buyers may pay less than a single monopoly price would charge.

## Vocabulary

- Perfect competition — many sellers, standard product, easy entry, no pricing power (p.41).
- Monopolistic competition — many sellers, differentiated products, some pricing power (p.39).
- Oligopoly — few sellers who anticipate each other's reactions (p.39).
- Pure monopoly — one seller, no close substitute (p.39, p.53).
- Consumer surplus — buyers' value minus what they pay (pp.43–44).
- Economic profit — revenue minus all costs including a normal return (p.46).
- Natural monopoly — scale economies make one seller cheapest (p.53).
- Network effects — a product gains value as users grow; a barrier (p.54).
- First/second/third-degree price discrimination — each buyer's maximum / quantity-or-quality menu / groups by an identifiable trait (p.58).
- Two-part tariff — per-unit price plus a fixed fee (p.59).

## Formulas

1. **Income elasticity**: ε_Y = %ΔQ_D ÷ %ΔY. Positive normal, above 1 luxury, negative inferior (p.42, PDF 50).
2. **Cross-price elasticity**: ε_X = %ΔQ_DA ÷ %ΔP_B. Positive substitutes, negative complements (p.42, PDF 50).
3. **Elasticity and revenue**: ε_P > 1, price up cuts revenue, price down raises it; ε_P = 1, no change; 0 < ε_P < 1, price up raises revenue, price down cuts it (p.43, PDF 51).
4. **MR and elasticity**: MR = P[1 − 1/ε_P], ε_P a positive magnitude. A perfect competitor has infinite ε, so MR = P (p.43, PDF 51; p.57, PDF 65).
5. **Consumer surplus** (linear demand): CS = ½ × quantity × (demand intercept price − price paid), equal to area under demand minus P × Q (pp.43–45).
6. **Market equilibrium**: set demand price equal to supply price, solve for Q, then P (pp.46–47, PDF 54–55).
7. **Competitive firm**: P = AR = MR; output where MR = MC; long run P = MC = minimum AC (pp.48–49, 51).
8. **Costs**: AC = TC/Q; MC = ΔTC/ΔQ; MC equals AC at minimum AC (pp.48–49, PDF 56–57).
9. **Linear demand and MR**: Q_D = a − bP, so P = a/b − (1/b)Q_D and MR = a/b − (2/b)Q_D: same intercept, twice the slope (p.54, PDF 62).
10. **Monopoly pricing rule**: P(1 − 1/ε) = MC, so P = MC/(1 − 1/ε); requires ε > 1. Average cost does not enter (p.57, PDF 65).
11. **Two-part tariff**: per-unit price at MC, fixed fee equal to consumer surplus at that price (p.59, PDF 67).
12. **Quadratic formula**: Q = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)] / 2a, or set Δprofit/ΔQ = 0 (p.56, PDF 64).

## Traps

- Zero economic profit is not zero accounting profit (p.46, p.51).
- Market demand slopes down; the competitive firm faces a flat line (p.47).
- MR = P only in perfect competition; elsewhere MR < P (pp.61–62).
- A monopolist has no supply curve; the reading says the same of monopolistic competition and oligopoly (pp.55–56).
- A profit-maximising monopolist never settles in the inelastic range, since MC is positive (p.56).
- Average cost is a distractor in the elasticity pricing rule; consumer surplus is an area, not a price (pp.44–45, 57).
- A price change moves along demand; income or related-price changes shift it (p.42).
- Monopoly is not automatically worse: scale economies, regulation or entry threat can beat competition (p.61).

## Original practice seeds

**A (hypothetical): competition erodes profit.** A wheat farm has TC = 4 + 2Q + Q² (USD, Q tonnes), so MC = 2 + 2Q. At price USD10, MR = MC gives Q = 4, TR = 40, TC = 28, economic profit USD12. After entry price falls to USD6: Q = 2, TR = 12, TC = 12, profit zero. AC at Q = 2 is 6, equal to MC, so it is minimum AC.

**B (hypothetical): monopoly cost pass-through.** Demand P = 100 − Q (USD per licence, Q in thousands), constant MC USD20. MR = 100 − 2Q, so Q = 40, P = 60, profit before fixed cost USD1,600 thousand. Check: ε = 1.5, and 20/(1 − 1/1.5) = 60. If MC rises to 30: Q = 35, P = 65, profit USD1,225 thousand. Price rises by 5 against a cost rise of 10.

**C (hypothetical): two-part tariff.** Cinema demand Q = 10 − P, MC = 2. At P = 2, Q = 8 and CS = ½ × 8 × 8 = USD32, so a USD32 monthly fee takes all of it. If MC rises to 4: Q = 6, CS = ½ × 6 × 6 = USD18.
