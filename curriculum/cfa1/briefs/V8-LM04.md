## Learning outcomes
1. Explain what real estate is and what makes it distinctive: property types, structures, direct vs indirect, REITs, uniqueness, local markets, opaque pricing. Real Estate Features, p.96–101 (PDF 104–109).
2. Explain real estate as an investment: return sources, the risk–return spectrum, leverage, inflation link, diversification. Real Estate Investment Characteristics, p.102–106 (PDF 110–114).
3. Explain what infrastructure is and how it is categorised and held: economic vs social, greenfield/brownfield/secondary-stage, direct vs indirect, PPPs, MLPs. Infrastructure Investment Features, p.107–112 (PDF 115–120).
4. Explain infrastructure as an investment: risk and return by stage and type, cash-flow stability, inflation and growth link, diversification. Infrastructure Investment Characteristics, p.113–116 (PDF 121–124).

## Core principles
- **Real estate here means developed property, valued mainly for income plus appreciation.** Tenants pay owners rent under multi-year leases; owners of the equity keep what is left after lenders.
- **Direct ownership buys control and costs liquidity.** The owner picks tenants, capex and lease terms and deducts depreciation and interest, but needs large capital and local knowledge and bears concentration and high transaction costs (p.98–99).
- **A REIT is a pass-through for tax.** Rent flows to the trust; if it pays out roughly 90%–100% of taxable net rental income, it avoids entity-level tax and unitholders pay tax on distributions, avoiding double taxation (p.100). Because depreciation is a large non-cash charge, analysts use FFO or NAV rather than EPS. Listed REITs are transparent and liquid but correlate more with stocks than private property does. The exact payout condition is national law.
- **Risk climbs from senior debt to core, core-plus, value-add, opportunistic.** Higher expected return pays for lease-up, redevelopment, development, distress and leverage risk. Borrowing magnifies gains and losses; a loan-to-value covenant forces repayment or extra collateral when values drop, which is when the borrower can least afford it (p.104). Open-end funds suit stable core; closed-end finite-life funds suit higher-risk strategies (p.101).
- **Measured real estate diversification is flattered by appraisals.** Appraisers revalue private property about annually on present conditions, while listed REITs reprice daily on expected cash flows, so private correlations look artificially low. Listed REIT correlation with equities rises in crises (p.105–106).
- **Infrastructure cash flows are contractual, not just rent.** Availability payments pay for keeping an asset ready (low demand risk); usage-based payments (tolls, fees) put demand risk on the investor; take-or-pay makes the buyer pay for a minimum volume regardless of need (p.107). A private operator earns under a concession in a PPP.
- **Stage and type drive infrastructure risk.** Greenfield (new build, negative early cash flow, construction risk) is riskiest and most return-hungry; brownfield (expansion, privatised or sale-leaseback assets) is in the middle; secondary-stage (fully operating) is lowest. By type: social infrastructure lowest, regulated industries middle, demand-based highest (p.109–110, 113).
- **Infrastructure diversifies through inelastic demand, entry barriers and regulated or contracted pricing.** Listed and private infrastructure both show low correlation with equities; inflation-linked fees give a partial hedge; stable long cash flows suit pension liabilities (p.115–116).

## Vocabulary
- Heterogeneity — no two properties are alike (p.97).
- Residential vs commercial — housing vs income-property sectors such as office, retail, industrial (p.96–97).
- Free and clear — owned with no loan secured on it (p.98).
- Direct vs indirect investment — owning the asset itself vs holding a pooled or securitised claim (p.98–99).
- REIT (equity, mortgage, hybrid) — tax-advantaged property trust that owns, lends, or both (p.99).
- FFO — earnings measure adding back depreciation, closer to cash income (p.100).
- NAV — net asset value per share; used for pricing non-traded vehicles (p.100–101).
- Open-end vs closed-end fund — perpetual with entry/exit vs finite life (p.101).
- Core, core-plus, value-add, opportunistic — rising risk/return property strategies (p.103–104).
- Sale-leaseback — owner sells then rents back; buyer gets contractual rent, seller gets financing (p.103).
- LTV — loan balance divided by property value (p.104).
- Availability, usage-based, take-or-pay payments — infrastructure cash-flow contracts (p.107).
- PPP — long-term public–private contract for private delivery of a public service (p.107).
- Economic vs social infrastructure — transport/communications/utilities vs education/health/housing/justice (p.108–109).
- Greenfield, brownfield, secondary-stage — new build, expansion or privatisation, fully operating (p.109–110).
- BOT and concession agreement — build-operate-transfer cycle, and the contract setting how the operator earns (p.110).
- MLP — exchange-traded pass-through partnership, mostly energy transport and storage (p.111).

## Formulas
- **LTV = mortgage balance / property value** (p.104; verified p.119, PDF 127). Same currency; a ratio.
- **Required balance = covenant LTV × current value; paydown = current balance − required balance** (p.119). Assumes one covenant ceiling and lender-assessed value.
- **Collateral cure: required value = balance / covenant LTV; extra collateral = required value − current value** (p.104).
- **Coefficient of variation = standard deviation / average return** (Exhibit 9, p.114). Both as decimals; risk per unit of return. Printed values are rounded.

## Traps
- Raw land and infrastructure are not "real estate" here (p.93).
- Low private-real-estate correlation is partly appraisal timing; REIT correlations jump in crises (p.105–106).
- REIT tax benefit needs a 90%–100% payout; shareholders are still taxed. EPS understates REIT cash; use FFO or NAV (p.100).
- Do not swap greenfield and brownfield; secondary-stage is the lowest risk (p.109–110, 113).
- Infrastructure income is contractual (availability, usage, take-or-pay), not simply rent; know which carries demand risk (p.107).
- Social infrastructure is the lowest-risk type, regulated industries are not (p.113).
- LTV direction: lower value raises LTV; cure by repaying debt or posting collateral, and use the right formula for each (p.104).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A fund buys a property for EUR50m with a EUR35m loan and a covenant LTV of 0.70. Year-1 value is EUR46m. LTV = 35/46 = 0.761, a breach. Repay: required loan 0.70 × 46 = 32.2, so repay EUR2.8m. Or collateral: 35/0.70 = 50, so add EUR4.0m. At EUR44m, LTV = 0.795, repayment EUR4.2m.
2. A company earns USD10m taxable income at a 25% tax rate: entity tax USD2.5m, at most USD7.5m left to distribute. A REIT meeting its payout condition distributes the full USD10m with no entity tax in this simplified case; shareholders pay their own tax.
3. A plant sells up to 100,000 MWh a year at USD40/MWh. Buyer needs 80,000. Take-or-pay (100,000 MWh minimum) pays USD4.0m; usage-based pays USD3.2m. At 50,000 MWh demand, take-or-pay still pays USD4.0m, usage-based only USD2.0m, so take-or-pay is more bond-like.
