## Core principles
- **One issuer, many instruments.** A company has one common stock but can owe money through loans, commercial paper and notes of different lengths and currencies. Issuers match funding to need: short-term or seasonal working capital through CP or credit lines, long-lived assets through long bonds. Risk: refinancing, since short funding must be rolled over. Lower credit quality narrows the menu and pushes issuers toward secured and bank borrowing (pp.57–62).
- **Credit and maturity form a grid; investors sit in different cells.** Money market funds and corporates' own cash sit short; pension funds and insurers sit long because their liabilities are long; hedge funds and distressed funds take high-yield risk; central banks and intermediaries hold government paper. Return expectation rises with risk taken: sovereign lowest, investment grade slightly higher, high yield highest as compensation for default; emerging-market debt held by foreign investors adds currency risk (pp.58–60, 66).
- **Ratings sort mandates.** Agencies grade probability of default and loss if default occurs. BBB- (Baa3) is the lowest investment grade; BB+ (Ba1) and below is high yield. Many mandates are investment-grade-only, so a downgrade across the line can force selling, the cash-flow link between a rating and a price (pp.59–60).
- **Bond indexes are structurally different from equity indexes.** Many more constituents, much higher turnover (bonds mature and new ones are issued), and weights by market value of debt outstanding, so heavy borrowers weigh most and the mix drifts toward more government debt without any manager decision. Full replication is impractical, so index funds hold a representative sample. A benchmark should match the fund's strategy (p.66).
- **Index rules shape measured returns.** Eligibility (rating, size, maturity, currency), rebalance timing, how coupons are reinvested, pricing side and currency hedging all change the reported return, so the analyst checks the rules before trusting a comparison (pp.67–69).
- **Primary market: debut vs repeat.** A debut issuer shifts from private to public debt (often replacing bank loans); bonds carry no voting rights; sale can be public or private. Repeat issuers move faster and usually create a new bond near par; adding to an old bond priced far from par (a reopening) is less common. In an underwritten deal the dealers take the risk of selling at an agreed price and allocate the order book; in best-efforts the intermediary only sells what it can for a commission. Complex or small deals fall to private placement (pp.71–72).
- **Secondary market: mostly OTC and dealer-quoted.** Bonds trade over the counter with dealers, increasingly on electronic platforms, unlike equities which trade largely on exchanges. Liquidity varies even within one issuer; the bid–offer spread, in bp, is the cost. Illiquid bonds get estimated prices from similar liquid ones. Distressed bonds trade far below par; forced sellers meet opportunistic buyers; bonds usually keep trading after equity is delisted (pp.73–75).

## Vocabulary
- Sector — category of issuer used to group bonds (p.57).
- Credit rating — agency letter grade of default probability and loss severity (p.59).
- Investment grade / high yield — BBB-/Baa3 and above vs BB+/Ba1 and below (p.60).
- Fallen angel — former investment-grade issuer downgraded to high yield (p.60).
- "Default risk free" — label for top-rated developed-market sovereign debt, assuming full payment (p.60).
- Commercial paper (CP), ABCP — short-term corporate and asset-backed borrowing (p.58).
- Credit facility / syndicate — pre-agreed bank-group borrowing line with a fluctuating balance (p.61).
- Term loan, callable note — floating-rate bank loan; bond the issuer may redeem early at a set price (p.62).
- Aggregate vs narrow index — broad market measure vs one sector, credit band, maturity band, region or ESG theme (p.66).
- Debut issuer — first-time bond issuer, the bond analog of an IPO (p.71).
- Public offering / private placement — registered sale to many investors vs unregistered sale to a few (p.71).
- Reopening — adding to an existing bond priced well away from par (p.71).
- Underwritten offering / best efforts — dealer bears sale risk vs broker only sells what it can (pp.71–72).
- Order book — investors' indications of demand during the issue (p.72).
- Shelf registration — standing registration letting frequent issuers come to market quickly (p.72).
- Quote-driven / OTC market — dealers quote prices at which they trade (p.73).
- Bid–offer spread — offer minus bid, quoted in bp (p.73).
- On-the-run — newest benchmark issue, typically the most liquid (p.73).
- Distressed debt — bonds of issuers at or near bankruptcy, priced far below par (p.73).

## Formulas
None in this module. Definition only: bid–offer spread = offer price − bid price, quoted in bp (1 bp = 0.01%) (p.73). Intuition: a wider spread is a bigger round-trip cost and signals lower liquidity.

## Traps
- Repeat bond issues are not equity follow-ons; usually a new bond near par, reopening is the exception (p.71).
- Debut issuance is private-to-public, often replacing bank loans, and does not have to be public; private placement is allowed (pp.71, 74).
- "Default risk free" is an assumption that payments are received, not a claim of zero risk (p.60).
- The investment-grade boundary is BBB- / Baa3. High yield starts at BB+ / Ba1 (p.60).
- Bond index weights follow debt outstanding, not equal weights or equity-style market cap; funds usually sample, not replicate (p.66).
- Aggregate-type index rules can exclude high yield and unrated bonds; read the eligibility rules (p.67).
- Spreads are quoted in bp, not percent; liquidity differs even among one issuer's bonds, so learn the full liquidity ranking from the text (p.73).
- High yield is not the same as distressed, and equity is likely delisted before bonds stop trading (pp.73, 77).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A dealer quotes a bond at bid 98.90 and offer 99.10 per 100 par. Spread = 0.20 points = 0.20% = 20 bp. Buying 1,000,000 par at the offer and selling at the bid costs 0.20% × 1,000,000 = 2,000. An on-the-run government bond with a 0.5 bp spread costs 0.005% × 1,000,000 = 50.
2. An index holds 600bn of government debt and 400bn of corporate debt by market value, so government weight is 600/1,000 = 60%. After 200bn of new government issuance with corporates unchanged, weight = 800/1,200 = 66.7%. The index drifted with no manager action.
3. An investment-grade-only fund holds a BBB- bond; the issuer is cut to BB+. The mandate bars it, so the fund must sell, which can depress the price. An unconstrained hedge fund may buy for recovery. Qualitative; no numbers.
