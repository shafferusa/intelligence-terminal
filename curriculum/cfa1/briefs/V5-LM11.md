2. Compare sell-side and buy-side reports and their conclusions (Sell-Side Versus Buy-Side and sub-sections, p.441–449, PDF 451–459).
3. Explain why analysts using the same valuation approach reach different values, through different revenue, profitability, investment and financing assumptions (Equity Research Reconciliation, p.450–460, PDF 460–470).

## Core principles
- **A report argues that price differs from value.** It turns analysis into a rating and price target: is the stock mispriced, by how much, over what horizon, relative to what? The thesis explains why; catalysts are events expected to move price toward intrinsic value. The first-page summary is written last (p.428–433).
- **Ratings are about expected return, often relative.** Expected appreciation is target over current price, minus 1, over a stated horizon (usually one or two years). Buy/hold/sell is usually absolute; overweight/neutral/underweight is relative to a benchmark, index or coverage universe. Rule of thumb: a gap of about 10–15% either side for the outer ratings; no standard exists. A positive expected return can still be underweight if the benchmark is expected to do better (p.430–431).
- **Transitory versus lasting.** Separate a one-off hit from a change in long-run earning power; treating a transitory miss as permanent mis-sets the target. Material risks belong in the valuation, not just a list (p.430, 437–438).
- **Sell-side incentives.** Banks earn from underwriting, advisory work and trading commissions, so research is a cost unless clients pay for it. Bankers hold non-public information and want mandates, so rules separate research from banking: no inside information to analysts, quiet periods, and (MiFID II) research paid for separately from execution. Analysts avoid sell ratings, so ratings cluster at buy and hold and tend to lag price. Buy-side users read it for information and consensus, not instructions (p.442–444).
- **Buy-side is confidential, customised and includes shorts.** It serves an internal investment committee, is less regulated, and can cover small, little-followed stocks. Names usually arrive via a screen: set a universe, apply quantitative criteria fitting the strategy, add secondary criteria, narrow until deep work is feasible. Screening focuses effort; it does not improve valuation accuracy, and rigid cut-offs can discard good candidates. Activist short sellers research stocks expected to fall because of fraud or misinformation not in the price, and publish publicly after informing their own investors (p.441, 444–447).
- **Reverse-engineer the price.** Set market price equal to a two-stage FCFE value and solve for long-run growth: this shows what growth the price embeds. It is a result of price, cost of equity and forecasts, not a forecast of the company. Longer explicit horizons with strong cash flows tend to lower implied growth, but not always (p.450–453).
- **Value comes from operations, and growth needs investment.** More revenue helps only if margins hold; if margins fall enough, equity value can drop, because terminal value rests on the last explicit cash flow. Revenue growth without matching investment overstates value. Financing moves value only modestly, so it should not quietly create a large gap. Changes matter relative to market expectations (p.454–458).
- **Disagreement is healthy.** Hard-to-value stocks show wide dispersion of targets among informed analysts, mostly from different views of revenue growth, profitability and terminal value. Dispersion aids price discovery (p.450, 458–460).

## Vocabulary
- Equity research report — estimates intrinsic value versus market price, ending in a recommendation (p.428).
- Sell-side research — reports from banks or brokers to outside clients (p.429).
- Buy-side research — confidential reports for an asset manager's own decisions (p.429).
- Initiation of coverage — first comprehensive report on a company (p.429).
- Recommendation — buy/hold/sell or overweight/neutral/underweight (p.430).
- Investment thesis — why the stock is mispriced and how it should converge (p.432).
- Catalyst — expected event that could trigger revaluation (p.432).
- Stock screening — filtering a universe by criteria to focus research (p.444).
- Activist short seller — short-focused researcher who publicises fraud or misinformation (p.447).
- Hard to value — informed analysts estimate intrinsic value very differently (p.450).
- Implied long-run growth — terminal growth making model value equal market price (p.451).
- Net margin — net income divided by revenue (p.454).
- Equity reinvestment rate — share of net income not paid out as FCFE (p.454).

## Formulas
1. **Expected appreciation** = (Target price − Current price) / Current price. Same currency, stated horizon (p.431, PDF 441).
2. **Equation 1 (two-stage FCFE value):** P_t = IV_t = Σ(i=1..n) FCFE_(t+i)/(1+r_e)^i + [FCFE_(t+n+1)/(r_e − g_l)] / (1+r_e)^n. Needs r_e > g_l; the terminal term is discounted by (1+r_e)^n, not n+1 (p.451, PDF 461).
3. **Equation 2 (implied growth):** g_l = r_e − FCFE_(t+n+1) / { (1+r_e)^n × [P_t − Σ PV of explicit FCFE] }. Cost of equity minus the terminal cash-flow yield on what the price leaves for terminal value (p.451, PDF 461).
4. **Equation 3:** FCFE = Net income − Net investment + Net debt issued (p.454, PDF 464).
5. **Equation 4:** Equity reinvestment rate = (Net investment − Net debt issued) / Net income = 1 − FCFE/Net income (p.454).
6. **Equation 5:** Long-run FCFE growth = Equity reinvestment rate × Return on equity. Assumes stable ROE and reinvestment (p.454).

## Traps
- Treating every rating as absolute; overweight/underweight is relative.
- Assuming more revenue always raises value; check margin and investment.
- Modelling growth with no extra investment, or letting financing drive the value gap.
- Reading implied growth as the company's forecast, or assuming a longer horizon must lower it.
- Assuming "buy-side" means only buying, or that sell-side ratings are balanced.
- Confusing screening's purpose with accuracy: it narrows the universe before valuation.
- Forgetting the (1+r_e)^n discount on the terminal term, or mixing GAAP and non-GAAP EPS in consensus data.
- Thinking analysts should converge on one value.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.

**A. Relative rating.** Benchmark expected +8% over 12 months. Stock X: price 90, target 100. Expected return = 10/90 = 11.1%; relative = +3.1 points, inside a 10-point band, so neutral. If the benchmark were expected flat, relative = +11.1 points, so overweight.

**B. Implied growth.** Market cap 1,000; r_e = 10%; n = 2; FCFE years 1–3 = 50, 60, 70. PV explicit = 45.45 + 49.59 = 95.04. Bracket = 1,000 − 95.04 = 904.96; × 1.21 = 1,095.0. Terminal yield = 70/1,095.0 = 6.39%. g = 10% − 6.39% = 3.61%. If year 3 FCFE were 90, yield = 8.22% and g = 1.78%.

**C. Revenue up, margin down.** Base revenue 1,000, margin 10%, net income 100. Revenue +10% to 1,100 at 9% margin gives net income 99, so with investment and debt unchanged FCFE falls by 1. At 9.5% margin, net income is 104.5 (+4.5%), so value rises by less than the 10% revenue gain.
