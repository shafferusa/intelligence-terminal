## Core principles
- **Fiat money needs a disciplined issuer.** Unbacked money rests on legal-tender status and public acceptance, so the central bank must protect the currency's value and confidence. As lender of last resort it can create reserves for a bank short of cash, which deters runs (pp.107–108, PDF 115–116).
- **Open market operations move reserves.** Buying government bonds from banks adds reserves, so lending capacity and broad money can grow via the money multiplier; selling drains them. Fails if banks hoard reserves (p.112, PDF 120).
- **The policy rate sets a floor for bank pricing.** Banks will not lend below what they pay the central bank for short secured loans (repos), so base rates follow. A higher rate slows lending and money growth (p.112, PDF 120). Raising reserve requirements caps money creation and is contractionary; they are rare in developed markets (p.113, PDF 121).
- **Transmission runs through four linked channels.** A rate rise lifts market and bank lending rates, lowers asset prices, weakens expectations and confidence, and tends to strengthen the currency. Demand and import prices fall, so inflation falls. Works because money is not neutral in the short run (pp.113–115, PDF 121–123).
- **Independence, credibility and transparency make a central bank effective.** Politicians may want low rates before elections, so decisions sit with an insulated institution (p.117, PDF 125). A heavily indebted government gains from inflation, so its target is doubted; a credible target is self-fulfilling because contracts follow expectations. Regular inflation reports build credibility (pp.118–120, PDF 126–128).
- **A peg imports another country's inflation but surrenders domestic control.** If home inflation exceeds the anchor's, the currency weakens; the central bank sells foreign reserves and buys its own currency, which shrinks money and lifts rates, even in a weak economy. Reserves are finite and visible, so credibility is the weak point (pp.123–124, PDF 131–132).
- **Stance is relative to the neutral rate, and the shock's source matters.** Policy above neutral is contractionary, below is expansionary (p.125, PDF 133). Tightening suits a demand-driven price rise; against a supply shock it can deepen the downturn (p.126, PDF 134).
- **Policy has limits.** Long rates follow expected short rates, so a hike can lower long yields if investors fear recession; bond vigilantes punish perceived loss of control. Nominal rates cannot go much below zero, and deflation raises real debt burdens and encourages delaying purchases. QE is the response but cannot force banks to lend (pp.126–131, PDF 134–139).
- **Fiscal and monetary policy are not interchangeable.** They act through different channels and shift the split of demand between government and private sector. Large central-bank purchases of government debt blur independence; persistent deficits can lift real rates and crowd out investment (pp.132–136, PDF 140–144).

## Vocabulary
- Fiat money — valued by decree and acceptance, not convertible to a commodity (p.107).
- Lender of last resort — supplies liquidity to banks in crisis (p.107).
- Open market operations — trading government bonds with banks to change reserves (p.112).
- Policy rate — official rate at which the central bank lends to banks (p.112).
- Repo — sale of securities with an agreed buy-back; a secured short loan (p.112).
- Base rate — a bank's reference rate for customer loans (p.112).
- Fed funds rate — US overnight interbank reserve rate; the key US rate (pp.112–113).
- Reserve requirement — mandated fraction of deposits held as reserves (p.113).
- Operational independence — bank chooses how to hit a government-set target (p.117).
- Target independence — bank also chooses the inflation definition, level and horizon (p.117).
- Exchange rate targeting; dollarization — fixing or banding a currency; replacing it with a foreign one (pp.123–124).
- Neutral rate — neither stimulates nor restrains (p.125).
- Demand vs supply shock — spending-led vs cost-led price pressure (p.126).
- Bond market vigilantes — investors who move long yields when they doubt policy (p.127).
- Liquidity trap — money demand so elastic that extra money does not lower rates or lift activity (p.127).
- Deflation — sustained fall in the general price level (p.127).
- Quantitative easing — very large asset purchases that create reserves (p.128).
- Ricardian equivalence; monetization — households save tax cuts; central bank funds the deficit (pp.132–135).

## Formulas
- Neutral policy rate = real trend growth rate + long-run expected inflation (the inflation target if credible) (p.126, PDF 134). % per year. Both inputs are estimates. Policy rate above neutral is contractionary, below is expansionary.
- Fiscal multiplier (words only, p.134, PDF 142): cumulative real GDP effect over two years divided by the stimulus as % of GDP.

## Traps
- Contractionary/expansionary is judged against neutral, not whether the rate looks high (p.125).
- Zero inflation is not the goal: 2% leaves room against deflation, which is hard to fight (p.119).
- The target horizon is forward-looking, about two years (p.119).
- Operational vs target independence: the ECB has both; New Zealand, Sweden and the UK have operational only (p.117).
- Under a peg, domestic rates cannot be set freely (p.124).
- A rate hike does not always raise long yields; check demand vs supply shock first (pp.126–127).
- QE is a balance-sheet tool, not a rate cut, and does not guarantee lending (p.128).
- Developing-economy impediments are testable: illiquid bond and interbank markets, an unclear neutral rate, financial innovation, a weak inflation record, limited independence (pp.121–122).
- Easy fiscal plus tight monetary raises the government's share of GDP (p.135).

## Original practice seeds
1. *Neutral rate (hypothetical).* Trend growth 1.8%, credible target 2.0%: neutral = 3.8%. A 3.0% policy rate is 0.8 below neutral, so expansionary. If trend growth is revised to 1.2%, neutral = 3.2% and the same rate is only 0.2 below. The stance changed, the rate did not.
2. *Peg defence (hypothetical).* A country pegs to the dollar; its inflation is 7% vs 2%. The central bank sells dollar reserves and buys local currency, so short rates rise. If reserves of 10 are drained at 1 per week, they last about 10 weeks, visible to speculators.
3. *Money multiplier (hypothetical).* Central bank buys 100 of bonds; reserve requirement 10%. Maximum expansion is 100/0.10 = 1,000. If banks hoard half, 50/0.10 = 500. At a 20% requirement, full lending gives 100/0.20 = 500 (simplified).
