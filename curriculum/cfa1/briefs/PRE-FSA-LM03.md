## Core principles
- **Snapshot, residual equity.** The balance sheet shows, at one date, what the firm controls (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and the owners' leftover claim (equity). Equity is book value, not market value: measurement mixes historical cost and fair value, numbers go stale after the date, and things like reputation are absent. The conclusion fails if you treat book equity as what shares are worth (pp.64–65).
- **Current vs non-current.** An asset is current if the firm expects to realise or use it within the longer of a year or one operating cycle (buying inventory through collecting cash). A liability is current if due within that window or if the firm lacks an unconditional right to defer settlement. Long-cycle inventory and operating payables stay current. Working capital (current assets minus current liabilities) tests short-term ability to pay: too little risks default to suppliers, too much ties up funds (pp.67–68).
- **Liquidity-based format.** Where the current/non-current split is not informative (banks), items are simply listed from nearest-to-cash to furthest (p.68).
- **Receivables are net, and the estimate is a lever.** Customers owe the firm for goods already delivered; the balance is shown after an allowance (a contra asset). Bad debt expense raises the allowance and cuts income with no cash moving; a write-off lowers receivable and allowance equally, leaving net receivables and income unchanged. Because the allowance is a management estimate, trimming it lifts profit without any cash change. Watch receivables vs sales and the allowance trend (pp.71–72).
- **Inventory and prepaid: cost, then write-down.** Inventory is carried at the lower of cost and net realisable value; write-downs hit income. IFRS permits reversal, US GAAP does not; LIFO is allowed only under US GAAP. A prepaid expense is cash already paid for a future benefit, expensed as used (pp.73–74).
- **Liabilities settled by cash vs by delivery.** Trade payables are supplier financing; accrued expenses are costs recognised but unpaid; deferred revenue is cash received before delivery, settled by delivering the good or service (or refunding), not by paying cash. (pp.74–78).
- **Long-lived assets.** PPE is capitalised at cost and depreciated over its life (land is not). Impairment follows when recoverable amount falls below carrying amount. IFRS allows a revaluation model and reversal of impairment; US GAAP allows cost only and no reversal. Investment property (IFRS) is held for rent or appreciation, at cost or fair value. Deferred tax assets arise when tax is paid ahead of tax expense, or from unused losses, and count only if future taxable income is expected (pp.78–81).
- **Equity components.** Contributed capital, preferred shares (liability if redemption is mandatory), treasury shares (a deduction at cost, no votes or dividends), retained earnings, accumulated other comprehensive income, non-controlling interest. The statement of changes in equity rolls each component from opening to closing (pp.82–85).

## Vocabulary
- Asset / liability / equity — resource controlled / obligation to pay out / residual claim (p.64).
- Liquidity — nearness to cash (p.66).
- Operating cycle — time from buying inventory to collecting cash (p.67).
- Working capital — current assets minus current liabilities (p.68).
- Cash equivalent — very short-term, low-rate-risk investment, original maturity about three months or less (p.71).
- Amortised cost / fair value — cost-based value / exit price (pp.70–71).
- Allowance for doubtful accounts — contra asset estimating uncollectible receivables (p.71).
- Commercial paper, accrued expenses, deferred revenue — short-term borrowing, unpaid recognised costs, cash received before delivery (p.76).
- Impairment, recoverable amount, value in use — drop below carrying amount, higher of fair value less selling cost and value in use (p.80).
- Deferred tax asset — tax paid or payable ahead of tax expense recognised (p.81).
- Issued / outstanding / treasury shares — outstanding = issued minus treasury (pp.82–83).
- Comprehensive income / AOCI — total change in equity from non-owner sources, and its accumulated non-profit part (p.83).

## Formulas
- A = L + E, equivalently E = A − L. Currency at one date. Always holds; equity is a residual (p.64, PDF 72).
- Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. Currency at one date. (p.68, PDF 76).
- Percent uncollectible = allowance / (net receivables + allowance). Net receivables are after the allowance, so add it back to get gross; unit-free percent (p.72, PDF 80).
- Equity roll-forward (implicit in the statement of changes in equity): ending = beginning + net income + OCI − dividends − repurchases ± other items, per component, OCI entered with its sign (p.86, PDF 94).

## Traps
- Quoting book equity as market value (pp.64–65).
- Computing percent uncollectible from net receivables instead of gross (p.72).
- Confusing bad debt expense (hits income) with a write-off (no income effect, net unchanged) (p.72).
- Judging cash equivalents by remaining maturity; the test is original maturity (p.71).
- Calling long-operating-cycle inventory non-current, or a payable settled after a year non-current (p.67).
- Treating deferred revenue as needing cash to settle (pp.77–78).
- Depreciating land, or mixing depreciation (allocation) with impairment (unexpected decline) (p.80).
- Assuming preferred shares are always equity, or that treasury shares are an asset; they reduce equity and cash (pp.82–83).
- Mixing up IFRS vs US GAAP: LIFO, reversal of write-downs and impairments, PPE revaluation, investment property models.

## Original practice seeds
1. (Hypothetical) A firm reports net receivables of 9,800 and an allowance of 200 ($ thousand). Gross = 10,000; uncollectible = 200/10,000 = 2.0%. If the allowance is cut to 100 and net rises to 9,900, gross is still 10,000, the rate is 1.0%, and pre-tax income rises by 100 with no cash change.
2. (Hypothetical) A firm pays $1,200 on 1 Jan for 12 months of insurance. Day 1: cash −1,200, prepaid +1,200. Monthly expense is 100. At 31 Mar prepaid = 900 and cumulative expense = 300.
3. (Hypothetical) A firm has assets $50m, liabilities $30m, equity $20m. A $5m cash buyback gives assets 45, liabilities 30, equity 15. Funded by $5m of new borrowing instead: assets 50, liabilities 35, equity 15.
