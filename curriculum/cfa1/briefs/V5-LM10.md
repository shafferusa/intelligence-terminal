## Core principles
- **Strategy is tested forward, not by the share price.** A sound strategy should (1) defend against Porter's Five Forces, (2) fit the external PESTLE environment, and (3) be executable with the firm's actual resources. Intentional strategies have measurable goals and feedback; unintentional ones drift from habit or incentives (p.386).
- **Market sizing is a funnel.** Total addressable market is the biggest pool; the serviceable market trims it by product focus and geography (legal barriers count); the obtainable market trims again by present scale, capacity and funding. A startup's risk is binary: one failed key trial can erase most value (pp.386-391).
- **Life cycle sets the strategy.** Startups: innovate, partner, raise equity. Growth entrants: take share of a serviceable market. Decline: falling or below-GDP revenue from changing tastes, substitutes or obsolescence; spare capacity intensifies rivalry, so firms cut cost, restructure, exit, or reinvent lines (pp.386-392).
- **Generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation, focus.** Cost leaders win on unit cost and suit commoditized, price-sensitive markets; risks are cost creep or customers trading up. Differentiators earn a premium only while rivals cannot copy the moat. Focus applies either approach to a narrow segment; large rivals can undercut it. A firm doing none is "stuck in the middle" (pp.392-398).
- **Revenue forecasts combine two views.** Top-down: economy, industry, then share of the obtainable market. Bottom-up: volume, price, mix, geography, same-store vs new outlets, renewals vs new customers. Mix shifts can move profit as well as revenue (pp.400-402).
- **Margins differ in what they hide.** Net margin captures everything but is distorted by financing, one-offs and tax; operating margin removes financing but depends on depreciation policy; EBITDA is comparable across firms but non-GAAP, ignores capital needs and varies in definition. Cheap-looking cost lines may mean underinvestment (pp.403-405).
- **Pricing power and operating leverage drive earnings swings.** Pricing power rises with inelastic demand, concentrated structure, relative share, entry barriers, few substitutes, switching costs and loyalty; price takers earn about their cost of capital long run. More fixed cost means profit moves by more than revenue, both ways (pp.406-410).
- **Balance sheet links to cash flow.** FCFE comes from earnings and non-cash charges plus borrowing, less working-capital and long-term-asset investment; borrowing is financing, not operating strength. A longer cash conversion cycle ties up cash; a negative one means suppliers fund the firm. ROIC above WACC means value creation. Debt raises financial leverage and distress risk (pp.411-420).

## Formulas
1. **Load factor = RPK / ASK** (p.394, PDF 404). Both in millions of distance units; result a percentage. Utilization only, not fare yield.
2. **Operating profit = Q × (P − VC) − FC** (Eq 1, p.409, PDF 419). Q units, P price, VC variable cost per unit, FC fixed cost per period. Short run, relevant range. More FC means bigger profit swings per sales change.
3. **FCFE = Net income + D&A − ΔWorking capital − ΔLT assets + ΔDebt** (Eq 2, pp.411–412, PDF 421–422). ΔWC = ΔCurrent assets − ΔCurrent liabilities; ΔDebt = borrowing − repayment; currency per period. The notes read ΔLT assets as gross investment in long-term assets (capex), inferred from the pre-test wording (CFO − capex + net borrowing).
4. **Cash conversion cycle = DIO + DSO − DPO** (Eq 3, p.412, PDF 422). Days. Negative means suppliers finance operations.
5. **ROIC = NOPAT / average invested capital** (Eq 4, p.416, PDF 426). Invested capital is the average of long-term liabilities plus equity; above WACC means excess return.
6. **Degree of financial leverage = %ΔNet income / %ΔOperating income** (Eq 5, p.418, PDF 428). Rises with interest burden; magnifies gains and losses.
7. **Net capex = capex − D&A** (p.415, PDF 425). Currency per period; positive means productive capacity growing.

Margin = profit measure / revenue (pp.403–404).

## Traps
- Serviceable vs obtainable: geography/focus narrows to serviceable; scale and capacity narrow further to obtainable (p.388).
- Cost leadership (broad market) vs cost-based focus (niche); scale and scope economies go with cost leadership, rarely with differentiation-based focus.
- Shorter CCC means less cash tied up; check the sign each component carries in the formula (p.412).
- Market share is top-down; mix, price, volume, geography are bottom-up (pp.400–401).
- Operating margin sits above interest, so financing choices do not change it; financial leverage is a separate measure (p.418).
- Pricing power goes with inelastic demand, not elastic; relative share matters, not only absolute (p.407).
- Operating leverage cuts both ways: a revenue fall hurts profit more than proportionally (p.409).
- ROIC is an after-tax return on all invested capital, not a pre-tax shareholder return and not the cost of capital (p.416).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A firm has DIO 40, DSO 25, DPO 30 days. CCC = 40 + 25 − 30 = 35 days. Stretching DPO to 45 gives 20 days, 15 days less cash tied up.
2. Firm X: 1,000 units, price 20, variable cost 12, fixed cost 6,000: profit 2,000. Firm Y: same volume and price, variable cost 16, fixed cost 2,000: profit 2,000. At +10% volume, X earns 2,800 (+40%), Y 2,400 (+20%). At −10%, X earns 1,200 (−40%), Y 1,600 (−20%). Higher fixed cost, bigger swing.
3. Net income 100, D&A 30, ΔWC 20, long-term asset investment 80, net borrowing 50: FCFE = 100 + 30 − 20 − 80 + 50 = 80. With net repayment of 20 instead: 10.
