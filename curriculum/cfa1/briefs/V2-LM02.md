## Core principles
- **Cycles recur but are irregular.** Output swings around a long-run path; the sequence of upswing, downturn and revival shows up across many activities at once, but length and depth vary widely, from just over a year to a decade or more (p.51).
- **Three ways to measure the cycle.** Classical = level of output; growth cycle = actual output versus potential (the output gap); growth rate cycle = change in the growth rate. The growth-rate view flags turns first and needs no trend estimate. At peaks the order is growth rate, then growth cycle, then classical; at troughs it is growth rate, then classical, then growth cycle (p.52–53, PDF 60–61).
- **Four phases (growth-cycle view).** Recovery: gap negative but closing, overtime before hiring, unemployment high. Expansion: gap turns positive, spending, hiring and investment rise. Slowdown: positive gap at its largest, growth decelerating, inflation accelerating (use this for exams, see slips). Contraction: output below potential, firms cut hours, freeze hiring, then lay off, unemployment rises (p.54).
- **Employment and costs lag.** Firms confirm a turn before hiring or firing, and hiring and training are costly, so they keep workers early in a downturn and use overtime before hiring in a recovery. Output per hour is highest late in a recession when staffing is lean (p.55, p.60–61, PDF 68–69).
- **Markets lead, safety rotates.** Risky assets are repriced upward 3–6 months before the economy bottoms (p.55). In a boom the riskiest assets gain most and government bonds fall (higher yields, inflation fear); in contraction investors pay up for safe government bonds and steady-cash-flow equities such as utilities and staples (p.55–56).
- **Capex and inventories amplify swings.** Capex is volatile because profit and cash flow swing; short-life items (software, light equipment) are ordered first in recovery and cancelled first in contraction, heavy equipment and construction later (p.61–62). Inventories are small but move growth: sales recover before production so inventories and the ratio fall early in recovery; production then rebuilds stock; in the slowdown sales fall faster than production so the ratio rises; contraction cuts production below sales to clear stock (p.63–64).
- **Credit amplifies the cycle.** Lenders loosen in strength and tighten in weakness; tighter credit lowers property values, weakens borrowers and raises defaults, deepening the downturn. Credit swings usually last longer and hit harder than ordinary business cycles, and the two do not always line up; recessions with credit and housing busts are longer and deeper (p.57–59, PDF 65–67).
- **Indicators are used in combination.** Leading turn before the economy, coincident with it, lagging after it (p.65). A lone leading signal is not enough: a rising equity index is confirmed only if the broad leading composite also rises (p.67). Composites bundle series with consistent timing (p.66).

## Vocabulary
- Business cycle — recurrent, non-periodic expansion and contraction across the economy (p.51).
- Classical cycle — swings in the level of output (p.52).
- Growth cycle — swings of output around potential (p.52).
- Growth rate cycle — swings in output growth (p.53).
- Output gap — actual minus potential output (p.54).
- Recession / depression — a fall in activity; depression is very severe (p.54).
- Boom — late expansion pressing capacity limits (p.55).
- Credit cycle — swings in availability and price of private-sector credit (p.57–58).
- Macroprudential policy — rules aimed at damping financial booms (p.59).
- Capacity utilization — share of productive capacity in use (p.60–61).
- Inventory–sales ratio — stock on hand relative to sales (p.63).
- Leading / coincident / lagging indicator — turns before / with / after the economy (p.65).
- Composite indicator — several indicators combined into one (p.66).
- Conference Board LEI — 10-component US leading index, classical cycle concept (p.66).
- OECD CLI — growth-cycle composite leading indicators across about 30 countries (p.68).
- Diffusion index — breadth: how many components are rising (p.71).
- Economic tendency survey — qualitative business, consumer or expert survey (p.70).
- Principal components analysis — extracts a common factor from many series (p.70).
- Nowcast / GDPNow — estimate of the current state of slow-to-publish data; the Atlanta Fed running GDP estimate (p.70–71, PDF 78–79).

## Formulas
- **Diffusion index = (sum of component scores / N) × 100.** Each component scores 1.0 if it rose by more than 0.05%, 0.5 if it changed by less than 0.05%, 0 if it fell by more than 0.05%; N = number of components. Scale 0–100, 50 neutral; thresholds can differ by index (p.71–72, PDF 79–80). Counts agreement, ignoring size.
- **Inventory–sales ratio = inventories / sales.** The book gives it in words only (p.63, PDF 71); in months of sales if sales are monthly.
- **Output gap = actual output − potential output.** Words only (p.50, p.54); positive when output exceeds potential.

## Traps
- Cycles are recurrent, not periodic.
- Slowdown is not recession: the gap is still positive and growth is only decelerating; inflation is still rising.
- Unemployment lags (firms hire and fire slowly because of costs).
- Falling inventories and a falling ratio early in recovery are normal, not weakness; a rising ratio near the peak is the warning.
- Memorize the indicator classification lists from the book's exhibit: lagging items are mostly labor-cost, inflation, lending and household-debt measures; leading items are mostly orders, permits, hours, market prices and expectations.
- Distinguish an illustrative indicator list from the Conference Board LEI membership; they differ (p.65–66).
- Diffusion index measures breadth, not size; a relative percent change in the index differs from index points.
- Two negative real GDP quarters is only a rule of thumb; dating committees decide late (p.56).

## Original practice seeds
1. **(Hypothetical) Diffusion index.** A composite has 5 components. In one month 2 rise by more than 0.05%, 1 changes by 0.02%, 2 fall by more than 0.05%. Score = 2 + 0.5 + 0 = 2.5; index = 2.5/5 × 100 = 50. If the 2 decliners had instead risen 0.3%, score 4.5, index 90.
2. **(Hypothetical) Inventory–sales ratio.** Inventory 120, monthly sales 60: ratio 2.0 months. Sales fall to 50 with inventory unchanged: ratio 2.4, a slowdown warning. Production cuts inventory to 100: ratio 2.0 again.
3. **(Hypothetical) Output gap.** Year 1: potential 100, actual 101, gap +1.0%. Year 2: potential 103, actual 102.5. Output still rose (no classical contraction), but gap = (102.5 − 103)/103 = −0.49%, so the growth cycle is below trend.
