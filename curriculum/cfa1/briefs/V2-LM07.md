## Core principles

- **Quotes.** A/B is units of A (price currency) per one B (base). If it rises, the base strengthens. A direct quote has the domestic currency as price currency; an indirect quote is its reciprocal. Markets use fixed conventions instead because "domestic" depends on viewpoint (pp.216, 227).
- **Asymmetry and spread.** Change = new/old - 1 with the price currency on top; the other currency's change needs the inverted rate, so size and sign differ. The dealer buys the base at the bid and sells at the offer, so the client pays the spread. Inverting a two-sided quote swaps bid and offer (pp.229–230).
- **Nominal vs real.** The nominal rate trades; the real rate is an analyst index adjusting for price levels, showing whether foreign goods got dearer for the domestic buyer. Weaker domestic currency or higher foreign inflation raises it (less purchasing power); higher domestic inflation lowers it. It rests on index choices and forecasts nominal moves poorly because PPP gaps last for years (pp.216–220).
- **Participants.** Dealers (sell side) quote; clients (buy side) include corporates, real money, leveraged funds, retail, governments, central banks and sovereign funds. Most volume is investment and speculation, not trade settlement; swaps are the largest instrument. Central banks intervene against too-weak, too-strong or disorderly moves (pp.221–226).
- **Regime conflict.** With free capital and a credible peg, a rate cut drives money out; the central bank must sell foreign currency, shrinking reserves and money, and rates are pushed back up, so independence is lost. Under a float, a cut weakens the currency, and the export boost reinforces the stimulus. Controls or a looser peg regain some independence at the cost of distortions (pp.231–232).
- **Regime ladder.** Least to most flexible: dollarization, monetary union, currency board, fixed parity, target zone, crawling peg or band, managed float, independent float. A board is legally committed and fully backed, so imbalances hit interest rates, not the rate; a parity rests on discretionary reserves and can be abandoned or attacked. Dollarization imports credibility, not creditworthiness. Gold standard: surplus brings gold, money and prices rise, exports fall (pp.232–242).
- **Trade mirrors capital.** A trade deficit must be financed by a capital account surplus (borrowing or asset sales to foreigners). Expectations move capital faster than goods, so capital flows dominate short- to intermediate-run rates and trade matters more long run. Under a peg, the central bank absorbs flows and rates adjust; under a float the rate jumps (pp.243–244).
- **Capital restrictions.** Free flows put money to best use and bring skills, but governments limit them for strategic or employment reasons, to stop crisis flight, to support a peg, or to raise revenue and steer credit. Tools: taxes, price rules (zero-interest reserve on inflows), quotas, approvals, bans. Costs: administration, loopholes, delayed adjustment, worse investor perception. Comprehensive inflow controls have better evidence than outflow controls (pp.244–247).

## Vocabulary

- Price / base currency — numerator / denominator of A/B (pp.216, 227).
- Nominal rate — the quoted, traded rate (p.216).
- Real rate — nominal rate scaled by relative price levels; an index, not traded (pp.216–217).
- PPP — idea that a standard basket should cost the same everywhere (p.216).
- Sell side / buy side — dealing banks / their clients (p.221).
- Real money — unleveraged institutions such as pensions and mutual funds (p.221).
- FX swap — spot plus offsetting forward (p.224).
- Direct / indirect quote — domestic as price / as base currency (p.227).
- Bid / offer — dealer's buy / sell price of the base (p.229).
- Convertibility — freedom to exchange currency for any use (p.231).
- Price-specie-flow — gold-standard self-correction (pp.232–233).
- Currency board — legally fixed rate, fully backed base money (p.237).
- Seigniorage — profit from issuing non-interest money against earning assets (p.237).
- Managed (dirty) float — intervention without a stated target (p.238).
- Mandatory reserve requirement — inflow rule: part of foreign deposits sits idle at the central bank (p.245).

## Formulas

1. **Real rate:** R(d/f) = S(d/f) x P_f / P_d. S = domestic per foreign unit; P = price indexes (unitless). Assumes income tracks domestic prices. Higher R, less purchasing power. p.217 (PDF 225).
2. **Real-rate change:** (1 + %ΔS)(1 + %ΔP_f)/(1 + %ΔP_d) - 1 ≈ %ΔS + %ΔP_f - %ΔP_d. Use the exact form for big moves. pp.217–218 (PDF 225–226).
3. **Percent change of A/B:** new/old - 1; positive means B appreciated. Other currency: old/new - 1. Not annualised. p.230 (PDF 238).
4. **Inverse:** B/A = 1/(A/B); bid(B/A) = 1/offer(A/B), offer(B/A) = 1/bid(A/B). pp.229, 231 (text-based).
5. **Starting rate:** initial = new/(1 + appreciation). p.249 (PDF 257).
6. **Trade identity:** X - M = (S - I) + (T - G); exports, imports, private saving, investment, net taxes, government spending, same currency and period (assumed). Silent on which assets finance the gap. p.243 (PDF 251).

## Traps

- Reading a bare code such as EUR as the currency, not USD per EUR (pp.216, 228).
- Saying the price currency appreciates when A/B rises; the base does (p.216).
- Treating percent changes as mirrors; the other quote's move is larger in magnitude for a fall (p.230).
- Treating a higher real rate as good for the domestic buyer, or expecting it to predict nominal moves (pp.217–220).
- Thinking spot is the largest instrument or corporates drive volume (pp.225–227).
- Mixing up bid and offer, or forgetting inversion swaps them (p.229).
- Assuming fixed means constant or as credible as a board, or that dollarization brings creditworthiness (pp.236–237, 241).
- Believing peg, free capital and independent policy can coexist, or that controls are costless (pp.232, 245).

**C. Bid/offer.** JPY/USD 150.20–150.24. Selling USD 2,000,000 yields JPY 300,400,000; buying back costs JPY 300,480,000; round trip JPY 80,000 (about 0.027%).
