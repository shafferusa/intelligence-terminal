## Learning outcomes
1. Describe the principal-agent relationship and conflicts between stakeholder groups. Stakeholder Conflicts and Management, p.70–75 (PDF 78–83).
2. Describe corporate governance and the mechanisms that manage stakeholder relationships and reduce the related risks. Governance Mechanisms, p.76–84 (PDF 84–92).
3. Describe the risks of poor governance and the benefits of effective governance. Risks and Benefits, p.85–91 (PDF 93–99).

## Core principles
- **Agency chain.** Shareholders (principals) elect directors, who hire managers, who hire employees. Agents know more than principals, so interests drift apart. Divergence costs money: direct costs (monitoring, such as audit fees) and indirect costs (opportunities lost). More information asymmetry makes investors demand a higher return (lower valuation multiple, higher bond yield). p.70–71 (PDF 78–79).
- **Manager vs shareholder: five divergences.** Insufficient effort, wrong risk appetite, empire building, entrenchment, self-dealing. Pay is the main alignment tool but cuts both ways: heavy option pay can push excess risk-taking; cash-only pay can make managers decline positive-value risky projects; pay tied to firm size rewards growth for its own sake. p.71–72 (PDF 79–80).
- **Controlling vs minority holders.** A concentrated holder (family, company, state) may prefer stability or diversification, while minorities want value maximisation; a patient controller can also clash with minorities wanting quick payouts. Dual-class shares give insiders extra votes per share, so control is decoupled from economic stake, and new issues do not dilute their vote. Fixing it needs a costly vote. p.72–73 (PDF 80–81).
- **Shareholder vs creditor.** Both draw on the same cash flows but hold different kinds of claim. Creditors hold a fixed claim and prefer more equity and restrained payouts; shareholders gain from leverage and distributions. Debt-funded payouts shift risk to lenders and other stakeholders. The conflict is sharper with long-term debt, so long-term lenders write limits on leverage and distributions. p.73–75 (PDF 81–83).
- **Mechanisms match stakeholders.** Shareholders: AGM/EGM votes, proxy voting, activism, derivative suits, takeover threat. Creditors: indenture terms and creditor committees. Board: independent committees, pay design. Employees: labor law, unions, board seats, ESOPs. Customers/suppliers: contracts. Government: law, listing rules, codes. Reporting and transparency underlies all of them. p.77–84 (PDF 85–92).
- **Takeover threat disciplines managers, but defences blunt it.** Staggered boards delay a bidder's control of the board; a rights plan (poison pill) lets other holders buy discounted shares once a threshold is crossed, raising the bidder's cost. Both can entrench management and weaken governance. p.79 (PDF 87).
- **Governance mitigates, it does not eliminate.** Weak controls let one group gain at others' expense (operational risk), invite investigations and suits (legal/regulatory), and damage reputation. Good governance lowers perceived default risk (cost of debt) and equity risk (cost of equity) and supports valuation. Fails if controls are box-ticking, the board is oversized, or layers slow decisions. p.86–91 (PDF 94–99).

## Vocabulary
- Corporate governance — the system of checks and incentives a firm uses to contain clashes between stakeholder interests (p.67).
- Principal-agent relationship — one party hires another to act for it; may or may not be a formal contract (p.70).
- Agency costs — direct (monitoring) and indirect (lost opportunity) costs of divergent interests (p.70).
- Information asymmetry — the agent knows more than the principal (p.70–71).
- Empire building, entrenchment, self-dealing, insufficient effort — the manager-shareholder divergences (p.71).
- Dual-class structure — share classes with unequal votes per share (p.72).
- Bond indenture — contract setting bond terms, required and prohibited actions, collateral (p.80).
- Trustee — intermediary that monitors bond terms for dispersed bondholders (p.77).
- AGM / EGM — annual routine meeting vs special meeting for extraordinary matters (p.77–78).
- Proxy voting / proxy contest — voting by delegate; a fight for board seats via proxies (p.78–79).
- Say on pay — shareholder vote on compensation, non-binding (p.77, 82).
- Shareholder activism; derivative lawsuit — pushing for change; suing on the company's behalf (p.78–79).
- Tender offer; hostile takeover — bid made directly to shareholders; bid opposed by the board (p.79).
- Staggered board; shareholder rights plan (poison pill) — anti-takeover defences (p.79).
- Creditor committee; ad hoc committee — official bankruptcy body; informal distress group not formally representing all holders (p.80).
- Audit, nominating/governance, compensation and risk committees (p.81–82).
- Long-term incentive plan; ESOP — multi-year performance pay; employee share ownership plan (p.82–83).
- Comply or explain — follow the code or justify not doing so (p.83).
- GRC — governance, risk and compliance functions working together (p.87).

## Traps
- "Governance eliminates fraud": it reduces and surfaces it earlier (p.87, 90).
- Treating all shareholders alike: controlling and minority holders can conflict (p.72).
- Assuming option-heavy pay always aligns interests, or cash-only pay is safe: each distorts risk appetite in opposite directions (p.71–72).
- Ownership percentage equals voting control: dual-class breaks the link (p.72).
- Poison pill as a misconduct safeguard: it only defends against takeovers and can entrench managers (p.79).
- Say on pay is advisory, not binding (p.77, 82).
- Mixing committee duties: governance-code adoption and director nomination sit with nominating/governance, not audit (p.81, 84).
- Believing employee relations matter only with unions, or that all controls slow decisions: only ineffective ones do (p.90–91).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. Dual class: founders hold 2,000,000 Class B shares at 10 votes each; the public holds 38,000,000 Class A at 1 vote. Votes: 20,000,000 of 58,000,000 = 34.5%; economic stake 2/40 = 5.0%. At 25 votes per Class B share: 50,000,000 of 88,000,000 = 56.8%, a majority with the same 5.0% stake.
2. Staggered board: 12 seats, 4 up per year. An activist winning every contested seat holds 4 of 12 after year 1 and 8 of 12 (a majority) after year 2. With 3 seats per year it holds 6 of 12 after year 2, not a majority, and needs year 3 (9 of 12). Without staggering it could win all 12 in one vote.
3. Leverage payout: debt 100, equity 100 (D/E 1.0, assets 200). Borrow 40 and pay it as a dividend: debt 140, equity 200 − 140 = 60, D/E = 140/60 = 2.33. Shareholders received 40 cash; lenders hold a larger claim on the same assets, so expect higher required yield or covenants.
