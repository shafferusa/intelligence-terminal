## Core principles
- **Duration is a tangent line; price-yield is a curve.** Duration describes the slope at today's yield. For an option-free fixed-rate bond the true curve bows above that line, so the line understates the price whichever way yields move. The error is negligible for small moves and grows with large ones. The risk of relying on duration alone is mis-stating large-move outcomes. (p.297, p.301)
- **Convexity is the second-order correction.** The extra term is half of convexity times the squared yield change. Squaring makes it positive for rises and falls alike, so it trims the estimated loss and enlarges the estimated gain. It is still an approximation, and it fails for bonds whose cash flows change with yield (embedded options), which later modules treat. (p.302, p.308)
- **What drives convexity.** Longer maturity, lower coupon and lower yield all raise it. Wider spread of cash flows in time raises it at a given duration. When comparing very different bonds, maturity tends to outweigh the coupon and yield effects. (p.304–305)
- **Convexity is valuable, but not automatically free.** Two bonds with the same price, yield and duration differ only in curvature. The more convex one gains more when yields fall and loses less when they rise, so it is preferred. If the market prices that in, it costs more (higher price, lower yield), so the investor pays for the benefit. (p.304)
- **Money measures restate everything in currency.** Money convexity is annual convexity times full price; the currency price change has the same structure as the percentage one. The position holder bears the currency loss; only the scale changes. (p.308)
- **Portfolio figures are market-value-weighted averages.** Bond durations and convexities are averaged using each bond's share of portfolio value. This implicitly assumes every yield moves by the same amount (a parallel shift). The theoretically correct method uses the portfolio's aggregate cash flows but is impractical. Accuracy is better when bond yields are similar and the curve is flat; real curves steepen, flatten and twist, which is when the average fails. (p.298, p.312)
- **Decisions use both measures against a view.** If you expect yields to fall, you want more duration and more convexity, so adding a bond that lowers both works against the view. If you expect rates to rise, floating-rate paper beats longer or lower-coupon fixed bonds because its coupons reset upward. (p.313–315)
- **Compare to a benchmark in order.** Compare duration first, then convexity. At matched duration, the difference in curvature is what separates outcomes in a large move, other things equal. (p.307, solutions)

## Vocabulary
- Convexity (annual convexity) — second-order sensitivity of price to yield; positive for option-free bonds (p.301–302).
- Convexity adjustment — the term half times convexity times squared yield change, added to the duration estimate (p.302).
- Approximate convexity — convexity estimated from prices after a yield rise and fall (p.303).
- Money convexity — annual convexity times full price, in currency (p.308).
- Dispersion of cash flows — how spread out in time the payments are; more dispersion means more convexity at equal duration (p.304).
- Parallel shift — all yields move by the same amount in the same direction (p.312).
- Weighted-average portfolio duration/convexity — market-value-weighted average of the bonds' figures (p.312).
- Aggregate cash-flow method — computing portfolio measures from the combined cash flows; theoretically correct, impractical (p.298, p.312).

## Formulas
1. **Percentage price change (Eq. 1):** %ΔPV_Full ≈ (−AnnModDur × ΔYield) + [½ × AnnConvexity × (ΔYield)²]. ΔYield is a decimal (100 bp = 0.01); duration and convexity annualized; result is a decimal fraction of full price. Option-free fixed-rate bonds; approximation. Intuition: slope term plus curvature term. (p.302)
2. **Convexity table column:** CFconvexity = t × (t+1) × weight × (1 + periodic YTM)^(−periods per year), t counted in periods. Annual convexity = column total ÷ m² (m = periods per year). The exponent equals the usual −2 only for semiannual bonds; use −2 when building your own table. Intuition: later cash flows contribute more. (p.302)
3. **Approximate convexity (Eq. 2):** ApproxCon = (PV₋ + PV₊ − 2×PV₀) / [(ΔYield)² × PV₀]. PV₊, PV₋ are prices after raising and lowering yield by ΔYield (decimal). Useful when cash flows are uncertain. Intuition: the second difference of price measures curvature. (p.303)
4. **Approximate modified duration:** ApproxModDur = (PV₋ − PV₊) / (2 × ΔYield × PV₀). Reused from the previous module. (p.305)
5. **Money convexity (Eq. 3):** MoneyCon = AnnConvexity × PV_Full. Currency units. (p.308)
6. **Money estimate (Eq. 4):** ΔPV_Full ≈ −(MoneyDur × ΔYield) + [½ × MoneyCon × (ΔYield)²]. Currency units. (p.308)
7. **Portfolio measures:** Duration_p = Σ wᵢ × Durᵢ; Convexity_p = Σ wᵢ × Conᵢ, wᵢ = bond market value ÷ portfolio market value. Assumes a parallel shift. The book gives this in words, not as a numbered equation. (p.312)
8. **Zero-coupon full price with accrued fraction:** PV = 100/(1+r)^N × (1+r)^(days/365). Works with negative r, where the spreadsheet PRICE function does not. (p.309)

## Traps
- Forgetting that the convexity adjustment is positive for both yield rises and falls (squared term); only the duration term flips sign. (p.302)
- Plugging basis points or percent into ΔYield instead of decimals. (p.302)
- Forgetting to divide the table total by m² to annualize. (p.302)
- Treating equal duration as equal risk; convexity decides large-move outcomes. (p.307)
- Assuming more convexity is a free lunch; if priced in, it costs yield. (p.304)
- Treating weighted-average portfolio measures as exact; they assume a parallel shift. (p.312)
- Getting the determinants backwards: higher coupon and higher yield lower convexity, longer maturity raises it. (p.305)

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. A bond has annual modified duration 7.0 and annual convexity 60. Yield rises by 2.00%: −7.0×0.02 = −0.14; ½×60×0.0004 = +0.012; estimate −12.8%. Yield falls 2.00%: +0.14 + 0.012 = +15.2%. Duration alone gives ±14%.
2. A portfolio holds 70% in a bond (duration 4, convexity 20) and 30% in a bond (duration 12, convexity 200). Duration = 6.4; convexity = 74. For +100 bp: −0.064 + ½×74×0.0001 = −6.03%. At 50/50 weights: duration 8, convexity 110, estimate −7.45%.
3. A USD20m position has duration 5, convexity 40. MoneyDur = 100m; MoneyCon = 800m. Yield falls 50 bp: +100m×0.005 = +500,000; ½×800m×0.000025 = +10,000; total about +USD510,000.

## Book slips and uncertainties
- The book's worked numbers contain small internal inconsistencies (a rounding difference in the convexity of the long-bond example between text and table, rounding differences in two money-measure figures, and stray duplicated digits in the extracted text). None changes a conclusion; use the book's printed answer for book questions.
- Practice Q1: the stated yield bump and the bump used in the solution's arithmetic differ by a factor of ten. The printed answer stands (p.316, p.318).
- The annual-pay bond's convexity table in the question set uses a discount exponent of −1 where the usual per-period exponent would be −2, giving a somewhat different figure; the comparison conclusion is unaffected (p.306). Trust the −2 form for your own tables.
