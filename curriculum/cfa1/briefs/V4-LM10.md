## Learning outcomes
1. Contrast the quality of the reporting with the quality of the results (pp.313–314).
2. Describe the spectrum of reporting quality, best to worst (pp.315–326).
3. Explain conservative vs aggressive accounting (pp.327–330).
4. Describe motives and enabling conditions for low-quality reports, including fraud (pp.331–332).
5. Describe disciplining mechanisms (markets, regulators, auditors, contracts) and their limits (pp.333–339).
6. Explain how presentation choices, notably non-GAAP measures, steer analysts (pp.340–346; introduced pp.319–324).
7. Identify policies and estimates used to shape earnings, operating cash flow and the balance sheet (pp.347–365).
8. Recognize warning signs and analytical checks for manipulated reports (pp.366–371).

## Core principles
- **Two questions.** Reporting quality: is the disclosure relevant, complete, neutral, error-free? Earnings quality: are results sustainable and earning an adequate return? Earnings quality can be judged only if reporting quality is adequate. Honest reports can show poor earnings (pp.313–316).
- **Spectrum, best to worst:** compliant and sustainable; compliant but unsustainable; compliant but biased; earnings management (real operating actions, or accounting estimate changes); non-compliant; fictitious. (pp.315, 325–326).
- **Timing, not total profit.** Aggressive choices lift current earnings, assets or operating cash flow and push cost into later periods; conservative choices do the reverse, and both can smooth earnings through hidden reserves. (pp.317, 327–330).
- **Conservatism is biased too.** Gains need stronger verification than losses. Impairment: IFRS writes down to recoverable amount; US GAAP tests undiscounted cash flows first, then writes down to fair value; IFRS usually impairs earlier and allows reversals. Big bath and cookie jar reserves overstate charges now to flatter later profit (pp.328–330).
- **Incentives and discipline.** Motives: hide weakness, beat forecasts, careers, covenant avoidance. Markets charge more for doubtful reporting; regulators enforce but standard setters set rules; auditors assure fair presentation, not fraud detection, and are paid by the auditee; covenants add a motive to manipulate (pp.331–339).
- **Non-GAAP** needs reconciliation to the closest GAAP measure; SEC adds equal prominence and bars calling likely-to-recur items non-recurring; IFRS needs definition, relevance, reconciliation. (pp.319–324, 341–343).
- **Cash flow is manageable.** Stretching payables lifts operating cash flow until paid. Classification (interest, dividends under IAS 7, capitalized interest) moves cash among sections, total unchanged (pp.359–361).
- **Warning signs** are clues read together: rising DSO, falling turnover, outlier capitalization, CFO/NI below 1 or falling, fourth-quarter surprises, serial "one-off" charges (pp.366–371).

## Vocabulary
- Reporting quality — relevant, complete, neutral, error-free disclosure (p.312); earnings quality — sustainable, adequate results (p.313).
- Earnings management — intentional biasing, real or accounting (p.325).
- Non-GAAP / APM — performance measure outside the standards (pp.319–320).
- Big bath / cookie jar — oversized charge or reserve shifting profit later (p.330).
- Adverse vs unqualified opinion; expectations gap (pp.335–338).
- Fraud triangle (p.332).
- FOB shipping point / destination — where title passes, so which period books the sale (p.347).
- Valuation allowance — write-down of the unusable part of a deferred tax asset (p.351).
- LIFO liquidation — selling old low-cost layers, lifting profit without matching cash (p.364).
- DSO / receivables turnover — collection-speed measures (p.367).

## Formulas
No typeset formulas in the source; relationships used:
- Interest coverage = EBIT / interest expense. (p.339).
- EPS = net income / weighted average shares (p.340).
- Impairment = carrying amount − recoverable amount (IFRS; recoverable = higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use); carrying − fair value (US GAAP, only after undiscounted cash flows fall below carrying) (pp.328–329).
- Receivables turnover = revenue / average receivables; DSO = 365 / receivables turnover (standard; p.367 gives words only).
- Inventory turnover = cost of sales / average inventory; asset turnover = revenue / total assets (p.367).
- CFO/NI = operating cash flow / net income; below 1.0 persistently, or falling, is a flag (p.368).
- Straight-line = (cost − salvage) / life; double-declining = (2 / life) × opening book value, salvage not deducted, capped at salvage; units-of-production = (cost − salvage) / total units × units used. Timing differs, total does not (pp.354–357).
- Deferred tax asset = tax rate × loss carry-forward; net = DTA − valuation allowance (p.352).

## Traps
- Reporting quality is not earnings quality; aggressive is not illegal.
- Bias is not only upward; conservatism distorts too.
- IFRS is not more conservative on every impairment: earlier timing, but reversals allowed; the US GAAP loss can be larger.
- A clean audit opinion is no fraud guarantee; clean statements can coexist with an adverse internal-control opinion.
- A big-bath charge means earlier expenses were probably understated; adjust prior years. A small valuation allowance raises assets and income.

## Original practice seeds
1. (Hypothetical) Covenant: EBIT/interest at least 2.0; interest 250; reported EBIT 500 includes 40 saved by lengthening asset lives. 500/250 = 2.00 passes; 460/250 = 1.84 breaches.
2. (Hypothetical) Cost 90,000, salvage 10,000, straight-line. Five-year life: 80,000/5 = 16,000 a year; eight-year: 10,000. Income rises 6,000 a year; total stays 80,000.
3. (Hypothetical) Net income 700, CFO 500: CFO/NI 0.71. Deferring 120 of supplier bills past year-end gives CFO 620 (0.89), reversing next year. Revenue 1,000 and receivables 100 give DSO 36.5; revenue 1,200 and receivables 240 give 73 days, a warning sign.
