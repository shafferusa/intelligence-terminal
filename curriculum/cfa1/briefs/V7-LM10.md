## Learning outcomes
1. Explain how to value a derivative with a one-period binomial model: build a hedged option-plus-underlying portfolio, find the hedge ratio, discount its riskless payoff, back out the option price. Taught in Binomial Valuation, The Binomial Model and Pricing a European Call Option (printed 225–233; PDF 233–241).
2. Describe risk neutrality in derivatives pricing: the price equals the discounted expected payoff under risk-neutral probabilities, so real-world probabilities and risk aversion do not matter. Taught in Risk Neutrality (printed 234–238; PDF 242–246).

## Core principles
- **Why options need a model.** A forward's payoff is symmetric, so it can be replicated and priced without any view of the underlying's future price. An option's payoff is lopsided (a right, not an obligation), so we must specify how the underlying might move. The one-period binomial model allows only two end prices over the option's whole life (printed 223, 225–226; PDF 231, 233–234).
- **Law of one price.** Two positions that pay the same amount in every possible state must cost the same today, otherwise someone locks in a riskless profit. This is the logic behind everything else (printed 226; PDF 234).
- **The hedge removes direction.** A dealer who sells a call and buys h units of stock pays the option's payoff to the buyer in the up state and nothing in the down state. Choose h so the combined portfolio is worth the same in both states; it is then riskless (printed 227–229; PDF 235–237).
- **Riskless means earning the risk-free rate.** The hedged portfolio's end value is discounted at r to get today's value; the option price is the stock cost minus that value. If the market option price differs, borrow at r and trade the hedge for a riskless gain above r (printed 229–230, 236–237; PDF 237–238, 244–245).
- **q and risk attitude drop out.** Because the hedge is riskless in both states, an optimist and a pessimist agree on the price. The real-world up-probability q is never used (printed 224–226, 232). What matters is r, Ru and Rd. A wider gap between the up and down prices (more volatility) raises both call and put values, since the payoff upside grows while the downside is floored at zero (printed 233).
- **Risk-neutral probability is a pricing device.** The same price can be written as a weighted average of the two payoffs, discounted at r, using weights chosen so the underlying itself earns exactly r on average. These weights are not forecasts and not q. They depend only on r, Ru, Rd, so one set prices calls, puts and any other payoff on that tree (printed 234–236; PDF 242–244).
- **Rate effects.** A higher r lowers a put's value (the book states this, printed 240–241). A higher r raises a call's value; the book does not state this, but it follows from the formulas (own derivation, Seed 3).
- **Hedge-ratio sign.** Positive h for a sold call means long stock. A negative h (puts) means the option and the stock are on the same side, both bought or both sold (printed 231, 241).

## Vocabulary
- Contingent claim — a derivative whose payoff depends on the underlying's outcome (p.223, 226).
- Law of one price — identical payoffs in all states imply identical cost today (p.226).
- Binomial model — the underlying moves to one of two prices over each period (p.226).
- q — real-world probability of the up move; irrelevant for pricing (p.226).
- Ru, Rd — gross returns (end price over start price) in the up and down states (p.226).
- Hedge ratio h* — units of underlying per option that make the portfolio riskless (p.229).
- Hedged portfolio — option plus h units of underlying with equal value in both states (p.228).
- Risk-neutral probability π — pseudo-probability that makes the underlying earn r in expectation (p.234).
- Risk-neutral pricing — no-arbitrage price independent of investors' risk views (p.235).
- Synthetic risk-free asset — option and underlying combined to earn r with certainty (p.237).
- European option — exercisable only at expiry; the only type valued here.

## Formulas
1. **Ru = S1u/S0 > 1** and **Rd = S1d/S0 < 1** (Eqs 1–2, p.226). Gross returns, so +20% is 1.20. No units.
2. **V0 = hS0 − c0; V1u = hS1u − c1u; V1d = hS1d − c1d** (Eqs 3–5, p.228). Sold call plus h long shares. For a put the book's solution uses the magnitude |h*| of the shares (p.241–242).
3. **h\* = (c1u − c1d)/(S1u − S1d)** (Eq 6, p.228–229). Shares per option; negative for puts. Intuition: option payoff swing over stock swing.
4. **c0 = hS0 − V1(1+r)^-1** (Eq 7, p.229–230). r is the riskless rate per period. Put analog: p0 = V0 − |h*|S0.
5. **V1 = V0(1+r)** (Eq 8, p.230). A riskless portfolio grows at r.
6. **c0 = [π c1u + (1−π) c1d]/(1+r)^T**, same form for puts (Eq 9, p.234). T in units matching r (T=1 for one period).
7. **π = (1 + r − Rd)/(Ru − Rd)** (Eq 10, p.234). Own check: π lies between 0 and 1 only if Rd < 1+r < Ru; outside that there is an arbitrage in the underlying itself.
8. **Payoffs**: call Max(0, S−X), put Max(0, X−S). **Put-call parity**: c0 − p0 = S0 − X(1+r)^-T, equivalently S0 + p0 = c0 + X(1+r)^-T (p.232, 236).

Assumptions: option life equals one period, two states, no dividends, known riskless rate, frictionless trading and divisible units, European exercise.

## Traps
- Using q (or an analyst's bullish view) in the price. It never enters.
- Reading π as the real chance of an up move.
- Mixing rates with gross returns: Ru is 1.25, not 0.25, for a 25% rise.
- Sign of h for puts: negative means buy both put and stock. Plugging it into the call formula gives the wrong price.
- Discounting the wrong thing: hedge method discounts V1; π method discounts the risk-neutral expected payoff.
- Period mismatch: with an annual rate and a six-month option, raise 1+r to the power 0.5 (p.233–234).
- Assuming a put gets cheaper with volatility. Both calls and puts rise with it.
- Rounding π early; round late.
- Measuring arbitrage return on the option cost alone; use the whole outlay.

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
- **Seed 1, call.** S0 = $40, up to $50, down to $30, X = $40, r = 2%. Payoffs 10 and 0; h = 10/20 = 0.5; V1 = 0.5×50 − 10 = 15 (and 0.5×30 = 15); V0 = 15/1.02 = 14.706; c0 = 0.5×40 − 14.706 = **$5.29**. Check: π = (1.02−0.75)/0.50 = 0.54; 0.54×10/1.02 = $5.29.
- **Seed 2, more volatility.** Same stock, up to $60, down to $20, same X and r. Payoffs 20 and 0; h = 0.5; V1 = 30 − 20 = 10; V0 = 9.804; c0 = 20 − 9.804 = **$10.20**. Check: π = 0.52, 0.52×20/1.02 = $10.20.
- **Seed 3, put and a rate change.** Seed 1 tree, put X = $40, payoffs 0 and 10. At r = 2%: p0 = 0.46×10/1.02 = **$4.51**; parity c0 − p0 = 0.78 = 40 − 40/1.02. At r = 5%: π = 0.60, call 0.6×10/1.05 = **$5.71** (up), put 0.4×10/1.05 = **$3.81** (down); parity 1.90 = 40 − 38.10.

- Solution 1 prints Max(1, X−S1d); it should read Max(0, X−S1d) (PDF 249).
