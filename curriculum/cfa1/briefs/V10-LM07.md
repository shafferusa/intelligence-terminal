2. Recommend practices and procedures that prevent violations: the three Compliance Practices sections (V(A) p.186/PDF 200; V(B) pp.195–196/PDF 209–210; V(C) p.202/PDF 216).
3. Tell conforming conduct from violating conduct: the 28 application examples (pp.186–203; PDF 200–217) and practice problems (p.204/PDF 218).

## Core principles
- Chain of the standard: do the work (V(A)), tell the client what matters (V(B)), keep proof (V(C)). Self-check: could you demonstrate adequate effort, explain the essentials to the client, and evidence both afterwards? (p.181/PDF 195)
- Diligence is proportional. Effort scales with the firm's philosophy, your role, the resources provided, and product complexity. Risk angle: diligence lowers but never removes the chance of loss, and a decision is judged on what was knowable then, so a losing but well-researched call is not a violation (pp.182–183; PDF 196–197).
- Reliance on others is allowed, not blind. Colleagues' (secondary) and outside (third-party) research can be used if you make reasonable efforts to confirm assumptions, rigor, timeliness and independence; drop it on any sign of bias. Incentive angle: brokers and vendors may have their own interests; blogs and aggregators warrant more checking than established houses (p.184/PDF 198).
- Models: users need not be experts but must grasp parameters, assumptions and limits, and test output before adopting; builders owe more, including negative and out-of-sample scenarios because the data history may miss full cycles. Failure mode: a model whose parts were tested separately but never as a whole (pp.184–185; PDF 198–199).
- External advisers are reviewed like securities: consistent, objective criteria (controls, ethics, data quality, process and adherence to strategy), never fees alone. Group research is a consensus; you need not dissociate if it is reasonable and objective, even when you disagree (p.185/PDF 199).
- Clients choose and keep advisers based on what they are told, so hidden costs, processes or risks mean uninformed choices. Disclose services and costs (reasonable detail, not exact dollars; costs borne via affiliates and third parties count; sophistication is no excuse), process and material changes (to existing clients, not only prospects), significant known risks and limits, factors behind the analysis, and separate fact from opinion (pp.192–195; PDF 206–209).
- Risk disclosure covers only risks known at the time; not knowing a risk is more a V(A) problem. Liquidity and capacity limits must be disclosed before they bind (p.194/PDF 208).
- Records back actions and decisions not to act, in any medium, and belong to the firm. Follow rules and policy; with none, keep at least 7 years (pp.201–202; PDF 215–216).

## Vocabulary
- Diligence — conscientious, steady, complete work effort (p.182/PDF 196).
- Reasonable basis — a logical process plus good judgment behind a view (p.182/PDF 196).
- Secondary research — a colleague's work from within your own firm (p.184/PDF 198).
- Third-party research — work from an outside organization (p.184/PDF 198).
- Quantitatively oriented models — backtests, screens, derivative valuation and portfolio-construction tools (p.184/PDF 198).
- Subadviser / external adviser — an outside specialist managing part of a mandate (p.185/PDF 199).
- Group research — consensus work product of a team (p.185/PDF 199).
- Liquidity — ability to sell in a timely way at reasonable cost (p.194/PDF 208).
- Capacity — asset level beyond which added money hurts returns (p.194/PDF 208).
- Fact vs opinion — forecasts and estimates are opinions (p.195/PDF 209).
- Record retention — keeping support for analyses and communications; 7 years if no rule applies (p.202/PDF 216).

## Formulas
None in this module. Only number to memorize: 7-year default retention (p.202/PDF 216).

## Traps
- A recommendation that lost money is not automatically a V(A) breach; the process is judged.
- A cheapest-fee or top-return pick for an adviser, or a single-factor method, is not a full review.
- Untested blog ideas or code patches added to a model: inspiration is fine, validation is required.
- Model integration: parts tested separately do not prove the whole works.
- A methodology or process change needs disclosure even if it lowers fees or helps clients; and existing clients must be told, not just prospects.
- Presenting a forecast as certain, or a model as flawless without its limits, breaches V(B); the fix is labeling opinion and stating inputs and limits.
- Exact dollar costs and every conceivable risk are not required: reasonable detail and significant known risks are.
- Records are firm property whoever made them or where; recreate support at a new firm instead of taking files.
- Disagreeing with group research does not by itself require dissociation.

## Original practice seeds
1. Hypothetical fee-base change. A firm charges 0.80% a year, billed quarterly. On a start-of-quarter base of 500,000: 500,000 x 0.0080 / 4 = 1,000. Switching to an end-of-quarter value of 520,000: 520,000 x 0.0080 / 4 = 1,040, so 40 more. Method changes need disclosure regardless of direction.
2. Hypothetical concentration. A 20% fall in one stock costs 0.60 x 0.20 = 12% of the portfolio if it is 60% of assets, and 0.03 x 0.20 = 0.6% if it is 3%. This is why process and risk disclosure matter differently by client.
3. Hypothetical capacity. A strategy has capacity of 5 billion. At 0.25 billion it uses 0.25/5 = 5%; at 4 billion it uses 4/5 = 80%. The limit is disclosed from the start, not when it binds.
