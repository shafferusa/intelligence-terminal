## Learning outcomes
1. Assess a company's historical performance and show how its strategy appears in the record. Intro and Evaluating Past Performance, pp.268–272 (PDF 276–280).
2. Show how to forecast future net income and cash flow. Projecting sections, pp.272–280 (PDF 280–288).
3. Describe what financial statement analysis contributes to judging the credit quality of a debt investment. Assessing Credit Risk, pp.281–283 (PDF 289–291).
4. Describe how financial statement analysis is used to screen for equity investments. Screening, pp.283–286 (PDF 291–294).
5. Explain which analyst adjustments to statements are appropriate for comparing companies. Framework, Investments, Inventory pp.287–292 (PDF 295–300); PPE pp.292–293 (PDF 300–301); Goodwill pp.294–296 (PDF 302–304).

## Core principles
- **Purpose first, then benchmark.** Purpose and context set the questions, data and report form. Past performance is judged to compare firms for relative valuation, to base forecasts, and to assess management. Say what happened and why, against a benchmark; test management's explanation against industry data. pp.268–271 (PDF 276–279).
- **Forecast sales top-down, then apply a margin.** Industry sales link to a macro driver (often a regression on real GDP); times share gives company sales; profit = sales x margin. Fixed costs make margins swing. History suits stable, mature firms only. pp.272–275 (PDF 280–283).
- **Margins higher up the income statement are often more useful.** Leverage and tax rates change by choice or regulation. Strip one-off items, but keep "special" charges that recur nearly every year. p.273 (PDF 281); p.277 (PDF 285).
- **Profit is not cash.** Working capital follows sales, so growth absorbs cash while earnings rise. Use scenario analysis or Monte Carlo simulation for risk, and keep forecasts consistent (DSO, sales, receivables). pp.277–280 (PDF 285–288).
- **Credit analysis looks at the downside and at cash.** A lender's upside is capped, and debt is paid in cash. In the rating framework shown, leverage and coverage carries the largest weight. Adjust statements before computing ratios. pp.281–283 (PDF 289–291).
- **Screens are blunt tools.** Criteria are correlated, one ratio can pass bad firms (negative values can look attractive), and a leverage cap can remove whole sectors. Add companion criteria. pp.283–286 (PDF 291–294).
- **Adjust before comparing.** Four questions: material? standards differ? methods differ? estimates differ? Statements are linked, so a balance sheet change flows to COGS, income and retained earnings. pp.287–293 (PDF 295–301).
- **Acquired versus homegrown intangibles.** An acquirer capitalizes goodwill; an organic grower expenses advertising, training and R&D. Compare using price to book excluding goodwill and price to tangible book; this is not proof of overpayment. pp.294–295 (PDF 302–303).

## Formulas
No boxed formulas in the book; these are the calculations used.
1. Sales forecast = market share x industry sales. p.273 (PDF 281).
2. Profit forecast = forecast sales x forecast margin. p.273 (PDF 281).
3. Operating margin = operating profit / sales; the book's own case actually used pre-tax income after interest. p.276 (PDF 284).
4. Multi-period model: interest income = rate x opening cash; tax = tax rate x pretax income; working capital = fixed % of sales. pp.278–279 (PDF 286–287).
5. Change in cash = net income + non-cash items − increase in working capital − capex (self-funded, no dividends). p.279 (PDF 287).
6. FCFE = net income + non-cash items − investment in net working capital − investment in net fixed assets + net borrowing. p.278 (PDF 286).
7. Receivable turnover = 365 / DSO; average receivables = sales / turnover. p.281 (PDF 289).
8. FIFO inventory = LIFO inventory + ending LIFO reserve; FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − change in LIFO reserve (rising prices). p.288 (PDF 296); p.292 (PDF 300).
9. Adjusted current ratio = (current assets + LIFO reserve) / current liabilities. pp.289–291 (PDF 297–299).
10. Approximate tax cost if LIFO ended = tax rate x LIFO reserve, spread over several years. p.290 (PDF 298).
11. PPE (pooled approximations): accumulated depreciation / gross PPE = share of life used; accumulated depreciation / depreciation expense = average age; net PPE / depreciation expense = remaining life; gross PPE / depreciation expense = total life; capex / (gross PPE + capex) = renewal rate. pp.292–293 (PDF 300–301).
12. MV/BV = market cap / equity; adjusted = market cap / (equity − goodwill); price to tangible book = market cap / (equity − goodwill − other intangibles). p.295 (PDF 303).
13. Dividend yield = dividends per share / price; leverage screen = total liabilities / total assets. p.284 (PDF 292).
14. Expected number passing all screens if independent = universe size x product of pass fractions; real counts are higher. p.285 (PDF 293).

## Traps
- Positive net income can coexist with falling cash.
- The whole LIFO reserve goes onto inventory; COGS changes only by the year's change in the reserve.
- Conversion to FIFO raises current assets and can flip a liquidity comparison; LIFO is not allowed under IFRS.
- "Special" charges are not automatically transitory.
- Lenders gain nothing from upside.
- Know the three screening biases by name, separate from the back-testing method itself.
- Average age and life ratios are pooled approximations.

## Original practice seeds
(All hypothetical.)
A. HypoCo has a 5-year average operating margin of 12% and a next-year sales forecast of $500M. Forecast operating profit = 0.12 x 500 = $60M. If the true underlying margin is 10%, profit = $50M, which is $10M (about 17%) lower. A one-off $15M charge on $450M sales understated that year's margin by 15/450 = 3.3 points, so remove it before averaging.
B. Firm X (LIFO, $M): current assets 400 including inventory 150; LIFO reserve 60; current liabilities 200. Unadjusted current ratio = 400/200 = 2.00. FIFO inventory = 150 + 60 = 210; adjusted current assets 460; ratio 460/200 = 2.30. If the reserve rose from 50 to 60 and LIFO COGS is 700, FIFO COGS = 700 − 10 = 690.
C. New firm: sales $200M in year 1 and $240M in year 2; net income 5% of sales; working capital 25% of sales; no fixed capital or non-cash items. Year 2 net income = 12; working capital increase = 0.25 x 40 = 10; change in cash = +2 despite 12 of profit. At 40% working capital, increase = 16 and cash change = −4.
