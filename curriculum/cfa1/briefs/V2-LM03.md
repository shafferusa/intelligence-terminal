## Learning outcomes
1. Contrast monetary policy with fiscal policy: who acts and with which lever (Section 2, pp.79–82).
2. Describe what fiscal policy is for (demand, income distribution, resource allocation) and weigh the arguments on whether debt relative to GDP matters (3.1, 3.2, pp.83–91; Ricardian equivalence again in the example after 4.3, PDF 105–106).
3. Describe the spending and tax tools with their advantages and disadvantages (Section 4, pp.92–97).
4. Explain how fiscal policy is implemented, why it is hard, and how to judge whether a stance is expansionary or contractionary (Section 5, pp.98–102; stance by deficit direction first appears p.85).

## Core principles
- **Two actors, two levers.** The central bank works on money and credit; government works on taxes and spending (pp.79–82). Both are used countercyclically.
- **Demand chain.** A tax cut or extra spending raises disposable income or profit, then private spending, then aggregate demand, then output (p.83). Government spending is someone's income directly. The chain fails if households save the cut (precautionary saving) or if there is no slack, in which case prices rise instead of output. Keynesians: powerful with spare capacity; monetarists: temporary effects.
- **Stance is read from the budget gap, but the gap is polluted by the cycle.** Bigger deficit means looser, bigger surplus means tighter (p.85). Automatic stabilizers move the deficit with no new decision; discretionary changes are deliberate (p.85). Use the structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit to judge stance (p.99). Judge the net effect on the deficit, not the spending side alone (p.85, p.99).
- **Deficit is a flow, debt a stock** (p.88). If real growth is below the real interest rate the debt ratio tends to worsen; a primary deficit makes it worse still (p.89). Inflation erodes real debt, so cash interest overstates the real cost (p.99).
- **Worry about debt vs relax.** Relax: owed to own citizens, funds investment that raises future income, may force tax reform, Ricardian offset, idle resources exist. Worry: higher future taxes dampen work and investment, the central bank may monetize debt and cause inflation, crowding out of private investment via higher rates (pp.89–91).
- **Tool trade-off: speed vs potency.** Indirect taxes act fastest and are cheap to collect; direct taxes and transfers need lead time; capital spending is slowest but adds to productive capacity (p.95). Direct spending moves output more than tax cuts, except transfers to the poorest, who spend nearly everything.
- **Multiplier.** Each round of spending becomes income, part leaks to saving and net taxes, and the rounds form a shrinking geometric series (pp.96–97). It needs idle capacity and constant prices.
- **Balanced budget still expands.** Spending adds the full amount while tax removes only the consumed share, so output rises (p.97). The fully balanced version has multiplier 1.
- **Fiscal policy cannot fully stabilize demand**: three lags, poor forecasts, hard-to-measure full employment, supply-side causes of idle resources, possible market refusal to finance, crowding out (pp.100–102).

## Vocabulary
- Fiscal policy — government decisions on taxing and spending (p.79, p.82).
- Monetary policy — central bank influence on money and credit (p.79, p.81).
- Aggregate demand — planned total spending; the target of stimulus (p.83).
- Expansionary / contractionary — policy that raises / lowers demand, usually a larger deficit / larger surplus (p.85).
- Budget deficit / surplus — spending minus revenue over a period; a flow (pp.80, 85).
- National debt — the accumulated stock of past deficits (p.88).
- Automatic stabilizer — tax or benefit rule that moves with the cycle without a new decision (p.85).
- Discretionary policy — deliberate change in spending or tax rates (p.85).
- Transfer payments — welfare-type payments with no output in exchange; not government spending on goods and services (p.92).
- Capital vs current spending — infrastructure vs recurring running costs (p.92).
- Direct vs indirect taxes — on income, wealth, profit vs on spending (p.93).
- Progressive tax — share paid rises with income (p.93).
- Horizontal / vertical equity — equals pay equally / richer pay more (p.93).
- Supply-side economics — tax cuts raise incentives to work, save, invest  (p.94).
- Crowding out — government borrowing reduces private investment access to funds or raises its cost (pp.90, 101).
- Ricardian equivalence — households foresee future taxes and save to meet them, offsetting deficit stimulus (p.90; example PDF 105–106).
- MPC / MPS — share of extra disposable income spent / saved; they sum to 1 (p.96).
- Net tax rate — net taxes as a share of income (p.96).
- Fiscal multiplier — output change per unit of spending change after all rounds (pp.96–97).
- Structural (cyclically adjusted) deficit — deficit that would exist at full employment (p.99).
- Recognition, action, impact lags — delays in noticing, enacting and feeling policy (p.100).

## Formulas
- **Budget balance = G − T + B** (p.96). G spending on goods and services, T taxes, B transfers, currency per period. Positive means deficit; the book does not state the sign, and debt interest is counted within spending elsewhere in the text.
- **YD = Y − NT = (1 − t)Y** (p.96). YD disposable income, NT net taxes (taxes minus transfers), t net tax rate as decimal. Assumes proportional net taxes.
- **c + s = 1** (p.96). MPC plus MPS, out of disposable income, not gross income.
- **Simple multiplier = 1/(1 − c)** (p.96). No taxes, constant prices, spare capacity. Sum of c + c² + c³ + ...
- **Fiscal multiplier = 1/[1 − c(1 − t)]** (p.97). Needs 0 < c(1 − t) < 1, proportional tax, constant prices, unused resources. More leakage means a smaller multiplier.
- **ΔY = multiplier × ΔG** (p.97), a restatement, not a boxed formula.
- **Balanced budget multiplier = 1** (p.97). Only when the spending rise equals the final total tax rise.
- No formula for debt dynamics; the condition is verbal (growth versus real interest rate, p.89).

## Traps
- Deficit is not debt: annual flow vs accumulated stock (p.88).
- A bigger deficit is not automatically expansionary; recessions create deficits via stabilizers. Use the structural deficit (p.99).
- Using 1/(1 − c) when taxes exist; with taxes use c(1 − t) (pp.96–97).
- Multiplier results need slack and constant prices (p.91).
- Balanced budget does not mean zero effect; the multiplier is 1, not 0 (p.97).
- Ricardian equivalence rests on households anticipating future taxes (p.90).
- Transfers are not government spending on goods and services (p.92).
- Quick tools are indirect taxes; capital spending is slow (p.95).

## Original practice seeds
All hypothetical.
1. MPC 0.8, net tax rate 0.25, spare capacity, constant prices. c(1 − t) = 0.60, multiplier = 1/0.40 = 2.5. A one-off spending rise of 40 raises output by 100 (rounds 40 + 24 + 14.4 + 8.64 + ...). If t falls to 0.10: c(1 − t) = 0.72, multiplier = 3.571, output rises about 142.9.
2. Recession: actual deficit 6.0% of GDP, cyclical part 2.5%, so structural 3.5%. Recovery cuts the actual deficit to 4.0% with no policy change; structural stays 3.5%, the cyclical part is now 0.5%. The fall is not tightening.
3. Debt 100, GDP 100 (real), no primary deficit, real interest 3%, one year. Debt becomes 103. Growth 1%: 103/101 = 102.0%, ratio worsens. Growth 4%: 103/104 = 99.0%, ratio improves.
